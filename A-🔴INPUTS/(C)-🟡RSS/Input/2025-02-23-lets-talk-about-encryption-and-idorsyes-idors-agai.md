<h3>Let’s Talk About Encryption And IDORs (Yes, IDORs Again)— @bxmbn</h3><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/305/1*UoYAwZlzKoeO6_Wjl0nNAw.png" /></figure><p>Imagine a system designed to protect sensitive user data — like insurance policies — only to have a single misstep turn it into an open book. That’s exactly what I stumbled upon while poking around a certain event registration protection service. What started as curiosity about an API endpoint spiraled into a discovery that could grant complete access to any user’s policy. Here’s how it unfolded, step by step, and why it matters.</p><h3>The Discovery: An API with a Secret</h3><p>It all began with a website that stores user policies — think of it as a digital filing cabinet for insurance plans. While exploring, I found that there was an API call to: <strong>/api/claims/encrypt?text=EUSP2386411060 </strong>with my Insurance Policy there (<strong>EUSP2386411060</strong>) and the response was the encrypted value of my policy ID, this value gives access to the policy</p><p>So in short, if your policy is <strong>EUSP2386411061 </strong>I can encrypt it and basically access your insurance policy as the encrypted value is used to access a PDF file containing your policy Information..</p><h3><strong>Cracking the Code: A Step-by-Step Breakdown</strong></h3><p>Let’s walk through how this works. say <strong>EUSP2386401065 </strong>is your Policy ID</p><p><strong>Step 1: Encrypting the Policy ID</strong></p><p>Using a simple HTTP GET request, the attacker sent the policy ID to the encryption endpoint. Here’s what it looked like:</p><pre>GET /api/claims/encrypt?text=EUSP2386401065 HTTP/2<br />Host: [redacted-]<br />User-Agent: Mozilla/5.0 (X11; Linux x86_64; rv:122.0) Gecko/20100101 Firefox/122.0<br />Accept: application/json, text/plain, */*</pre><p>The response? A neat little encrypted string: <strong><em>BnmnNGD6EFL2Eeo85nTHA</em></strong>.</p><p><strong>Step 2: Unlocking the Policy</strong></p><p>Next, the attacker takes that encrypted string and plugged it into: /<strong>api/certificates/policies/<em>{e</em>ncrypted_string_from_step_1} </strong>Like so:</p><p>/api/certificates/policies/<strong><em>BnmnNGD6EFL2Eeo85nTHA</em></strong></p><p>Boom — The PDF popped up, confirming the details of your policy <strong>EUSP2386401065</strong>. Full names, Date of birth, Addresses, but most importantly the Dates and coverage since these will allow the attacker to then authenticate..</p><p><strong>Step 3: Taking Control</strong></p><p>Here’s where it gets downright eerie. Armed with the policy ID and a purchase date from the PDF, I headed to the service’s management portal. I selected “Policy Number” from the login options, entered <strong>EUSP2386401065</strong>, picked “Purchase Date,” and typed in the date from the policy details. Just like that, I was in — full access to manage or even take over the policy.</p><p>The kicker as mentioned, This wasn’t a one-off. The policy IDs follow a numeric pattern, meaning you could feasibly encrypt any ID, get its access key, and repeat the process for every user in the system.</p><p><strong>The Flaw: Encryption as a Weak Link</strong></p><p>So, what’s going on here? The system uses the same encryption algorithm for every policy ID, and the encrypted output doubles as an access key. There’s no additional verification — no secret handshake, no second factor. If you can guess or obtain a policy ID (and they’re sequential), you can encrypt it, grab the key, and waltz into the policy vault. It’s like locking a safe with a key that’s printed on the outside.</p><p><strong>The Stakes: Why This Matters</strong></p><p>The impact is staggering. This isn’t just a peek at someone’s insurance plan — it’s complete access. An attacker could view, modify, or even cancel policies, all without the user’s knowledge. For a service handling potentially thousands of users, this is a skeleton key to the entire database. Personal data, financial details, event plans — all up for grabs.</p><p><strong>The Bigger Picture</strong></p><p>This isn’t about pointing fingers at a specific company (hence the redactions). It’s a wake-up call for how we design and secure systems. Encryption is powerful, but when it’s the only gatekeeper — and a predictable one at that — it’s not enough. Sequential IDs, exposed endpoints, and lax access controls are a recipe for trouble.</p><p>I reported this responsibly, but it’s a reminder: the smallest oversight can turn a fortress into a house of cards. Next time you trust a service with your data, ask yourself — what’s stopping someone from finding the key?</p><p>Thank you for reviewing this Writeup! .
