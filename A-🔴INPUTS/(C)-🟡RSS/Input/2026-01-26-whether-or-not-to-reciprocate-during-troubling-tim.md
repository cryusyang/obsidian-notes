<p>I wrote the post below in the summer of 2025. Given the recent events in Minneapolis, it&#8217;s worth revisiting. Leaders on both sides of the immigration issue need to ratchet down rather ratchet up or more people will be hurt or possibly killed.</p>
<p>The Apostle Peter tells Christians, &#8220;Do not repay evil with evil or insult with insult, but with blessing, because to this you were called so that you may inherit a blessing.&#8221; (1 Peter 3:9)</p>
<p>Both sides have legitimate grievances. Many of the same ICE agents who served under presidents Obama and Biden are now being attacked, doxed, and have to deal with family threats.</p>
<p>People in many communities are afraid, see what they believe is excessive use of force, wonder where constitutional rights come into play, and see U.S. citizens detained in some instances.</p>
<p>Jesus went further than Peter, telling followers, &#8220;love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you.&#8221; (Matthew 8:44) Then He modeled it on the cross.</p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400; text-align: center;"><strong>To reciprocate, or not to reciprocate, that is the question:</strong></p>
<p style="font-weight: 400; text-align: center;"><strong>Whether it’s right to absorb</strong></p>
<p style="font-weight: 400; text-align: center;"><strong>Words or actions that are untrue or unjust,</strong></p>
<p style="font-weight: 400; text-align: center;"><strong>Or to retaliate, returning insult or slight for insult or slight</strong></p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;"><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/robertcialdini/">Dr. Robert Cialdini’s</a> principle of reciprocity is one of the most powerful drivers of human behavior. It’s built into each of us and has helped us survive and thrive as a species.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">When someone gives, we feel compelled to give back. Done well, it creates what I call a <em>virtuous cycle</em>—a positive exchange that builds trust, strengthens relationships, and multiplies goodwill.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">But reciprocity isn’t always virtuous, and sometimes the best choice is not to reciprocate at all.</p>
<h3 style="font-weight: 400;">The Virtuous Cycle of Reciprocity</h3>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">When reciprocity is guided by the principles of unity or liking, supported by the desire to do good, it can transform relationships. Think about when you go out of your way to help a friend or coworker—not because you expect something back, but because you value them and the relationship. More often than not, your kindness comes back to you in ways you didn’t anticipate. That’s reciprocity at its best. It builds bridges, not walls.</p>
<h3 style="font-weight: 400;">The Vicious Cycle of Retaliation</h3>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">But, like most things in life, good can become bad, and so it is with reciprocity. It has a dark side. If someone slights us, we feel the natural urge to retaliate. When we do, they respond in kind. The back and forth continues until both sides are entrenched in hostility.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">This is the vicious cycle. You see it everywhere:</p>
<ul style="font-weight: 400;">
<li>Political parties locked in endless conflict</li>
<li>Families divided by old wounds</li>
<li>Countries, neighbors, or coworkers who can’t let go of a grievance</li>
</ul>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">What began as an offense spirals into bitterness and broken relationships.</p>
<h3 style="font-weight: 400;">When Not to Reciprocate</h3>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Here’s where wisdom comes into play. Not every action deserves a response in kind. When wronged, sometimes choosing <em>not</em> to reciprocate can be the most powerful move you can make.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Imagine yourself carrying two buckets: one full of gasoline, the other full of water. When conflict arises, which will you throw on the fire?</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In my upcoming book, <a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/1733178562/"><em>Influenced from Above</em></a>, I write about how love calls us to something higher than score-keeping. Love and unity invite us to break the chain reaction of hostility. They ask us to absorb the wrong, to forgive, and oftentimes to respond with kindness even though it’s not deserved.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">This approach doesn’t mean becoming a doormat. It means refusing to be controlled by another person or your emotions, refusing to let someone else’s negativity dictate your response.</p>
<h3 style="font-weight: 400;">Breaking the Cycle</h3>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">So how do you do it? Here are three ideas.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Pause before reacting. </strong>Viktor Frankl, a holocaust survivor, reminded readers in <em>Man’s Search for Meaning</em>, there is freedom between stimulus and response—choice.  Ask yourself, “Will my response escalate or de-escalate?”</li>
<li><strong>Choose vulnerability. </strong>Sometimes forgiveness—without defensiveness—softens even the hardest heart. Jesus was reviled but did not revile in return and in so doing, showed us what true love looks like.</li>
<li><strong>Lead with love. </strong>Look for what unites you with another, not what divides. In many instances, people want the same thing, they just have different ideas about how to make that happen.</li>
</ol>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">When you choose to stop the cycle, you give others permission to do the same. And even if they don’t, you protect your own peace and integrity. Proverbs 15:1 encourages this, “A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger.”</p>
<h3 style="font-weight: 400;">Your Impact, Your Choice</h3>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Reciprocity is built into us. But the direction it takes—virtuous or vicious—is a choice that each of us is capable of making. Every interaction is an opportunity to decide whether you’ll escalate conflict or deescalate with kindness.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">When we choose love over retaliation, we reflect something bigger than ourselves. We model the kind of unity our families, communities, and country desperately need.</p>

## 中文译文

此文写于2025年夏季。鉴于明尼阿波利斯近期发生的事件，重读此文尤为必要。移民议题双方的领导者都亟需降温，而非火上浇油；否则，将有更多人受伤，甚至丧命。

使徒彼得劝勉基督徒：“不要以恶报恶，以辱骂还辱骂，倒要祝福，因你们正是为此蒙召，好叫你们承受福气。”（《彼得前书》3:9）

双方皆有其正当的不满与委屈。许多如今正遭受攻击、被“人肉搜索”（doxed）、甚至家人遭死亡威胁的美国移民与海关执法局（ICE）特工，曾在奥巴马与拜登总统任内履职。

许多社区的民众心怀恐惧，目睹他们所认为的过度使用武力，质疑宪法权利究竟在何处得以落实，并发现部分美国公民在某些情况下竟遭拘留。

耶稣的教导比彼得更进一步，祂告诉门徒：“要爱你们的仇敌，为那逼迫你们的祷告。”（《马太福音》5:44）随后，祂在十字架上亲身示范了这一真理。

以牙还牙，抑或不以牙还牙——这的确是个问题：  
是否应当默默承受  
那些失实或不公的言语与行为，  
还是选择反击，以辱还辱、以怨报怨？

罗伯特·西奥迪尼博士（Dr. Robert Cialdini）提出的“互惠原则”，是驱动人类行为最强大的力量之一。它深植于我们每个人天性之中，助我们这个物种得以存续并繁荣。

当他人给予时，我们内心便本能地渴望回馈。若运用得当，这种互惠便能形成我所称的“良性循环”——一种正向的互动，可建立信任、巩固关系、倍增善意。

但互惠并非总是良性的；有时，最明智的选择恰恰是完全不予回应。

互惠的良性循环  
当互惠受“合一”或“好感”等原则引导，并辅以行善的意愿时，它便能彻底转化人际关系。试想：你主动帮助一位朋友或同事，并非期待回报，而是出于珍视对方、珍视这段关系。而往往，你的善意会以意想不到的方式回流到你身上——这便是互惠最美好的模样：它搭建桥梁，而非筑起高墙。

报复的恶性循环  
然而，正如世间多数事物一样，善亦可转为恶，互惠亦不例外。它同样具有阴暗面：当有人冒犯我们，我们自然萌生反击冲动；一旦出手，对方随即以牙还牙。如此往复，直至双方深陷敌意、壁垒森严。

这便是恶性循环。你处处可见其踪影：  
——政党陷入无休止的对抗；  
——家庭因陈年旧伤而四分五裂；  
——国家之间、邻里之间、同事之间，执拗于一桩旧怨，久久无法释怀。  

最初的一次冒犯，终将螺旋式演变为苦毒与关系的彻底破裂。

何时不该以牙还牙？  
此时，智慧便显得尤为关键。并非每一次冒犯都值得同等回应。当你受到伤害时，有时选择沉默不还击，反而是你所能做出的最具力量之举。

试想自己手中提着两只桶：一只盛满汽油，另一只装满清水。当冲突燃起，你打算往火上泼哪一桶？

在我即将出版的新书《自上而来的影响力》（Influenced from Above）中，我写道：爱呼唤我们超越斤斤计较的狭隘格局。爱与合一邀请我们斩断敌意的连锁反应；它要求我们接纳伤害、选择饶恕，且常常——即使对方并不配得——仍以善意回应。

这种态度绝非沦为任人践踏的“软柿子”。它意味着拒绝被他人或自己的情绪所操控，拒绝让别人的消极言行来定义你的回应方式。

如何打破循环？  
那么，我们该如何做到呢？以下提供三点建议：

一、先暂停，再回应。纳粹集中营幸存者维克多·弗兰克尔（Viktor Frankl）在《活出生命的意义》（Man’s Search for Meaning）中提醒读者：在刺激与回应之间，存在着自由——即选择的自由。请扪心自问：“我的回应，会加剧冲突，还是平息纷争？”

二、选择坦诚与脆弱。有时，不带防御姿态的饶恕，足以软化最坚硬的心。耶稣曾遭凌辱，却未以辱还辱；祂以此向我们显明何为真正的爱。

三、以爱为先。努力寻找你与他人之间的共通之处，而非分歧所在。事实上，在许多情形下，人们追求的目标本就一致，只是对实现路径各持己见。

当你主动终止恶性循环，便等于赋予他人同样停手的许可。即便对方并未跟进，你仍保全了自己的内心安宁与人格完整。《箴言》15:1如此劝勉：“回答柔和，使怒消退；言语暴戾，触动怒气。”

你的影响，源于你的选择  
互惠本能早已铭刻于我们基因之中；但这条本能之路最终导向良性循环，抑或恶性循环，则取决于我们每一个人清醒自主的选择。每一次互动，都是一个抉择时刻：你选择激化矛盾，还是以善意化解纷争？

当我们选择以爱代替报复，我们所映照的，便远不止自身；我们所展现的，正是我们的家庭、社区乃至整个国家迫切渴求的那种合一。

让我们一起聊聊吧  
那么，你呢？  
你是否曾选择不以牙还牙，并亲眼见证事态因此向好转变？  
又或者，你是否曾眼睁睁看着报复不断升级，最终摧毁了一段关系？  
我诚挚邀请你分享自己的经历——因为此刻，或许正有人需要听到你的故事。
