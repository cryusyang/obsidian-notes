<h3>&#8220;We humanize what is going on in the world and in ourselves only by speaking of it, and in the course of speaking of it we learn to be human.&#8221;</h3>
<hr />
<p><a href="http://www.amazon.com/Men-Dark-Times-Hannah-Arendt/dp/0156588900/?tag=braipick-20" target="_blank"><img alt="Hannah Arendt on the Power of Being an Outsider" class="cover alignright size-medium" height="482" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/hannaharendt_menindarktimes.jpg?fit=320%2C482&amp;ssl=1" width="320" /></a></p><p><em>&#8220;The sole purpose of human existence is to kindle a light in the darkness of mere being,&#8221;</em> Carl Jung wrote as he <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2012/03/13/memories-dreams-reflections/">contemplated life and death</a>. And the most astonishing part of it all is just how resourceful we can be in kindling that flame even amid the most oxygen-deprived and suffocating of circumstances &#8212; a kind of spiritual survival instinct the vitalizing beauty of which only the oppressed, the marginalized, and the otherwise banished from mainstream society have the painful privilege of knowing.</p>
<p>This painful privilege is what the great German writer and political theorist <strong>Hannah Arendt</strong> (October 14, 1906&ndash;December 4, 1975) explores in <a href="http://www.amazon.com/Men-Dark-Times-Hannah-Arendt/dp/0156588900/?tag=braipick-20" target="_blank"><strong><em>Men in Dark Times</em></strong></a> (<a href="http://www.worldcat.org/title/men-in-dark-times/oclc/391716&#038;referer=brief_results" target="_blank"><em>public library</em></a>) &#8212; a 1968 collection of essays that have grown all the more timely and radiant in the half-century since. (The book&#8217;s title, it bears pointing with a mixture of lamentation and delight, comes from an era when <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2014/10/17/ursula-k-le-guin-gender/">a great woman was a &#8220;he&#8221;</a> &#8212; a paradox of which Arendt herself, one of the greatest minds of the twentieth century and the first woman to <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2015/12/02/hannah-arendt-the-life-of-the-mind-time-thinking/">deliver the prestigious Gifford Lectures</a>, was the epitome.)</p>
<figure class="wp-caption alignnone"><a href="http://www.amazon.com/Men-Dark-Times-Hannah-Arendt/dp/0156588900/?tag=braipick-20" target="_blank"><img src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/09/hannaharendt2.jpg?w=600&#038;ssl=1" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text">Hannah Arendt by Fred Stein, 1944 (Photograph courtesy of the Fred Stein Archive)</figcaption></figure>
<p>A generation after Viktor Frankl penned his timeless treatise on <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/03/26/viktor-frankl-mans-search-for-meaning/">how the darkest of circumstances illuminate the human search for meaning</a>, Arendt writes:</p>
<blockquote><p>Even in the darkest of times we have the right to expect some illumination, and that such illumination may well come less from theories and concepts than from the uncertain, flickering, and often weak light that some men and women, in their lives and their works, will kindle under almost all circumstances and shed over the time span that was given them on earth&#8230; Eyes so used to darkness as ours will hardly be able to tell whether their light was the light of a candle or that of a blazing sun. But such objective evaluation seems to me a matter of secondary importance which can be safely left to posterity.</p></blockquote>
<p>From our vantage point as Arendt&#8217;s posterity, one thing is certain &#8212; her own ideas about freedom and justice and human nature are more blazing than ever. Half a century before Rebecca Solnit&#8217;s <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2016/03/16/rebecca-solnit-hope-in-the-dark-2/">lucid and luminous case for hope in the dark</a>, Arendt writes:</p>
<blockquote><p>The world lies between people, and this in-between &#8230; is today the object of the greatest concern and the most obvious upheaval in almost all the countries of the globe.</p></blockquote>
<p>Arendt argues that in dark times &#8212; times of injustice, when certain groups are oppressed by certain other groups and personal liberty is imperiled &#8212; something magical happens to this in-between space, &#8220;a special kind of humanity develops,&#8221; a fierce fellowship between and among the oppressed. More than a century after Kierkegaard contemplated <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2014/11/26/kierkegaard-individual-crowd-conformity-minority/">the power of the minority</a>, she writes:</p>
<blockquote><p>Humanity manifests itself in such brotherhood most frequently in “dark times.” This kind of humanity actually becomes inevitable when the times become so extremely dark for certain groups of people that it is no longer up to them, their insight or choice, to withdraw from the world. Humanity in the form of fraternity invariably appears historically among persecuted peoples and enslaved groups&#8230; This kind of humanity is the great privilege of pariah peoples; it is the advantage that the pariahs of this world always and in all circumstances can have over others.</p></blockquote>
<figure class="wp-caption alignnone"><a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2014/12/17/morris-micklewhite-and-the-tangerine-dress/"><img src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/morrismicklewhite4.jpg" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text">Art from <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2014/12/17/morris-micklewhite-and-the-tangerine-dress/"><em>Morris Micklewhite and the Tangerine Dress</em></a>, a modern fable of paraihdom and acceptance</figcaption></figure>
<p>Arendt herself, in addition to being a female intellectual in a almost entirely male realm, belongs to one such pariah population &#8212; the group of Jews expelled from Germany by Hitler at an early age &#8212; and it is from this meeting point of the personal and the political that she adds:</p>
<blockquote><p>The privilege is dearly bought; it is often accompanied by so radical a loss of the world, so fearful an atrophy of all the organs with which we respond to it &#8212; starting with the common sense with which we orient ourselves in a world common to ourselves and others and going on to the sense of beauty, or taste, with which we love the world &#8212; that in extreme cases, in which pariahdom has persisted for centuries, we can speak of real worldlessness.</p></blockquote>
<p>But out of this worldless in-betweenness, Arendt observes, something else is born &#8212; a new sort of kindred humanism, which can&#8217;t be replicated or manufactured under any other circumstances:</p>
<blockquote><p>It is as if under the pressure of persecution the persecuted have moved so closely together that the interspace which we have called world (and which of course existed between them before the persecution, keeping them at a distance from one another) has simply disappeared. This produces a warmth of human relationships which may strike those who have had some experience with such groups as an almost physical phenomenon&#8230; In its full development it can breed a kindliness and sheer goodness of which human beings are otherwise scarcely capable. Frequently it is also the source of a vitality, a joy in the simple fact of being alive, rather suggesting that life comes fully into its own only among those who are, in worldly terms, the insulted and injured.</p>
<p>[&#8230;]</p>
<p>But &#8230; this kind of humanitarianism, whose purest form is a privilege of the pariah, is not transmissible and cannot be easily acquired by those who do not belong among the pariahs. Neither compassion nor actual sharing of suffering is enough.</p></blockquote>
<p>There is something larger and more expansive at the heart of the matter, Arendt argues &#8212; &#8220;the question of selflessness, or rather the question of openness to others, which in fact is the precondition for &#8216;humanity&#8217; in every sense of that word&#8221; &#8212; and this openness, in its highest form, is an openness to each other&#8217;s joy rather than only each other&#8217;s suffering:</p>
<blockquote><p>It seems evident that sharing joy is absolutely superior in this respect to sharing suffering. Gladness, not sadness, is talkative, and truly human dialogue differs from mere talk or even discussion in that it is entirely permeated by pleasure in the other person and what he says. It is tuned to the key of gladness, we might say. What stands in the way of this gladness is envy, which in the sphere of humanity is the worst vice&#8230;</p></blockquote>
<p>This sharing of gladness, Arendt notes with an eye to <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/09/19/aristotle-friendship/">Aristotle&#8217;s ideas about friendship</a>, is the glue for the most powerful of human bonds:</p>
<blockquote><p>The ancients thought friends indispensable to human life, indeed that a life without friends was not really worth living. In holding this view they gave little consideration to the idea that we need the help of friends in misfortune; on the contrary, they rather thought that there can be no happiness or good fortune for anyone unless a friend shares in the joy of it. Of course there is something to the maxim that only in misfortune do we find out who our true friends are; but those whom we regard as our true friends without such proof are usually those to whom we unhesitatingly reveal happiness and whom we count on to share our rejoicing.</p>
<p>[&#8230;]</p>
<p>Humanity is exemplified not in fraternity but in friendship.</p></blockquote>
<p>Therein lies Arendt&#8217;s most salient, most beautiful point &#8212; it is in this act of connecting that we create the world:</p>
<blockquote><p>The world is not humane just because it is made by human beings, and it does not become humane just because the human voice sounds in it, but only when it has become the object of discourse. However much we are affected by the things of the world, however deeply they may stir and stimulate us, they become human for us only when we can discuss them with our fellows&#8230; We humanize what is going on in the world and in ourselves only by speaking of it, and in the course of speaking of it we learn to be human.</p></blockquote>
<p>Complement the thoroughly invigorating <a href="http://www.amazon.com/Men-Dark-Times-Hannah-Arendt/dp/0156588900/?tag=braipick-20" target="_blank"><strong><em>Men in Dark Times</em></strong></a> with Rebecca Solnit on <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2016/03/16/rebecca-solnit-hope-in-the-dark-2/">finding hope amid despair</a> and Albert Camus on <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2015/12/28/albert-camus-almond-trees-character/">how to ennoble our minds in difficult times</a>, then revisit Arendt on <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2015/12/02/hannah-arendt-the-life-of-the-mind-time-thinking/">time, space, and the thinking ego</a>, <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2014/09/16/hannah-arendt-the-life-of-the-mind/">the crucial difference between truth and meaning</a>, <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2015/10/14/hannah-arendt-life-of-the-mind-being-appearing/">our impulse for self-display</a>, and <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2014/10/14/hannah-arendt-life-of-the-mind-free-will/">what free will really means</a>.</p>

## 中文译文

“唯有通过言说，我们才能使世间发生之事以及我们自身内部发生之事变得富有人性；而正是在言说的过程中，我们才学会成为人。”

卡尔·荣格在思索生与死时曾写道：“人类存在的唯一目的，便是在纯粹存在的黑暗中点燃一盏明灯。”而这一切最令人惊叹之处在于：即便身处最缺氧、最令人窒息的境遇，我们仍能展现出惊人的创造力，去点燃那束光——这是一种精神层面的生存本能，其焕发的生命力与美，唯有被压迫者、被边缘化者，以及被主流社会放逐者，才拥有这份以痛苦为代价换来的特权去真切体认。

这份痛苦的特权，正是伟大的德国作家兼政治理论家汉娜·阿伦特（1906年10月14日－1975年12月4日）在《黑暗时代的人们》（初版于1968年，公共馆藏）一书中所探讨的主题。这本由多篇随笔组成的文集，在出版半个世纪之后，非但未显陈旧，反而愈发切中时弊、熠熠生辉。（值得怀着既遗憾又欣喜的复杂心情指出的是，该书标题诞生于一个伟大女性仍被默认为“他”的时代——而阿伦特本人，这位二十世纪最杰出的思想家之一、首位获邀主讲享有盛誉的吉福德讲座的女性，恰恰正是这一悖论的化身。）

汉娜·阿伦特，弗雷德·斯坦摄于1944年（照片由弗雷德·斯坦档案馆提供）

在维克多·弗兰克尔写下那部关于至暗时刻如何照亮人类意义追寻之路的不朽著作整整一代人之后，阿伦特写道：

即便在最黑暗的时代，我们仍有权期待某种启明；而这种启明，或许更少来自理论与概念，更多来自某些男女在其生命与作品中，在几乎一切境况下所点燃的、飘忽不定、摇曳微弱却始终不灭的光亮，并将其倾洒于他们被赋予的有限人生时光之中……我们这些早已习惯黑暗的眼睛，恐怕已难以分辨，那光究竟是烛火之微光，抑或骄阳之炽烈。然而，此类客观评判，在我看来仅属次要之事，尽可安然交付后世。

站在阿伦特的后世立场回望，有一件事确凿无疑——她关于自由、正义与人性的思想，比以往任何时候都更为炽烈耀眼。在丽贝卡·索尔尼特以澄澈而明亮的笔触为“黑暗中的希望”作出雄辩论述的半个多世纪之前，阿伦特便已写道：

世界存于人与人之间，而这“之间”……如今已成为全球几乎所有国家最令人忧心、也最显而易见的动荡之源。

阿伦特指出，在黑暗时代——即不公盛行、某些群体受制于另一些群体、个人自由岌岌可危的时代——这一“之间”的空间会发生某种奇妙的转化：“一种特殊形态的人性由此生成”，一种在受压迫者之间、彼此之间迸发的顽强情谊。在基尔克果沉思少数派力量一个多世纪之后，她写道：

人性最常借由这种兄弟情谊，在“黑暗时代”中彰显自身。当时代对某些人群而言变得极端晦暗，使其再无可能凭借自身的洞见或选择而退出世界之时，此种人性便成为一种必然。以手足之情为形式的人性，历史上向来恒常地出现在受迫害民族与被奴役群体之中……这种人性，是贱民族群的伟大特权；是此世所有贱民，无论何时何地，皆可拥有的、超越他者的优长。

阿伦特本人，不仅是一位闯入几乎全由男性主宰的知识疆域的女性思想家，亦属于这样一群贱民——早年即遭希特勒治下的德国驱逐的犹太人。正是从这私人经历与政治现实交汇之处，她进一步阐释道：

然而，阿伦特观察到，正是从这种“无世界性”的“之间”状态中，催生出另一种崭新的人文主义形态——一种无法在任何其他条件下复制或人为制造的、崭新的亲缘式人文主义：

仿佛在迫害的压力之下，受迫害者彼此靠得如此之近，以至于我们称之为“世界”的那个“之间”空间（当然，在迫害发生前，它本就存在于他们之间，将他们彼此隔开）竟彻底消失了。这便催生出一种人际关系的暖意，那些曾与这类群体有过接触的人，甚至会将其感知为一种近乎物理性的现象……当它充分发展时，便能孕育出一种仁慈与纯粹良善，而人类在其他情境下几乎难以企及。它也常常成为一种生命力的源泉，一种对“活着”这一简单事实本身所怀有的欢悦，仿佛生命唯有在世俗意义上遭受侮辱与伤害之人中间，方能真正臻于圆满。……但……这种人道主义，其最纯粹的形式乃是贱民的特权，它无法轻易传递，也无法被非贱民者轻易习得。单凭同情，抑或实际分担苦难，皆不足够。

阿伦特认为，问题的核心深处，蕴藏着某种更宏大、更开阔的东西——“无私的问题，抑或更确切地说，是‘对他者开放’的问题，而这实际上正是‘人性’在一切意义上的前提条件”；而这种开放，在其最高境界上，乃是对彼此喜悦的开放，而非仅仅对彼此苦难的开放：

显然，在这方面，分享喜悦绝对优于分享苦难。欢欣，而非悲伤，才是健谈的；真正意义上的人类对话，有别于单纯的闲聊，甚至有别于一般意义上的讨论，正在于它完全浸透着对他者及其言说的愉悦。我们不妨说，它调准了欢欣的音调。而阻碍这种欢欣的，是嫉妒——在人性领域中，嫉妒乃是最恶劣的恶德……

阿伦特援引亚里士多德关于友谊的思想指出，这种喜悦的分享，乃是人类最强大联结的黏合剂：

古人视朋友为人类生活的必需品，甚至认为没有朋友的人生根本不值一活。持此观点者，极少考虑人在不幸中需要朋友援助的想法；相反，他们倒更倾向于认为：若无人共享其喜悦，则任何人都不可能获得幸福或好运。诚然，“唯患难中方知真友”这句箴言自有其道理；但未经此类考验而被我们视为真友者，通常正是那些我们毫不犹豫向其袒露幸福、并笃信其必与我们同喜之人……人性并非体现于手足之情，而体现于友谊。

这便是阿伦特最切中要害、也最动人心魄的洞见——正是在这种联结的行动中，我们创造了世界：

世界之所以为人道，并非因为它由人类所造，亦非因为其中回响着人类的声音，而唯当它成为言说的对象时，方始成其为人道。无论世间万物如何触动我们，无论它们如何深切地激荡与鼓舞我们，它们唯有在我们能与同伴就此展开讨论之时，才真正成为属于我们的“人之物”……我们唯有通过言说，才能使世间发生之事以及我们自身内部发生之事变得富有人性；而正是在言说的过程中，我们才学会成为人。
