<p>A reader writes:</p>
<blockquote><p>I have recently made it to the second round of interviews for a role I’m very interested in. The conversation is with the person who is leaving the role I&#8217;m interviewing for.</p>
<p>I’ve never interviewed with the person who is currently in the job in question, but I take that to mean that she’s leaving the organization on good terms and for her own reasons, and that they trust her to make a recommendation on who will succeed her. Would you agree with that take on the situation, and if so what kinds of questions do you think I should ask or expect? How do I sell myself for the role without coming across as “I’m going to be better at this than you were,” which I’m sure would be a turn-off?</p></blockquote>
<p>There are two possibilities:</p>
<p>1. The interview is primarily for her to evaluate you as a candidate and, while you’ll still have the opportunity to ask your own questions, it’ll be more or less like any other interview and you should approach it that way.</p>
<p>2.. Or, the main purpose of this meeting is for you to be able to talk to the person who’s currently doing the job and get your own questions about the role answered. In this scenario, she will likely still provide feedback to the hiring manager about you and other candidates, but it’s not the primary purpose of the conversation.</p>
<p>Have they said anything to indicate which it is? Sometimes an employer will say something like, “We’d like to give you some time to talk with the person who’s doing the job now so she can tell you about the work with more nuance” — and that’s a sign that it’s more likely to be #2 (or at least <em>mostly</em> #2). Or they might not say anything like that in advance, but when you sit down with her she’ll make it clear that that’s the bulk of the agenda.</p>
<p>Either way, you should prepare for both scenarios — meaning that you should come into it expecting #1, but be ready with a lot of your own questions if it turns out to be #2. (You should be ready with a lot of <a href="https://www.askamanager.org/2018/01/what-are-the-best-questions-to-ask-in-a-job-interview.html">your own questions</a> regardless — because in either scenario it’s an opportunity to hear firsthand from the person who’s doing the job now — but if it turns out to be #2, you don’t want the conversation to stall because you only prepared a couple of questions.)</p>
<p>Questions you can ask the person who’s doing the job you’re interviewing for include things like the best things about the job, the most challenging things about the job, the <a href="https://www.askamanager.org/2022/01/dont-trust-the-answers-to-how-would-you-describe-your-management-style.html">manager’s management style</a>, secrets to success for doing well in the role, and whether there’s anything she was surprised by or wished she’d known before she started. You should also ask about <a href="https://www.askamanager.org/2019/08/how-can-i-avoid-jobs-with-horrible-hours.html">workload</a>, what the busiest times of the year are, and what those look like, because you might get a more accurate/honest answer than you will <a href="https://www.askamanager.org/2019/10/how-do-i-avoid-toxic-jobs.html">from others</a>. And depending on the job, you might ask technical questions too, like what software they’re using for X, or how they’re handling a particular known challenge with that software, etc.</p>
<p>As for selling yourself without coming across like you think you’ll be better at the job than she was … I’d argue you should never really be coming across that way in an interview, even when you’re not talking to the person you’d be replacing, since you can’t possibly know from the outside if it&#8217;s true! Good interviews don’t feel like sales pitches; the best ones feel like a conversation between two potential colleagues trying to figure out if a collaboration between them would make sense — and that’s how you should approach this too. Listen to what they’re looking for, talk about how you might be able to help with that, pull out things from your professional history that relate to what they need, and — while they’re assessing <i>you</i> — ask the questions that will help <a href="https://www.askamanager.org/2012/05/im-afraid-im-about-to-be-offered-a-job-i-wont-be-good-at.html">you assess them back</a>.</p>

## 中文译文

一位读者来信写道：

我最近成功进入了自己非常感兴趣的一个职位的第二轮面试。这次面试的对象，正是即将离职、腾出该职位的现任者。此前我从未与这位现任者进行过面试，因此我推测：她此次离职是和平离职，纯属个人原因，且公司信任她，愿意请她就继任人选给出推荐意见。您是否认同我对这一情况的理解？如果认同，那么您认为我应该提出哪些问题，或者又会遇到哪些问题？此外，我该如何在面试中展现自身优势，又不至于让对方觉得我在暗示“我会比你做得更好”——毕竟这种态度显然会令人反感。

我最近成功进入了自己非常感兴趣的一个职位的第二轮面试。这次面试的对象，正是即将离职、腾出该职位的现任者。

此前我从未与这位现任者进行过面试，因此我推测：她此次离职是和平离职，纯属个人原因，且公司信任她，愿意请她就继任人选给出推荐意见。您是否认同我对这一情况的理解？如果认同，那么您认为我应该提出哪些问题，或者又会遇到哪些问题？此外，我该如何在面试中展现自身优势，又不至于让对方觉得我在暗示“我会比你做得更好”——毕竟这种态度显然会令人反感。

这种情况存在两种可能：

1. 此次面试主要目的是由她对你作为候选人的资质进行评估；虽然你依然有机会提出自己的问题，但整体流程与其他常规面试并无二致，你也应以同样方式应对。

2. 或者，本次会面的主要目的，是让你有机会与现任者直接交流，就该职位的具体情况提出你关心的问题，并获得第一手解答。在此情形下，她很可能仍会向招聘经理反馈你及其他候选人的表现，但这并非本次谈话的核心目标。

雇主方面是否已透露过倾向哪一种情形？有时，雇主会明确说明：“我们希望安排你与现任者交流一段时间，让她更细致地为你介绍工作内容。”——这通常意味着情形#2（或至少主要是情形#2）的可能性更大。又或者，他们事前并未作任何提示，但当你真正与她坐下来交谈时，她会清楚表明：本次会面的重心正是为你答疑解惑。

无论哪种情形，你都应为两种可能性同时做好准备——即：以情形#1为基本预期进入面试，但若实际演变为情形#2，则须随时准备好大量你自己的问题。（事实上，无论属于哪一种情形，你本就该准备充足的问题，因为这是难得的机会，能直接听取现任者亲口讲述岗位实况；而一旦确认是情形#2，你绝不能因只草草准备了两三个问题，导致对话陷入冷场。）

你可以向这位现任者提出的问题包括：这份工作中最吸引人的方面是什么？最具挑战性的部分又是什么？直属上司的管理风格如何？胜任该岗位有哪些不为人知的成功诀窍？她在入职之初是否遇到过什么意料之外的情况，或有没有什么特别希望当初就了解的信息？你还可以询问工作量情况、一年中业务最繁忙的时段是何时，以及这些时段具体呈现怎样的工作状态——因为从她口中得到的答案，可能比从其他人那里听到的更为准确、坦诚。此外，根据岗位性质，你也可以提出一些技术性问题，例如：目前使用的是哪款软件来完成X任务？针对该软件已知的某项特定难题，团队又是如何应对的？

至于如何在不显得“我比你更胜任此职”的前提下有效展现自我……我想强调一点：你在任何面试中都不该流露出这种姿态，哪怕面对的并非你将要接替的人选——因为你根本无从知晓这种判断是否客观成立！一场优质的面试从来不该像一场推销演说；最好的面试，应当是一场两位潜在同事之间坦诚的对话，双方共同探讨彼此协作是否契合——你也应以这样的心态来面对本次会面。认真倾听对方所寻求的特质，结合自身经历，说明你如何能助力实现他们的目标；从你的职业履历中提炼出与岗位需求高度相关的实例；与此同时，在对方评估你的过程中，也主动提出那些有助于你反过来评估对方及该岗位的问题。
