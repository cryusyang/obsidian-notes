<p><a href="https://www.amazon.com/Miraculous-Material-Understanding-Wonders-Nature/dp/0593701488/?tag=braipick-20" target="_blank"><img alt="Grace Against Gravity and the Physics of Vulnerability: How Birds Fly and Why They Flock in a V Formation" class="cover alignright size-medium" height="417" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/lightman_miraculous.jpg?fit=320%2C417&amp;ssl=1" width="320" /></a></p><p>&#8220;What we see from the air is so simple and beautiful,&#8221; Georgia O&#8217;Keeffe wrote after her first airplane flight, &#8220;I cannot help feeling that it would do something wonderful for the human race &#8212; rid it of much smallness and pettiness if more people flew.&#8221; </p>
<p>I am writing this aboard an airplane. An earthbound ape in my airborne cage of metal and glass, I wonder who we would be, in the soul of the species, if we could fly &#8212; really fly, the way birds do; if we were born not just seeing &#8220;the world all simplified and beautiful and clear-cut in patterns,&#8221; as Georgia did out of that small round window, but feeling it. And yet you and I shall never know the open sky as a way of being &#8212; never know the touch of a thermal or the taste of a thundercloud, never see our naked shadow on a mountain or slice a cirrus with a wing. What cruel cosmic fate to live on this Pale Blue Dot without ever knowing its blueness. And yet we are recompensed by a consciousness capable of wonder &#8212; that edge state on the rim of understanding, where the mind touches mystery. </p>
<p>It is wonder that led us to invent science &#8212; that quickening of curiosity driving every discovery &#8212; so that science may repay us with magnified wonder as it reveals the weft and warp of nature &#8212; the tapestry of forces and phenomena, of subtleties and complexities, woven on the enchanted loom of reality. To look at any single thread more closely, in all its hidden wonder, is to see more clearly how the entire tapestry holds together, to strengthen how we ourselves hold together across the arc of life. For, as Rachel Carson <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/12/23/rachel-carson-on-wonder/">so memorably wrote</a>, the greatest gift you could give a child &#8212; or the eternal child in you &#8212; is &#8220;a sense of wonder so indestructible that it would last throughout life, as an unfailing antidote against the boredom and disenchantments&#8230; the sterile preoccupation with things that are artificial, the alienation from the sources of our strength.&#8221;</p>
<figure class="wp-caption alignnone"><a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2024/03/13/something-about-the-sky-carson-mcclure/"><img src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/somethingaboutthesky3.jpg" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text">Art by Nikki McClure from <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2024/03/13/something-about-the-sky-carson-mcclure/"><em>Something About the Sky</em></a> &#8212; Rachel Carson&#8217;s serenade to the wonder of the clouds</figcaption></figure>
<p>Take the wonder of a bird &#8212; this living poem of feather and physics, of barometric wizardry and hollow bone, in whose profoundly other brain <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2024/07/02/birds-dream-rem/">evolution invented dreams</a>. That so tiny a creature should defy the gravitational pull of an entire planet seems impossible, miraculous. And yet beneath this defiance is an active surrender to the same immutable laws that make the whole miracle of the universe possible. </p>
<p>In one of the three dozen fascinating essays collected in <a href="https://www.amazon.com/Miraculous-Material-Understanding-Wonders-Nature/dp/0593701488/?tag=braipick-20" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><strong><em>The Miraculous from the Material: Understanding the Wonders of Nature</em></strong></a> (<a href="https://search.worldcat.org/title/1423243347" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><em>public library</em></a>), the poetic physicist and novelist <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/tag/alan-lightman/">Alan Lightman</a> illuminates the lawful wonder of avian flight, from evolution to aerodynamics, from molecules to mathematics, beginning with the fundamental wonderment of how a bird creates strong enough an upward force to counter gravity&#8217;s pull on its weight:</p>
<blockquote><p>[The force] is created by a net upward air pressure, which in turn is created by the bird’s forward motion and the shape of its wings. The topside of an avian wing is curved, while the bottom side is rather flat. This difference in shape, together with the angle and some smaller adjustments of the wing, cause the air to flow over the top of the wing at higher speed than on the bottom. The higher speed on top reduces the air pressure above the wing compared to the air pressure below the wing. With more pressure pushing up from below than pressure pushing down from above, the wing gets an upward lift.</p></blockquote>

(Available <a href="https://society6.com/product/paul-sougy-the-pigeon-1950s-proceeds-benefit-the-nature-conservancy2463644_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">as a print</a>, benefitting The Nature Conservancy.)</figcaption></figure>
<p>It may seem counterintuitive that a higher air speed above the wing would produce a lower pressure, but our creaturely intuitions have often been poor reflections of reality &#8212; it took us eons to discern that the flat surface beneath our feet is a sphere, that the sphere is not at the center of the universe, and that there is an invisible force acting on objects without touching them to make the universe cohere &#8212; a force which <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2020/04/06/newton-plague/">a bored twenty-something sitting in his mother&#8217;s apple orchard</a> called gravity. </p>
<p>Alan explains the reality of chemistry and physics that makes flight possible as air molecules strike against the underside of the wing to lift the bird up:</p>
<blockquote><p>Air consists of little molecules that push against whatever they strike, causing pressure. Molecules of air are constantly whizzing about in all directions. If no energy is added, the total speed of the molecules must be constant, by the law of the conservation of energy. But that speed is composed of two parts: a horizontal speed, parallel to the wing, and a vertical speed, perpendicular to the wing. Increase the horizontal speed of air molecules above the wing, and the vertical speed of those molecules must decrease. Lower speed of molecules striking the wing from above means less pressure, or less push. The molecules on the bottom of the wing, moving slower in the horizontal direction but faster in the vertical direction (with greater upward pressure), lift the wing upward.</p>
<p>The lift is greater the larger the wing area and the faster the speed of air past the wing. There’s a convenient trade-off here. The necessary lift force to counterbalance the bird’s weight can be had with less wing area if the animal increases its forward speed, and vice versa. Birds capitalize on this option according to their individual needs. <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2024/09/11/great-blue-heron/">The great blue heron</a>, for example, has long, slender legs for wading and must fly slowly so as not to break them on landing. Consequently, herons have relatively large wingspan. Pheasants, on the other hand, maneuver in underbrush and would find large wings cumbersome. To remain airborne with their relatively short and stubby wings, pheasants must fly fast.</p></blockquote>

<p>There are, however, limits to this factorial conversation between surface and speed. Alan considers why there are no birds the size of elephants:</p>
<blockquote><p>As you scale up the size of a bird or any material thing, unless you drastically change its shape, its weight increases faster than its area. Weight is proportional to volume, or length times length times length, while area is proportional to length times length. Double the length, and the weight is eight times larger, while the area is only four times larger. For example, if you have a cube of 1 inch on a side, its volume is 1 cubic inch, while its total area is 6 (sides) × 1 square inch, or 6 square inches. If you double the side of the cube to 2 inches, its volume goes up to 8 cubic inches, or 800 percent (with a similar increase in weight), while its area goes up to 24 square inches, or 400 percent. Since the lift force is proportional to the wing area while the opposing weight force is proportional to the bird’s volume, as you continue scaling up, eventually you reach a point where the bird’s wing area is not enough to keep it aloft. Although birds have been experimenting with flight for 100 million years, the heaviest true flying bird, the great bustard, rarely exceeds 42 pounds. The larger gliding birds, such as vultures, are lifted by rising hot air columns and don’t carry their full weight.</p></blockquote>
<p>But all this elaborate molecular and mathematical aerodynamics of upward motion is not enough to make flight possible &#8212; birds must also propel themselves forward without propellers. For a long time, how they do this was a mystery. (The mystery was even deeper for the singular flight of the hummingbird, <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2021/05/07/sy-montgomery-the-hummingbirds-gift/">hovering between science and magic</a>.) It was the birth of modern aviation that finally shed light on it. In the early nineteenth century, watching how birds glide, the pioneering engineer and aerial investigator George Cayley became the first human being to discern the mechanics of flight, identifying the three forces acting on the weight of any flying body: lift, drag, and thrust. </p>
<figure class="wp-caption alignnone"><a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2024/06/07/swan-sky-tejima/"><img alt="" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-83761" height="461" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/swansky_tejima9-1.jpg?resize=680%2C461&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text">Art by Keizaburō Tejima from <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2024/06/07/swan-sky-tejima/"><em>Swan Sky</em></a></figcaption></figure>
<p>Alan details the physics of drag and thrust that allow birds to move forward:</p>
<blockquote><p>Birds do in fact have propellers, in the form of specially designed feathers in the outer halves of their wings. These feathers, called primaries, change their shape and position during a wingbeat. Forward thrust is obtained by pushing air backward with each flap. In a similar manner, we are able to move forward in a swimming pool by vigorously moving our arms backward against the water.</p></blockquote>
<p>All of this helps explain why larger birds often fly in a V formation &#8212; each bird benefits from the uplifting air pockets produced by the bird in front of it, conserving 20 to 30 percent of the calories needed for flight compared to flying solo. Because the lead bird takes most of the aerodynamic and caloric brunt shielding the rest from the wind, the flock takes turns in the frontmost position. </p>
<p>This, too, is the physics of any healthy community, any healthy relationship &#8212; the physics of vulnerability and trust. Because life always exerts different pressures on each person at different times, internal or external, thriving together is not a matter of always pulling equal weight but of accommodating the ebb and flow of one another&#8217;s vulnerability, each trusting the other to shield them in times of depletion, then doing the shielding when replenished. One measure of love may be the willingness to be the lead bird shielding someone dear in their time of struggle, lifting up their wings with your stubborn presence.</p>

## 中文译文

“从空中俯瞰，我们所见的一切如此简洁而美丽。”乔治亚·欧姬芙在首次乘飞机飞行后写道，“我不禁觉得，倘若更多人能飞上天空，这将为人类带来某种奇妙的改变——使人类摆脱诸多狭隘与琐碎。”

此刻，我正坐在一架飞机上写作。身为一个被囚禁于金属与玻璃制成的空中牢笼中的、扎根于大地的猿类，我不禁思索：倘若我们真能如鸟类一般飞翔——并非借助机械，而是生来便拥有这种能力；倘若我们不仅如乔治亚透过那扇小小的圆窗所见，目睹“整个世界变得简洁、美丽、轮廓清晰”，更能切身感受它，那么作为物种的我们，在灵魂深处又会是怎样的存在？然而，你我永远无法将开阔的天空视作自身存在的自然状态——永远无法亲身体验热气流的抚触，或尝到雷雨云的味道；永远无法看见自己赤裸的身影投映在山巅，也无法用羽翼劈开一缕卷云。何其残酷的宇宙宿命：我们栖居于这颗淡蓝色的星球之上，却终生无缘体认它的湛蓝。然而，我们亦获得补偿——一种足以萌生惊奇的意识；那是理解之边缘的临界状态，心智在此处轻轻触碰到神秘。

正是惊奇引领我们发明了科学——这股好奇心的跃动，驱动着每一次发现；而科学则以加倍的惊奇回馈我们，向我们揭示自然的经纬——那由力量与现象、精微与繁复共同织就的锦缎，正于现实这架施了魔法的织机上徐徐展开。只要更贴近地凝视其中任何一根丝线，细细体味它所蕴藏的隐秘惊奇，我们便能更清晰地看清整幅锦缎如何彼此勾连、浑然一体，也由此加固我们自身在生命长河中彼此支撑、相互维系的方式。正如蕾切尔·卡森曾令人难忘地写道：你能给予孩子——或给予你内心那个永恒的孩子——最珍贵的礼物，便是“一种坚不可摧的惊奇感，它将贯穿一生，成为抵御无聊与幻灭……抵御对人工之物的贫瘠执迷、以及疏离于我们力量源泉的永恒解药。”

妮基·麦克卢尔绘《天空之妙》——蕾切尔·卡森献给云朵奇迹的颂歌

再看鸟儿的惊奇——这由羽毛与物理法则写就的活体诗篇，蕴含气压变化的玄妙技艺与中空骨骼的轻盈构造；在其迥异于人类的深邃大脑中，进化甚至孕育出了梦境。如此微小的生命竟能违抗整颗行星的引力牵引，这看似不可能，近乎神迹。然而，在这份“违抗”背后，实则是一种主动的臣服——臣服于同样恒定不变的自然法则；正是这些法则，让宇宙间一切奇迹成为可能。

在《物质中的奇迹：理解自然之奇观》（公共馆藏）一书所辑录的三十余篇引人入胜的散文中，诗人兼物理学家、小说家艾伦·莱特曼在其中一篇里，以诗意笔触照亮了鸟类飞行这一“有法可循的惊奇”：从演化历程到空气动力学，从分子结构到数学原理。他首先叩问最根本的惊奇：一只鸟如何产生足够强大的向上作用力，以抗衡重力对其体重的下拉？

[这股力]源于净向上的空气压力，而该压力又由鸟的前向运动及其翅膀的形状共同产生。鸟翼上表面呈弧形，下表面则相对扁平。这种上下表面的形状差异，配合翅膀的迎角及一些细微调整，使得气流流经翼面上方的速度高于下方。上方气流速度加快，导致翼面顶部的气压低于底部气压。由于下方气压向上推举的力量大于上方气压向下压迫的力量，翅膀便获得了向上的升力。

或许令人费解的是：翼面上方气流速度越快，气压反而越低。但我们的生物直觉常常是对现实拙劣的映照——人类耗费了数个地质纪元，才终于认识到脚下平坦的大地其实是个球体；认识到这个球体并非宇宙中心；认识到存在着一种无形之力，无需接触物体即可作用于其上，维系着宇宙的秩序——一位百无聊赖、坐在母亲苹果园里的二十多岁青年，将它命名为“引力”。

艾伦进一步阐释了使飞行成为可能的化学与物理现实：当空气分子撞击鸟翼下表面时，便产生了托举之力。

空气由无数微小分子构成，它们撞击任何物体表面时都会形成压力。空气分子始终朝四面八方高速运动。若无额外能量输入，依据能量守恒定律，分子的总速度必须保持恒定。但这一总速度由两部分组成：平行于翼面的水平速度，以及垂直于翼面的垂直速度。当翼面上方空气分子的水平速度增加时，其垂直速度必然减小。自上方撞击翼面的分子速度降低，意味着压力减小，即向下的推力减弱。而翼面下方的分子，其水平方向运动较慢，但垂直方向运动更快（因而产生更强的向上压力），从而将翅膀向上托起。升力的大小取决于翼面面积的大小，以及气流掠过翼面的速度。此处存在一项便捷的权衡：若鸟儿加快前飞速度，便可用较小的翼面面积获得平衡自身重量所需的升力；反之亦然。鸟类依各自需求灵活运用这一策略。例如，大蓝鹭拥有细长双腿，便于涉水觅食，故须缓慢飞行，以免着陆时折断双腿；因此，大蓝鹭的翼展相对宽大。而雉鸡则需在灌木丛中灵活穿行，过大的翅膀反成累赘。为凭借其短而粗壮的双翼维持飞行，雉鸡必须高速飞行。

然而，表面积与速度之间的这种精妙互动亦有其极限。艾伦进而探讨为何不存在大象般庞大的鸟类：

当你按比例放大一只鸟（或任何实体）的尺寸，除非彻底改变其外形，否则其重量的增长速度将快于表面积。重量与体积成正比，即长度×长度×长度；而表面积则与长度×长度成正比。长度翻倍，重量变为原来的八倍，而表面积仅增至四倍。举例而言，一个边长为1英寸的立方体，其体积为1立方英寸，总表面积为6（个面）×1平方英寸＝6平方英寸。若将其边长增至2英寸，体积则升至8立方英寸（即增长800%，重量亦同步增长），而表面积仅增至24平方英寸（即增长400%）。由于升力与翼面面积成正比，而与其对抗的重力则与鸟的体积成正比；持续放大体型，终将抵达一个临界点：翼面面积已不足以支撑其重量使其腾空。尽管鸟类已为飞行实验了整整一亿年，现存最重的真正飞鸟——大鸨——体重极少超过42磅。至于体型更大的滑翔鸟类（如秃鹫），则依靠上升的热气流托举，无需承载全部体重。
