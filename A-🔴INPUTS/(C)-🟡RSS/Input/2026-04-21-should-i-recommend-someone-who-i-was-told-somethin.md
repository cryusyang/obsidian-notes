<p>A reader writes:</p>
<blockquote><p>Years ago, I got to know Fergus, the head of a local organization I worked with through my previous job. Fergus eventually left the organization to pursue other opportunities, and shortly afterward, I had a meeting with other members of the team, including the person who had succeeded him. At one point in the conversation, I asked if they knew how Fergus was doing and received a fairly non-committal answer.</p>
<p>A couple days later, I received an email from the new manager that had very clearly been written by lawyers, informing me that after Fergus&#8217; departure they had discovered financial improprieties during his time running the organization and had severed all ties with him. This was surprising to me because, while I had never worked closely with Fergus, he had never given me any reason to question his integrity.</p>
<p>A few months after that, the CEO of my organization mentioned to me that he had had conversations with Fergus about joining our team. I felt duty-bound to tell him about the email I had received. I&#8217;m not sure how much of an impact that had, but in any event he never came to work for us.</p>
<p>Fast forward to last week when Fergus, with whom I&#8217;ve stayed in touch with over the years, asked me for an introduction to the CEO of a company where he is applying for a job. My instinct is to let bygones be bygones and make the introduction. It&#8217;s been five years and I don&#8217;t even know the details of what he was alleged to have done, much less whether it&#8217;s true. And as I said, other than this one incident, I&#8217;ve never had any reason to doubt Fergus&#8217; integrity.</p>
<p>Still, I&#8217;ve found myself wondering, if I felt an obligation to tell my boss about the email five years ago, why wouldn&#8217;t the same obligation extend to my professional contacts at this other company? (I know the CEO, but not particularly well, and he&#8217;s certainly not someone I would consider a friend.)</p>
<p>There&#8217;s also the question of, if I do make the referral, whether I should give Fergus an enthusiastic recommendation or simply pass along his resume without comment. Given how difficult it is for job candidates to stand out these days, I almost feel as if the latter action would be equivalent to not making the intro at all.</p></blockquote>
<p>Ugh, this is hard. The fact that Fergus had never given you reason to question his integrity doesn’t mean that he wasn’t involved in financial improprieties; in fact, the way many successful embezzlers (to use one example) are able to get away with it for a long time is that they come across as friendly and trustworthy.</p>
<p>On the other hand, it’s a little odd that the other organization felt the need to send you that letter. Was there any reason for them to spill Fergus’s business like that, other than sullying his name? Maybe there was! Depending on the work Fergus did, there could be reasons that you/your organization needed to know what happened. But if there weren&#8217;t, I’d be uncomfortable with that and trying to figure out why I was being informed.</p>
<p>In any case, when your CEO mentioned he was considering hiring him, you were right to share what you&#8217;d been told with him. You had relevant info that he had the right to consider.</p>
<p>It’s different in this latest situation, where you don’t work for the person he’s applying with, so there’s not as clear an imperative. But Fergus is asking you to use your reputation to vouch for him. Before you can do that, I think you’ve got to know more. Would you be willing to ask Fergus point-blank about what happened with the old job? You could say, “Before I contact Joe, can I ask about what happened when you left OldOrg? My sense was that there might have been some issues there, and candidly I feel like I’ve got to ask you that first. I’m sorry if I&#8217;m putting you in an awkward position.”</p>
<p>This won’t necessarily clarify things for you, but it might. Or it might further muddy them! But I don’t see how you can vouch for him — which is what you’d be doing — without at least asking him about it. If you don’t want to do that, I don’t think you can ethically refer him, given the info you do have. And so if he didn’t do anything wrong, it’s actually fairer to give him a chance to clear things up.</p>

## 中文译文

一位读者来信写道：

多年前，我通过上一份工作结识了弗格斯——一家本地组织的负责人。后来，弗格斯离开了该组织，转而追寻其他发展机会；不久之后，我与该团队其他成员（包括接替他职位的人）开了一次会。交谈中，我随口问起大家是否知道弗格斯近况如何，得到的回答颇为含糊、模棱两可。

几天后，我收到新任主管发来的一封邮件。这封邮件措辞明显经过律师审阅，明确告知我：弗格斯离职后，组织在其任职期间发现了财务方面的不当行为，并已与其彻底断绝一切往来。这令我颇感意外——尽管我从未与弗格斯共事密切，但他此前从未表现出任何有损诚信之处，也从未让我对他的人品产生过丝毫疑虑。

几个月后，我所在机构的首席执行官向我提及，他正与弗格斯就加入我们团队一事进行初步沟通。我当时深感有责任将此前收到的那封邮件内容如实相告。我不确定此举产生了多大影响，但最终弗格斯确实未曾入职我们公司。

时间快进到上周：多年来一直与我保持联系的弗格斯，请我为他引荐一家他正在应聘的公司的首席执行官。我的本能反应是“既往不咎”，欣然应允引荐。毕竟事情已过去五年，我甚至不清楚当年所谓“不当行为”的具体细节，更无从判断其真实性。如前所述，除却这一桩事件外，我从未有任何理由怀疑弗格斯的正直品格。

然而，我却不禁自问：倘若五年前我有义务将那封邮件内容告知我的上司，那么如今面对另一家公司的职业人脉，这份义务是否同样适用？（我虽认识那家公司CEO，但交情并不深厚，更谈不上是朋友。）

此外还有一个问题：倘若我决定引荐，究竟该热情洋溢地为他背书，还是仅将他的简历转交、不做任何评价？在当下求职者竞争如此激烈的环境中，后者几乎等同于拒绝引荐。

哎呀，这真难办。弗格斯此前从未让你质疑其诚信，并不等于他未涉及财务不当行为；事实上，许多成功的挪用公款者（仅举一例）之所以能长期得逞，恰恰因为他们给人以友善可信的印象。

另一方面，那家组织竟特意向你发送这样一封邮件，也略显奇怪。他们究竟有何必要如此公开弗格斯的私事？难道仅仅为了抹黑他的声誉？或许确有其他原因！视弗格斯所从事工作的性质而定，也许你或你的机构确有必要了解相关情况。但若并无此必要，我对此举便会心生不安，并试图弄清自己为何会被特意告知此事。

无论如何，当你们公司的首席执行官提到有意聘用弗格斯时，你如实转达所获知的信息是完全正确的——你提供了对方有权知晓且应予考量的相关信息。

但此次情况则有所不同：你并不受雇于弗格斯正在应聘的那家公司，因此并不存在同等明确的责任义务。然而，弗格斯却是请求你动用个人声誉为其担保。在你做出这一举动之前，我认为你必须掌握更多信息。你是否愿意直接、坦率地向弗格斯询问当年旧职的情况？你可以这样说：“在我联系乔之前，能否请你谈谈当初离开‘老组织’时发生了什么？我隐约听说当时可能有些问题，坦白说，我觉得有必要先向你确认这一点。若因此让你感到尴尬，还请见谅。”

这未必能为你厘清全部事实，但或许有所帮助；又或者，反而会使情况更加扑朔迷离！但在我看来，在至少没有就此向他本人求证之前，你实在无法为他作保——而这恰恰正是引荐行为的本质。倘若你不愿开口询问，那么基于你目前已知的信息，从伦理角度出发，你便不应为他提供推荐。反过来说，倘若他确实清白无辜，给予他一次澄清的机会，其实才更显公平。
