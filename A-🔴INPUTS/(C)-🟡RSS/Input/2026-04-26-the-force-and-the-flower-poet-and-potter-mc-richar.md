<p>On Valentine&#8217;s Day 1971, a year after the publication of her classic <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2020/01/03/m-c-richards-centering/"><em>Centering</em></a>, the poet and potter <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/tag/m-c-richards/">M.C. Richards</a> (July 13, 1916&ndash;September 10, 1999) was invited to speak at an arts festival in Maine. Going &#8220;from horticulture to alchemy to the history of consciousness, with a few poems sprinkled in, and relying heavily on paradox,&#8221; the address she delivered, later included in <a href="https://www.amazon.com/Crossing-Point-Selected-Talks-Writings/dp/0819540609/?tag=braipick-20" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><strong><em>The Crossing Point: Selected Talks and Writings</em></strong></a> (<a href="https://search.worldcat.org/title/767498410" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><em>public library</em></a>), is one of the most honest, imaginative, and articulate investigations of creativity I have encountered &#8212; a bold defiance of the fracturing of culture anchored in the passionate insistence that &#8220;the center is everywhere,&#8221; that it is &#8220;made up of differences, uniquenesses, in a tissue of relationships, interactions, interpenetrations.&#8221;</p>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_69641"><a href="https://www.amazon.com/Centering-Pottery-Poetry-Caroline-Richards/dp/0819562009/?tag=braipick-20" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-69641" height="1354" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/mcrichards.jpg?resize=680%2C1354&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text">Mary Caroline Richards at Black Mountain College (Getty Research Institute. Photographer unknown.)</figcaption></figure>
<p>At the center of her cosmogony of creativity are the connections between the life of the individual human being and the life of the universe; between the inner invisible realm, which she calls &#8220;the force,&#8221; and the outer visible realm of its manifestation, which she calls the &#8220;the flower&#8221;; between the different fields of study and work through which we explore these realms. She writes:</p>
<blockquote><p>Artists are sometimes particularly attuned to these connections, scientists too, mystics too&#8230; There may be a message in this way of working. Maybe that’s what a subject is, a gathering of ideas as set in motion by a central impulse. Like a magnetic field. Start the field going, and elements begin to swarm. By what logic? By attraction. By resonance. Maybe that’s what relevance is: the feeling of attraction and resonance between ideas and people.</p></blockquote>
<p>This feeling, Richard observes, is what we call creativity &#8212; the mystery to which we try to give shape in matter &#8212; and it begins not in the mind but in the heart. She considers the force by which the cabbage flowers:</p>
<blockquote><p>Cabbage&#8230; grows a big heart. Out of this heart come leaves. As the leaves grow, the heart grows. The cabbage gets its leaves from the inside, where there aren’t any. Cabbages grow from the inside, from the heart. And by growing they create their hearts. </p>
<p>A neurophysiologist from Yale says that brains too are created in this way: from un-brain forces. He says that the human brain is created by thinking, that ideas and values create chemical reactions in tissue. Like a cabbage, somehow the physical form grows from an invisible realm.</p>
<p>This invisible realm must be a powerfully creative region. It furnishes us not only with cabbages and brains, but with our scientific hypotheses, religious experiences, and works of art.</p></blockquote>
<p>With the recognition that works of art begin with &#8220;a feeling for things, a feeling which is a way of knowing about things,&#8221; she adds:</p>
<blockquote><p>We tend to call any undertaking an art when it seems to be drawing upon the fullness of inner feeling and upon careful regard for physical expression. To live and to work in the world mindful of the processes which are necessary to infuse matter with soul forces, to use techniques on behalf of living forms, is a great art.</p></blockquote>
<p>In this sense, she observes, living itself is an art &#8212; the art of connection. Just as Erich Fromm was formulating the ideas that would become <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2016/03/23/erich-fromm-the-art-of-living/"><em>The Art of Being</em></a>, Richards writes:</p>
<blockquote><p>Life is best understood and practiced as an art, the way that art is understood and practiced. We rely on inspiration, feeling for materials, knowledge of how to put things together well, patience, physical strength and awareness that we are part of a process which we don’t know much about yet but which we live within and are sustained by. The verbal arts we practice, or visual arts, or graphic arts, or theater arts, or musical arts, or liberal arts, are part of something. They are not the whole story. And they are interconnected at the center with all the other parts.</p></blockquote>

<p>Pulsating beneath this interconnected totality is the essence of all creative work. While the young Jane Goodall was <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2025/10/01/jane-goodall-letters-art/">contemplating the indivisibility of art and science</a>, Richards considers what creativity in all its forms asks of us and what it gives us:</p>
<blockquote><p>Total concentration, total focus, enjoyment, discovery, inner effort, creating something, feeling secure in the process yet not knowing or demanding to know how it will come out. Many of the things we do may have this quality. Take gardening, for example, or making lab experiments, or working out a new equation, or cooking supper, or having a child, or teaching a class, or running a college, or praying, or going for a walk, or getting married, or dying.</p></blockquote>
<p>This feeling of generative not-knowing &#8212; something the artist Ann Hamilton <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2016/12/12/making-not-knowing-ann-hamilton/">so beautifully articulated</a> a generation after Richards &#8212; is also our best path to knowledge, integral to the creative process of science: </p>
<blockquote><p>When we live in the spirit of science, we live in a quality of inquiry, of wonder. We put one foot in front of the other, standing firmly balanced on the earth, finding our way on. Each step is both an answer and a question. We both know and don’t know what we are doing&#8230; We need to learn to hear the <em>yes</em>in the <em>no</em>; the <em>no</em> in the <em>yes</em>. To hear what is not said. To see what is not visible.</p></blockquote>
<p>This, of course, is why <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/uiv-book/">poetry and science so naturally meet</a>, why <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2026/03/09/sprezzatura/">openness to wonder</a> may be the best measure and the deepest meaning of our aliveness, the wellspring from which everything that is creative springs. </p>

## 中文译文

最近，我与诗人物理学家朋友艾伦·莱特曼（Alan Lightman）展开了一场对话，就“创造精神是否可被有益地划分为互补的文艺与科学两部分”（艾伦的观点）抑或“二者不过是人类持续渴望弥合物质与奥秘、从而赋予存在以意义这一根本诉求的不同入口”（我的观点）展开了思辨。这场交锋让我想起了一位二十世纪最具原创性思想与最明亮精神的人物——她一段曾被遗忘的演讲。

1971年情人节，即其经典著作《中心》（Centering）出版一年后，诗人兼陶艺家M.C.理查兹（M.C. Richards，1916年7月13日－1999年9月10日）受邀赴缅因州一场艺术节发表演讲。这场演讲“从园艺学跃至炼金术，再跃至意识史，其间穿插几首诗作，大量倚赖悖论”，后来收入《交汇点：精选讲稿与文集》（The Crossing Point: Selected Talks and Writings，公共馆藏图书）。这是我所读到的关于创造力最真诚、最具想象力、也最富表现力的探索之一——它大胆挑战文化割裂的现状，以炽热的信念坚称：“中心无处不在”，它“由差异与独特性构成，在关系之网、互动之流与彼此渗透之中生成”。

理查兹指出，这种感受，正是我们所称的“创造力”——即我们试图以物质为媒介赋予其形态的那个奥秘；而它的起点，并非头脑，而是心灵。她以卷心菜开花之力为例：

卷心菜……长出一颗硕大的心。叶片即由此心生发。随着叶片生长，心亦随之壮大。卷心菜的叶片自其内部萌生，而那里原本空无一物。卷心菜自内而长，自心而生；它在生长过程中，不断创造着自己的心。耶鲁大学一位神经生理学家指出，大脑亦以同样方式生成：由“非脑之力”生成。他说，人类大脑乃由思考所造就，观念与价值会在组织中引发化学反应。正如卷心菜一般，某种意义上，有形之体正从那不可见之域生长而出。这不可见之域必然是一个极具创造力的区域——它不仅赐予我们卷心菜与大脑，也赐予我们科学假说、宗教体验与艺术作品。

在此意义上，她指出，生活本身即是一门艺术——联结的艺术。就在埃里希·弗洛姆（Erich Fromm）酝酿日后成书《存在的艺术》（The Art of Being）之际，理查兹写道：

生命最宜被理解并践行为一门艺术，一如我们理解并践行艺术本身。我们仰赖灵感、对材质的体悟、对如何精妙组合事物的认知、耐心、体力，以及一种清醒的自觉：我们自身即是某个宏大过程的一部分——尽管我们对其所知甚少，却正栖居其中，并为其所滋养。我们所习练的语言艺术、视觉艺术、平面设计艺术、戏剧艺术、音乐艺术或人文艺术，皆属于某种更大整体的一部分；它们并非全部故事，且在中心处与其他所有部分彼此交织、互为依存。

在这彼此交织的整体脉动之下，潜藏着一切创造性工作的本质。当年轻的珍·古道尔（Jane Goodall）正思索艺术与科学不可分割的统一性时，理查兹则思考着：一切形式的创造力向我们索取什么，又馈赠我们什么？
