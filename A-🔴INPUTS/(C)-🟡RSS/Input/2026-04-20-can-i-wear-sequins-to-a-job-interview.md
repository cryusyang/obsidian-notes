<p>A reader writes:</p>
<blockquote><p>Something happened to me 15 years ago that I continue to wonder about. When I was a senior in college, I was applying to internships in my field (comms/PR if it matters) in Washington, D.C., with the help of my academic advisor.</p>
<p>One in-person interview at one of the big legacy PR firms went really well. When my academic advisor followed up about it, they said the company thought I was a fantastic candidate and they&#8217;d absolutely love to hire me, except for one thing: they thought the shirt I was wearing was inappropriate for an interview setting and, particularly, that it had sequins on it. Ultimately, I did not get the fellowship because of it.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.askamanager.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/sequins.jpeg"><img alt="" class="alignleft size-medium wp-image-37528" height="300" src="https://www.askamanager.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/sequins-300x300.jpeg" width="300" /></a>I found an almost exact replica of the shirt that I’m attaching. If I recall correctly, I wore it with a nicely tailored black pantsuit that I was very proud to have purchased on my limited college budget.</p>
<p>Do you think that the company was right in 2010 (given I was a 23-year-old who knew nothing about the working world at the time, beyond a few internships, and in particular the dress code standards of the time) or not? Would you have made the same call 16 years ago? And, do you think this would still happen in 2026? Should we be warning new grads away from all sequins? For the record, I would not wear that shirt now — but really only because it&#8217;s very 2010s. I remember it being part of my regular office job rotation once I got my first job later that year.</p></blockquote>
<p>I wouldn’t recommend sequins at a job interview at all, then or now, just because they tend to read more “nighttime attire” than professional interview wear &#8230; unless you’re in a field with a lot more leeway than D.C. communications firms tend to have. D.C. is notoriously conservative about work wear.</p>
<p>But it’s a ridiculous reason not to hire you — particularly since you were 23 and still figuring this stuff out, but even if you’d been older.</p>
<p>And as sequins go, this particular shirt is less of an issue than, like, a full sequined top or sequined dress would be &#8212; and the fact that you were wearing it under the jacket of a pantsuit makes their reaction even more over-the-top.</p>
<p>I’d put it in the category of stuff I’d advise a candidate not to wear in order to make the most professional impression, but wouldn&#8217;t advise an employer not to hire over (because it really doesn’t matter). And a candidate who they said was fantastic and who they&#8217;d otherwise love to hire &#8212; in other words, where you obviously didn&#8217;t give them any other reason to doubt your judgment, and where this could be easily solved by explaining their dress code to you upon hire? Absurd.</p>

## 中文译文

一位读者来信写道：

15年前发生在我身上的一件事，至今仍让我百思不得其解。当时我正读大四，在学术导师的帮助下，向华盛顿特区几家我所学专业（传播/公关，若这有关系的话）的实习岗位投递简历。其中一次，我在一家老牌大型公关公司参加线下面试，过程非常顺利。后来，我的学术导师跟进询问结果，对方回复说：他们认为我是一位极为出色的候选人，非常希望能录用我——但有一个问题：他们觉得我当天所穿的衬衫不适合面试场合，尤其指出上面缀有亮片。最终，我因这件衬衫而未能获得该实习职位。

我找到了一件几乎一模一样的衬衫，随信附上。据我回忆，当时我搭配了一套剪裁合体的黑色裤装西装——那是我用极其有限的大学预算购置的，为此还颇为自豪。

想请教您：以2010年的标准来看（彼时我23岁，除了几段实习经历外，对职场几乎一无所知，更遑论当时通行的着装规范），这家公司的决定是否合理？若您身处16年前，会做出同样的判断吗？而到了2026年，类似情况还会发生吗？我们是否应当告诫应届毕业生彻底避开所有带亮片的服饰？坦白说，如今我不会再穿这件衬衫了——但主要原因只是它太“2010年代”了。我记得，就在那年晚些时候我入职第一份正式工作后，它还曾是我日常办公穿搭的一部分。

无论过去还是现在，我都不会建议求职者在面试时穿着带亮片的服装，因为亮片通常给人的感觉更偏向“晚间着装”，而非专业面试所需的得体形象……除非你所从事的行业比华盛顿特区的公关公司拥有更大的着装自由度。而众所周知，华盛顿特区的职场着装向来以保守著称。

但仅凭这一点就拒绝录用你，实在荒谬至极——尤其考虑到你当时才23岁，尚在摸索职场规则；即便你年长一些，这一理由也依然站不住脚。

况且，就亮片而言，你这件衬衫的问题远不如整件亮片上衣或亮片连衣裙那么严重；更何况你还把它穿在一套裤装西装的外套之下——如此反应，未免过于夸张。

我会将这类着装归入“为呈现最专业形象，求职者宜避免穿着”的范畴，但绝不会建议雇主因此放弃录用某位候选人（毕竟这真的无关紧要）。更何况，对方已明确表示你是一位“极为出色”的候选人，且“非常希望聘用你”——换言之，你显然并未在其他方面令他们质疑你的判断力；而这一着装问题，完全可在录用后通过简单说明公司着装规范便轻松解决。如此因小失大，实在荒唐。
