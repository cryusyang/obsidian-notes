<h3>Reflected XSS in Parser Endpoint — $366 Bounty Earned</h3><p>While testing the <strong>Parser service of a SaaS automation platform (example.com)</strong>, I identified a <strong>Reflected Cross-Site Scripting (XSS)</strong> vulnerability. The issue was caused by improper handling of the error_description parameter, which was directly reflected in the HTML response.</p><p>Interestingly, I had tested this target a year ago and missed this endpoint. When I came back later with a deeper approach, I found not only this XSS but multiple other bugs in the same flow. This shows why re-testing with fresh eyes is always valuable.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*3377YQ4tgjS1fUxedQ-7pA.png" /></figure><h3>Understanding the Target</h3><p>The <strong>Parser service</strong> is part of the platform’s email-to-automation workflow. Its purpose is to parse incoming email content, validate it, and trigger automated actions (“Zaps”).</p><p>During error handling, the service displays error messages back to the user when something goes wrong in the parsing/validation stage. That’s where I noticed the parameter error_description being reflected in the UI.</p><p><strong>Key observation:</strong></p><ul><li>Error messages are often overlooked by developers.</li><li>They commonly take backend error strings and push them into the front-end response.</li><li>Perfect spot to check for <strong>XSS or HTML injection</strong>.</li></ul><h3>Root Cause &amp; Flow</h3><ul><li>The endpoint /complete/parser/ accepts query parameters like error and error_description</li><li>Instead of sanitizing, the app <strong>directly reflected the value of error_description</strong> into the HTML response.</li><li>This created a sink where user-controlled input could become executable JavaScript.</li></ul><p>So the flow looked like this:</p><blockquote>User Input (error_description param) → Server → HTML Response → Browser Executes</blockquote><h3>Steps to Reproduce</h3><ol><li>Navigate to the vulnerable endpoint.</li></ol><p><a href="https://example.com/complete/parser/?error=invalid_request&amp;error_description=Transform+algorithm+not+supported.%3Cscript%3Ealert(document.cookie)%3C/script%3E&amp;state=cc%0Ass">https://example.com/complete/parser/?error=invalid_request&amp;error_description=Transform+algorithm+not+supported.%3Cscript%3Ealert(document.cookie)%3C/script%3E&amp;state=cc%0Ass</a></p><p>2. Observe that the injected script &lt;script&gt;alert(document.cookie)&lt;/script&gt; executes in the browser.</p><h3>Impact</h3><ul><li>Theft of session cookies or tokens.</li><li>Malicious script injection to phish users.</li><li>DOM manipulation to redirect or mislead users.</li><li>Could be chained with CSRF/Clickjacking for larger impact.</li></ul><h3>🕒 Timeline</h3><ul><li><strong>March 31, 2025</strong> — Report submitted.</li><li><strong>May 23, 2025</strong> — Triaged by the program.</li><li><strong>June 25, 2025</strong> — Report rewarded with <strong>$366 bounty</strong>.</li></ul><h3>💡 Takeaway</h3><ul><li>Always test <strong>all parameters</strong>, even those that look harmless like error_description.</li><li>Don’t ignore error-handling flows — they’re often a goldmine for XSS.</li><li>Revisiting the same target after some time can expose bugs you missed earlier.</li></ul><p>✅ <strong>Connect and Engage</strong></p><p>💬 What’s your experience with XSS in error-handling parameters?</p><p>Follow me on Twitter: <a href="https://twitter.com/a13h1_">@a13h1_</a></p><p>Keep clapping, commenting, and sharing your thoughts — your support motivates me to share more real bug bounty stories!</p><img alt="" height="1" src="https://medium.com/_/stat?event=post.clientViewed&amp;referrerSource=full_rss&amp;postId=28ca95251ab2" width="1" />
