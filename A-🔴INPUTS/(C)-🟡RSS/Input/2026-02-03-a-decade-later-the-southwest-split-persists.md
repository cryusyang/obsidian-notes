<p style="font-weight: 400;">I wrote the following in February 2016. It was about a negative experience I had with Southwest Airlines. Because of it, I stopped flying them from that point forward.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In hindsight, Southwest lost a loyal customer and gained nothing. On the other hand, I’ve enjoyed great experiences with United, American, and Delta. Their prices and schedules have been comparable, but more importantly, the airplanes (especially the leg room) have been much nicer.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Southwest stuck to their guns and lost but I feel like I won.</p>
<h3>The Southwest Airlines Love Affair Is Over—and It’s Completely Irrational</h3>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Yes, you read that correctly; my 12-year love affair with Southwest Airlines is over, and truthfully, it’s irrational on my part—and Southwest’s too. Perhaps you could say we have irreconcilable differences.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Dan Ariely, author of <em>Predictably Irrational</em> and <em>The Upside of Irrationality</em>, uses studies from behavioral economics to prove we humans are not the rational beings we like to think we are—at least most of the time. One such study that highlights our irrationality.</p>
<h3>The Ultimatum Game</h3>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">In the ultimatum game, Person A is given $10 and can choose to give any amount to a playing partner, Person B, and keep the rest for himself or herself. How much would you give Person B? Is $1 enough? After all, that’s better than nothing. Would you give $4 or $5? That seems like something a fair-minded individual would do. How about $6 or $7? It’s a rare person who would give away more than they would keep.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">There’s a catch to the game: Person B can reject the whole deal—meaning neither side gets to keep any of the money—if they don’t like what’s being offered.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Things change rather dramatically under conditions of perceived fairness. Person A almost always offers $4 or $5 in hopes of being viewed as fair, because that usually leads to agreement. When agreement is reached, everybody wins because both parties leave better off financially than they were before the game started.</p>
<h3>Consider Rationality</h3>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">If you think about it rationally, though, if you were offered $1, that’s better than nothing—and yet the vast majority of people don’t view it that way. If something “fair” isn’t offered, Person B will almost always reject it…even to their own financial detriment.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Consider that for a moment—people willingly subject themselves to “injury” (take no money instead of a few free bucks) just to punish the other person when they feel they’re being treated unfairly. You need look no further than divorce court to see this play out in real life!</p>
<h3>Application for Southwest and Me</h3>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">I fly a good bit but recently learned I had lost my A-List status with Southwest. When I called to find out why, I was told I needed 25 flights in 2015 but only had…24. I thought it reasonable to ask for an exception given my loyalty, increased flights in recent years, and because I had a December business trip I needed to reschedule until this spring. I’d be hard-pressed to think of a handful of times I’ve flown other airlines in the past five years, and when I have, it’s because I traveled with colleagues who had already booked flights.</p>
<h3>Denied Several Times</h3>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">My request was rejected three times at various levels over the phone and one last time after writing a letter. The reason Southwest wouldn’t budge was “to maintain the integrity of the [frequent flier] program.” I was shocked, given the level of customer service I’d experienced with Southwest and my loyalty over the past dozen years. I would have expected that response from many other companies—but not my beloved Southwest!</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Being a persistent guy, I finally emailed CEO Gary Kelly (you’ll never hear “yes” if you don’t ask, right?). At each level, Southwest dug in their heels—and now I have, too—because I’ve made the choice to take at least a couple of flights on other airlines this year. It’s irrational because Southwest flights are almost always on time, their flight attendants are great, and the more I fly, the better my chances of getting my coveted A-List status back. But like the person who feels they were treated unfairly in the ultimatum game, I don’t care!</p>
<h3>A Better Response?</h3>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">For Southwest’s part, they could have made a loyal customer even more loyal by saying, “Mr. Ahearn, seldom do we make an exception like this, but we can see you’re a loyal customer and we appreciate your business, so we’ll do it this one time. Will you still be flying with us every chance you get?” Boom! I would have been happy and would have told them I’d absolutely fly Southwest at every opportunity. And you know what? I would have—because they would have used the principles of scarcity, reciprocity, and consistency on me at the same time.</p>
<h3>The Cost</h3>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Making the exception would have cost them almost nothing other than letting me accrue frequent flier miles 25% faster. That benefit equates to me getting a free ticket 25% sooner, which might cost Southwest about $100—assuming I earn a $400 round-trip ticket a year.</p>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">So, Southwest has made an irrational choice, too, because when I choose to fly other airlines, Southwest will lose more revenue than they would have “given up” if they’d simply accommodated my request.</p>
<h3>The Takeaway</h3>
<p style="font-weight: 400;">Much like the ultimatum game, there comes a point when everyone loses despite their best efforts to persuade the other side. In this instance, I lost—and Southwest lost too. But we’re human, after all, so I’m sure it’s not the last time Southwest will stick to their guns, nor will it be the last time I stick to mine.</p>

## 中文译文

我于2016年2月写下以下文字。内容涉及我与西南航空（Southwest Airlines）一次不愉快的经历。自此之后，我便不再乘坐该航司的航班。

事后看来，西南航空失去了一位忠实客户，却一无所获。相反，我近年来多次享受了联合航空（United）、美国航空（American）和达美航空（Delta）提供的优质服务。它们的价格与航班时刻表与西南航空相差无几；更重要的是，其客机（尤其是腿部空间）明显更舒适宜人。

西南航空坚持己见，最终输了；而我则感觉自己赢了。

西南航空的“爱情长跑”已然终结——而且这完全是非理性的

没错，您没看错：我持续12年之久的西南航空“恋爱史”，已经画上了句号。坦白讲，这在我自己看来是非理性的——对西南航空而言，同样如此。或许我们之间已存在不可调和的分歧。

丹·艾瑞里（Dan Ariely）是《可预测的非理性》（Predictably Irrational）与《非理性的积极面》（The Upside of Irrationality）两书作者，他借助行为经济学领域的多项研究证明：人类远非我们自以为的那样理性——至少在大多数时候并非如此。其中一项研究便清晰揭示了我们的非理性倾向。

最后通牒博弈（The Ultimatum Game）

在此博弈中，A先生获得10美元，可自主决定向搭档B先生分配任意金额，其余归自己所有。那么，你会给B先生多少？1美元够吗？毕竟，有总比没有强。你会给4美元或5美元吗？这似乎才符合一个公平之人的做法。那6美元或7美元呢？极少有人愿意分给对方多于自己保留的数额。

但博弈有个关键规则：若B先生认为所获分配不公平，便可拒绝整笔交易——结果是双方均一无所获。

一旦引入“公平感知”的条件，局面便会急剧变化。A先生几乎总会提出4美元或5美元的分配方案，以期被视作公平——因为这通常能促成共识。一旦达成协议，双方皆大欢喜：相较于博弈开始前，双方财务状况均有所改善。

再思“理性”

然而，若冷静理性地思考，哪怕你只被分得1美元，也毕竟好过一无所获——但绝大多数人并不这样看待。倘若未获“公平”对待，B先生几乎总会拒绝分配方案……哪怕此举会损害自身经济利益。

请稍作思考：人们竟甘愿承受“损失”（宁可一分不得，也不接受几美元的免费馈赠），只为惩罚他们眼中对自己不公的另一方。现实中，只需看看离婚法庭上上演的一幕幕，便足以印证这一点！

此博弈对我与西南航空的启示

我经常乘飞机出行，但最近才得知自己已失去西南航空的A-List会员资格。致电客服询问原因时，被告知：2015年需完成25次飞行，而我仅完成了24次。考虑到自己长期忠诚、近年飞行频次持续增加，且原定于12月的一次商务旅行因故推迟至今年春季，我认为申请一次例外合情合理。过去五年中，我屈指可数地选择过其他航司——那也往往是因为与同事同行，而他们早已预订了其他航司的机票。

屡遭拒绝

我的请求先后三次经由电话不同层级客服人员驳回，最后一次则是在寄出书面信函后仍被拒绝。西南航空给出的理由是：“为维护[常旅客计划]的完整性。”面对这一答复，我震惊不已——这与我多年来体验到的西南航空优质客户服务及其对我十余载忠诚的认可，可谓天壤之别！类似回应，我本可在许多其他公司听到，却万万没想到会出自我深爱的西南航空之口！

身为一个执着的人，我最终直接致信首席执行官加里·凯利（Gary Kelly）（毕竟，不问，永远得不到“是”的答案，对吧？）。西南航空在每一层级都寸步不让——如今，我也同样毫不妥协：我已决定今年至少搭乘几次其他航司的航班。此举看似非理性：西南航空航班几乎从不延误，空乘人员素质极高，而且我飞得越多，就越有机会重获梦寐以求的A-List会员资格。但正如最后通牒博弈中感到受不公平对待的参与者一样，我根本不在乎！

更优的回应方式？

对西南航空而言，一句“阿赫恩先生，此类例外我们极少破例，但我们深知您是一位忠诚客户，非常感谢您的支持，因此我们愿意为您特批一次。您今后是否仍会尽可能选择我们？”——瞬间就能让我心满意足，并欣然承诺：“当然，我一定每次都选西南航空！”事实亦将如此——因为他们同时运用了稀缺性、互惠性与一致性这三大影响力原则。

成本几何？

给予这次例外几乎无需额外成本，仅意味着我可加速25%累积常旅客里程。换言之，我将提前25%获得一张免费机票——按我每年约可赚取一张400美元往返机票计算，西南航空为此付出的成本可能仅为100美元左右。

因此，西南航空自身也做出了非理性决策：当我转投其他航司时，西南航空所损失的收入，将远超当初满足我这一请求所“让渡”的微小成本。

核心启示

正如最后通牒博弈所示，有时无论双方如何努力说服对方，最终仍会陷入双输困局。此次事件中，我输了，西南航空也输了。但毕竟，我们都是凡人——所以我确信，西南航空绝不会是最后一次固执己见，我也绝不会是最后一次坚守立场。
