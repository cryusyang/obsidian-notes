<h3>&#8220;The self, the place where we live, is a place of illusion. Goodness is connected with the attempt to see the unself&#8230; to pierce the veil of selfish consciousness and join the world as it really is.&#8221;</h3>
<hr />
<p><a href="https://www.amazon.com/Sovereignty-Good-Routledge-Great-Minds/dp/0415854733/?tag=braipick-20" target="_blank"><img alt="An Occasion for Unselfing: Iris Murdoch on Imperfection and How to Be More Unselfish" class="cover with-border alignright size-medium" height="488" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/murdoch_sovereigntyofgood.jpg?fit=320%2C488&amp;ssl=1" width="320" /></a></p><p>To recognize that <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2018/11/01/figuring/">there are infinitely many kinds of beautiful lives</a> is to step outside the self, beyond its particular conceptions of beauty &#8212; which includes, of course, moral beauty &#8212; and walking beside it with humble, nonjudgmental curiosity about the myriad other selves afoot on their own paths, propelled by their own ideals of the Good. </p>
<p>Such recognition requires what the great moral philosopher and novelist <strong>Iris Murdoch</strong> (July 15, 1919&ndash;February 8, 1999) termed <em>unselfing</em> &#8212; a difficult, triumphant act for which, Murdoch argues in her 1970 masterpiece <a href="https://www.amazon.com/Sovereignty-Good-Routledge-Great-Minds/dp/0415854733/?tag=braipick-20" target="_blank"><strong><em>The Sovereignty of Good</em></strong></a> (<a href="https://www.worldcat.org/title/sovereignty-of-good/oclc/846846754&#038;referer=brief_results" target="_blank"><em>public library</em></a>), nature and art uniquely train us. </p>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_67043"><a href="https://www.npg.org.uk/collections/search/portrait/mw201586/Iris-Murdoch?LinkID=mp05608&#038;wPage=0&#038;role=sit&#038;rNo=16" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-67043" height="687" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/IrisMurdoch_IdaKar_NationalPortraitGallery.jpg?resize=680%2C687&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text">Dame Iris Murdoch by Ida Kar (National Portrait Gallery)</figcaption></figure>
<p>A century and a half after Emerson observed that <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2015/06/26/emerson-beauty/">&#8220;the question of Beauty takes us out of surfaces, to thinking of the foundations of things,&#8221;</a> Murdoch defines what we commonly call <em>beauty</em> as &#8220;an occasion for ‘unselfing’&#8221; &#8212; an occasion most readily experienced in our communion with nature and our contemplation of art. She writes:</p>
<blockquote><p>Beauty is the convenient and traditional name of something which art and nature share, and which gives a fairly clear sense to the idea of quality of experience and change of consciousness. I am looking out of my window in an anxious and resentful state of mind, oblivious of my surroundings, brooding perhaps on some damage done to my prestige. Then suddenly I observe a hovering kestrel. In a moment everything is altered. The brooding self with its hurt vanity has disappeared. There is nothing now but kestrel. And when I return to thinking of the other matter it seems less important. And of course this is something which we may also do deliberately: give attention to nature in order to clear our minds of selfish care.</p></blockquote>
<figure class="wp-caption alignnone"><a href="https://society6.com/product/peace-from-trees-at-night-by-art-young_print?curator=brainpicker" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-68428" height="1806" src="https://i1.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/artyoung_treesatnight16.jpg" width="1200" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text">Art from <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2019/08/06/trees-at-night-art-young/"><em>Trees at Night</em></a>, 1926. (Available <a href="https://society6.com/product/peace-from-trees-at-night-by-art-young_print?curator=brainpicker" target="_blank">as a print</a>.)</figcaption></figure>
<p>Oliver Sacks would come to echo the sentiment decades later in his observation that meeting nature on its own terms and timescales broadens our perspective by effecting <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2018/07/17/oliver-sacks-beauty-deep-time/">&#8220;a detachment from the timescale, the urgencies, of daily life.&#8221;</a> But this unselfing, Murdoch cautions, cannot arise from a straining of the will, for the will is a clenching of the very self which true beauty deconditions; rather, it comes as a gladsome relaxing of the spirit, of our essential nature, into the shared pulse of existence:</p>
<blockquote><p>A self-directed enjoyment of nature seems to me to be something forced. More naturally, as well as more properly, we take a self-forgetful pleasure in the sheer alien pointless independent existence of animals, birds, stones and trees.</p></blockquote>
<figure class="wp-caption alignnone"><a href="https://society6.com/product/illustration-by-arthur-rackham-for-the-tempest-by-william-shakespeare2375856_print?curator=brainpicker" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-67500" height="970" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/rackham_tempest20.jpg?resize=680%2C970&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text">Art by Arthur Rackham for <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2019/04/03/arthur-rackham-tempest/">a rare 1926 edition</a> of Shakespeare&#8217;s <em>The Tempest</em>. (Available <a href="https://society6.com/product/illustration-by-arthur-rackham-for-the-tempest-by-william-shakespeare2375856_print?curator=brainpicker" target="_blank">as a print</a>.)</figcaption></figure>
<p>This &#8220;self-forgetful pleasure&#8221; calls to mind Jeanette Winterson&#8217;s wonderfully paradoxical notion of <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2014/10/27/jeanette-winterson-art-objects/"><em>active surrender</em></a> as the crucible of our joy in art and the fulcrum for art&#8217;s transformative power over the self. But while there is a distinct difference between how nature and art each effect unselfing, Murdoch argues that what separates great art from the bad and the mediocre is precisely this capacity for stripping down the self rather than inflating the ego &#8212; a notion evocative of Tolstoy&#8217;s insistence that <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/09/09/leo-tolstoy-what-is-art-infectiousness/">&#8220;a real work of art destroys, in the consciousness of the receiver, the separation between himself and the artist.&#8221;</a> Murdoch writes of this dissolution of the self in the presence of great art:</p>
<blockquote><p>The experience of art is more easily degraded than the experience of nature. A great deal of art, perhaps most art, actually is self-consoling fantasy, and even great art cannot guarantee the quality of its consumer&#8217;s consciousness. However, great art exists and is sometimes properly experienced and even a shallow experience of what is great can have its effect. Art, and by &#8220;art&#8221; from now on I mean good art, not fantasy art, affords us a pure delight in the independent existence of what is excellent. Both in its genesis and its enjoyment it is a thing totally opposed to selfish obsession. It invigorates our best faculties and, to use Platonic language, inspires love in the highest part of the soul. It is able to do this partly by virtue of something which it shares with nature: a perfection of form which invites unpossessive contemplation and resists absorption into the selfish dream life of the consciousness.</p></blockquote>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_69014"><a href="https://society6.com/product/every-atom-belonging-to-me-as-good-belongs-to-you-art-by-lia-halloran-for-the-universe-in-verse2373447_print?sku=s6-11773478p4a1v1?curator=brainpicker"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-69014" height="680" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/Lia_1200.jpg?resize=680%2C680&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text">Art by Lia Halloran for <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/the-universe-in-verse/"><em>The Universe in Verse: The Astronomy of Walt Whitman</em></a>. (Available <a href="https://society6.com/product/every-atom-belonging-to-me-as-good-belongs-to-you-art-by-lia-halloran-for-the-universe-in-verse2373447_print?sku=s6-11773478p4a1v1?curator=brainpicker">as a print</a>.)</figcaption></figure>
<p>And yet, Murdoch argues, any real understanding of goodness is necessarily an embrace of imperfection &#8212; something philosopher Martha Nussbaum, in many ways Murdoch&#8217;s only worthy intellectual heir, would argue brilliantly a generation later in her incisive case for <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2015/11/23/martha-nussbaum-upheavals-of-thought-neediness/">the intelligence of emotions</a>. Murdoch writes:</p>
<blockquote><p>The concept of Good&#8230; is a concept which is not easy to understand partly because it has so many false doubles, jumped-up intermediaries invented by human selfishness to make the difficult task of virtue look easier and more attractive: History, God, Lucifer, Ideas of power, freedom, purpose, reward, even judgment are irrelevant. Mystics of all kinds have usually known this and have attempted by extremities of language to portray the nakedness and aloneness of Good, its absolute for-nothingness. One might say that true morality is a sort of unesoteric mysticism, having its source in an austere and unconsoled love of the Good. When Plato wants to explain Good he uses the image of the sun. The moral pilgrim emerges from the cave and begins to see the real world in the light of the sun, and last of all is able to look at the sun itself.</p>
<p>[&#8230;]</p>
<p>We may also speak seriously of ordinary things, people, works of art, as being good, although we are also well aware of their imperfections. Good lives as it were on both sides of the barrier and we can combine the aspiration to complete goodness with a realistic sense of achievement within our limitations.</p></blockquote>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_64206"><a href="https://society6.com/product/i-do-not-know-what-it-is-except-that-it-is-grand-and-that-it-is-happiness_print?sku=s6-8967947p4a1v45?curator=brainpicker" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-64206" height="854" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/margaretcook_leavesofgrass13.jpg?resize=680%2C854&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text">Art by Margaret C. Cook from a <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2018/04/11/leaves-of-grass-margaret-cook/">rare 1913 edition</a> of Whitman&#8217;s <em>Leaves of Grass</em>. (Available <a href="https://society6.com/product/i-do-not-know-what-it-is-except-that-it-is-grand-and-that-it-is-happiness_print?sku=s6-8967947p4a1v45?curator=brainpicker" target="_blank">as a print</a>.)</figcaption></figure>
<p>With an eye to the legacy of the Romantics, who married nature and art in <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2018/10/23/mary-shelley-lake-geneva/">their model of happiness and transcendence</a>, Murdoch returns to the notion of unselfing and the beautiful tessellation of possibility and limitation that defines our nature:</p>
<blockquote><p>The self, the place where we live, is a place of illusion. Goodness is connected with the attempt to see the unself, to see and to respond to the real world in the light of a virtuous consciousness. This is the non-metaphysical meaning of the idea of transcendence to which philosophers have so constantly resorted in their explanations of goodness. &#8220;Good is a transcendent reality&#8221; means that virtue is the attempt to pierce the veil of selfish consciousness and join the world as it really is. It is an empirical fact about human nature that this attempt cannot be entirely successful.</p></blockquote>
<p><a href="https://www.amazon.com/Sovereignty-Good-Routledge-Great-Minds/dp/0415854733/?tag=braipick-20" target="_blank"><strong><em>The Sovereignty of Good</em></strong></a> is an immensely insightful read in its entirety. Complement this particular fragment with Robinson Jeffers on <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2019/06/03/robinson-jeffers-sister-mary-james-power/">nature and moral beauty</a> and Oliver Sacks on <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2019/05/27/oliver-sacks-gardens/">the healing power of gardens</a>, then revisit Murdoch on <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2019/02/12/iris-murdoch-salvation-by-words/">art as a force of resistance to tyranny</a>, <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2018/07/18/iris-murdoch-existentialists-mystics-philosophy-literature-art/">the key to great storytelling</a>, and her uncommonly beautiful <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2016/01/26/iris-murdoch-love-letters-brigid-brophy/">love letters</a>.</p>

## 中文译文

“自我”——我们栖居之所——乃幻象之域。善，与努力看见“非我”紧密相连：刺穿自私意识的面纱，从而与真实世界相联结。

认识到世间存在着无穷多种美好的生命形态，便意味着我们已迈出自我之域，超越了自我对美（当然也包括道德之美）的特定理解，并以谦逊、不加评判的好奇心，与无数正各自踏上人生旅途、受其自身“至善”理想所驱动的其他自我并肩而行。

这种认知，需要伟大的道德哲学家兼小说家艾里斯·默多克（Iris Murdoch，1919年7月15日—1999年2月8日）所称的“去自我化”（unselfing）——一种艰难却辉煌的精神实践。默多克在其1970年的杰作《善的主权》（The Sovereignty of Good，公共馆藏版）中指出，唯有自然与艺术，能独特地训练我们完成这一实践。

艾里斯·默多克女爵士肖像，伊达·卡尔（Ida Kar）摄（英国国家肖像馆）

在爱默生（Emerson）于一个半世纪前写下“美之问题将我们引离表象，导向对万物根基的思索”之后，默多克将我们通常所称的“美”界定为“一次‘去自我化’的契机”——而这种契机，最易在我们与自然的交融及对艺术的沉思中得以体验。她写道：

美，是艺术与自然所共有的某种东西的传统而便利的称谓；它使“经验品质”与“意识转变”的概念获得相当清晰的意义。我正焦虑而愤懑地望向窗外，对周遭浑然不觉，或许正沉溺于某次伤及我声望的打击之中。忽然间，我注意到一只悬停的红隼。顷刻之间，一切已然改变：那个因虚荣受挫而郁郁沉思的自我消失了，此刻唯余红隼。当我再度想起先前那桩事时，它已显得不那么重要了。当然，我们亦可主动为之：专注凝望自然，以涤除心中自私的挂虑。

数十年后，奥利弗·萨克斯（Oliver Sacks）曾呼应此观点，指出：唯有依循自然自身的节律与尺度去与其相遇，方能拓宽我们的视野，从而“脱离日常生活的时限与紧迫感”。然而默多克提醒道，这种“去自我化”绝非意志强行用力的结果，因为意志本身恰恰是那种被真正之美所消解的“自我”的紧绷状态；相反，它源于精神——我们本真天性——欣然松弛下来，融入存在那共通的脉动之中：

在我看来，一种以自我为中心地欣赏自然，实属强求之举。更自然、也更恰当的方式，是我们忘却自我，在动物、飞鸟、石头与树木那全然异质、看似无目的、独立自足的存在中，获得一种忘我的欢愉。

亚瑟·拉克姆（Arthur Rackham）为1926年罕见版莎士比亚《暴风雨》所作插画。（可购印刷品。）

这种“忘我的欢愉”，令人联想到珍妮特·温特森（Jeanette Winterson）所提出的精彩悖论式概念——“主动的臣服”（active surrender）：它正是我们从艺术中获得喜悦的熔炉，亦是艺术改造自我的支点。然而，默多克指出，自然与艺术各自实现“去自我化”的方式虽有显著差异，但区分伟大艺术与平庸或拙劣艺术的关键，恰恰正在于前者剥离自我的能力，而非膨胀自我——这一观念令人想起托尔斯泰（Tolstoy）的坚定主张：“一件真正的艺术品，会在接受者意识中摧毁他与艺术家之间的隔阂。”默多克如此描述人在面对伟大艺术时自我的消融：

艺术体验比自然体验更易被贬损。大量艺术——或许绝大多数艺术——本质上不过是自我抚慰的幻想；甚至伟大艺术，亦无法确保其接受者意识的质量。然而，伟大艺术确乎存在，且有时确被恰当地体验；即便对伟大之作仅作浅层体验，亦能产生影响。此处所谓“艺术”，此后皆指优秀艺术，而非幻想艺术——它赋予我们一种纯粹的欢愉，令我们欣然赞叹那卓越之物独立自存的庄严。无论其诞生过程抑或欣赏过程，它都彻底背离自私的执迷。它激荡我们最优异的禀赋；若借用柏拉图式的语言，它激发灵魂最高部分的爱。它之所以能做到这一点，部分正因其与自然共享的特质：一种形式的完满，召唤着不占有式的静观，抗拒被吸纳进意识那自私的梦幻生活之中。

然而默多克强调，任何对“善”的真切理解，必然是对不完美的拥抱——这正是哲学家玛莎·努斯鲍姆（Martha Nussbaum）日后一代人所雄辩论证的洞见：她有力地阐明了情感的智性。努斯鲍姆在诸多方面堪称默多克唯一当之无愧的思想继承者。默多克写道：

“善”的概念……不易理解，部分原因在于它拥有太多虚假的替身——那些由人类自私所炮制出的、浮夸的中介物，旨在使德性这一艰难任务显得更轻松、更具吸引力：历史、上帝、路西法、权力、自由、目的、报偿乃至审判等观念，皆与此无关。各类神秘主义者通常深谙此理，遂以极致的语言尝试描绘“善”的赤裸与孤绝，及其绝对的“无所为而为”。我们或可言：真正的道德，是一种非秘传的神秘主义，其源泉乃是对“善”一种严苛而无慰藉的挚爱。当柏拉图欲阐释“善”时，他借用了太阳的意象：道德朝圣者走出洞穴，在阳光照耀下开始看见真实世界，最终得以直视太阳本身。……我们亦可严肃地称寻常事物、普通人、艺术作品为“善”，尽管我们对其缺陷亦了然于心。“善”仿佛横跨藩篱两侧而存在，我们既能怀抱对至善的向往，亦能以清醒的现实感，在自身局限之内体认所达成的成就。

默多克目光投向浪漫主义者——他们将自然与艺术融为一体，构建起幸福与超越的典范——继而重返“去自我化”这一观念，并指出：定义我们本性的，正是可能性与局限性之间那精妙的镶嵌结构：

“自我”——我们栖居之所——乃幻象之域。善，与努力看见“非我”紧密相连：以德性意识之光，去看见并回应真实世界。这便是哲学家们在阐释“善”时屡屡诉诸的“超越”观念之非形而上学内涵。“善乃超越性实在”意即：德性即努力刺穿自私意识的面纱，从而与真实世界相联结。而人类本性的一个经验事实是：这一努力永远无法臻于完全成功。
