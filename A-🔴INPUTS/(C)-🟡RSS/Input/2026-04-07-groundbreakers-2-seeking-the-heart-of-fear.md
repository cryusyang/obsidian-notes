									<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Six years ago, I attended a week-long course in Northern California called Groundbreakers.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Facilitated by Joe Hudson and his wife, Tara, it was one of the most impactful experiences of my life. The experience I had there reshaped my relationship with the “voice in my head,” that is, with my own inner monologue. It helped me tap into my anger, which I’d cut off as a child. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I remember exactly the intention I set on the first day of GB1, as we now call it. The 12 of us sat in a circle, taking turns saying what we wanted to get out of the week. I’d expected to say something strategic or impressive-sounding, but as my turn came, my heart was beating furiously. With all eyes on me, I found myself instead almost choking out the words “I want to let all the love I have in my heart out into the world.” </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I can remember the feeling behind those words as well: that I knew I had so much to contribute. So much to give. And I wanted to, more than anything, but had no idea how. There seemed to be an immense blockade stopping me from the simple act of giving. I could feel all the love stored up in my heart like a dam about to break, and all I wanted was to let it.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">And reader, from the vantage point of today, I can say that I absolutely fulfilled that intention in the years since. I can remember walking in Golden Gate Park a couple days after Groundbreakers, my emotions newly unblocked and flowing through me like a torrent. And then I stopped in my tracks and realized that the flow of emotions inside me was indistinguishable from the flow of creativity. That they were one and the same.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">From that day forward, I undertook a series of creative projects that completely transformed my life. I wrote several books, which have now sold over 500,000 copies. I rebooted my cohort-based course and taught over 5,000 students. I built a multimillion-dollar business and a team that has served tens of thousands all over the world. It was all an expression of my profound love for the world and its people.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">But as the years passed, I started to sense there was still something missing. A new part of myself that wanted to emerge, that wanted to speak. I couldn’t have said what it was. But when the invite arrived to join Groundbreakers Part II, I knew I had to sign up. Six years after the first leg of the journey, it was time to embark on the second.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">(If you want to read about my experience at the first Groundbreakers, </span><a href="https://fortelabs.com/blog/groundbreakers/" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><span style="font-weight: 400;">you can do so here</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">.)</span></p><h2><span style="font-weight: 400;">Part Two</span></h2><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I flew from our home near Mexico City to Northern California, and took an Uber from Sonoma County Airport to the venue, a couple hours away in Lake County to the north. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It was a beautiful rural retreat center nestled in an idyllic valley, the former site of a kids’ summer camp. Simple cabins dotted the grounds. It was March, and the early spring sunshine kept the days warm while the nights hovered near freezing.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">We gathered in the largest meeting hall on the first day. There were 36 people attending, divided into 3 groups of 12, plus a dozen facilitators, group leaders, and assistants. There were many familiar faces, including people who’d found out about the </span><a href="https://www.artofaccomplishment.com/" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Art of Accomplishment</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> (the company that delivers these programs, known as AOA) through me or my writing. Others I’d taken courses alongside previously.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">We moved into our groups and settled into a tight circle on chairs and couches to state our intentions. I only knew one other person in the group, a fellow internet creator. I had prepared nothing beforehand, but as we went around the circle and my turn came, I once again found an intention forming on my lips.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Reliving the last six years in fast forward, I suddenly saw that in my desire to express my love for others, as important as that had been, something else was lost in the outward rush: </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">receiving</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> the love of others. I could see my whole life in miniature: giving, giving, giving, and yet when it came time to receive, I saw myself blocking, avoiding, and withdrawing from the very love that I so desperately wanted.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I stated my intention for the week: “To receive all the love.” As I did so, I immediately sensed my rational mind recoil. It seemed selfish, self-indulgent. My brain sought a qualification or a scope to this goal – “What do you mean ALL the love?!” </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">And in the quiet of my mind, I also had a secondary intention: “To go through the experience without resisting it.” That is, to have it be fun. After undergoing </span><a href="https://fortelabs.com/blog/a-quest-for-self-knowledge-from-self-help-to-somatic-healing-part-i-opening-my-mind/" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><span style="font-weight: 400;">so many of these kinds of personal development experiences</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> over the last 15 years with my teeth gritted, fighting the process every step of the way, I was tired of it. I wanted it to be enjoyable and exhilarating instead.</span></p><h2><span style="font-weight: 400;">Limiting beliefs</span></h2><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The official theme of GB2 (as we called it) was “Limiting beliefs.” </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Our preparation involved listening to two episodes of the AOA podcast: </span><a href="https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/limiting-beliefs-the-hidden-rails-that-guide-our-lives/id1540650504?i=1000555971058" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Limiting Beliefs: The Hidden Rails That Guide Our Lives</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, and </span><a href="https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/how-to-see-through-limiting-beliefs/id1540650504?i=1000584283150" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><span style="font-weight: 400;">How to See Through Limiting Beliefs</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">. I share them here because they are excellent introductions to the topic if you want to dive deeper.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">We were to list out as many limiting beliefs as we could find in ourselves in our journal. What I found curious as I started doing so was the particular way they were framed. I’d always thought of limiting beliefs in terms of logical fallacies, as artifacts of our cognitive biases and incomplete knowledge.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The definition that Joe introduced was quite different:</span></p><ul><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Any thought that causes constriction in the body </span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Any source of tension in your life</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Any habits you don’t want in your life</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Tension in your relationships</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Places where you or your business aren’t performing with ease</span></li></ul><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In other words, limiting beliefs were defined mainly in terms of the </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">tension</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> they caused in our body, our relationships, or our work. Using that much broader definition, they started pouring out of me:</span></p><ul><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;You can either be smart, or be happy&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;I have to be a certain way to be liked&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;I&#8217;ll never be able to fully feel the depth of my emotions&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;I&#8217;ve peaked. The years to come won&#8217;t be as good as the ones that have passed&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;I have to keep doing self-development to be happy, free, joyful&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;My family will never overcome their limiting beliefs&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;I have to discipline my son to keep him in check&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;I&#8217;m destined to become my father&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;Creative work and financial success are in tension&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;I&#8217;m not a good father or husband&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;I can only afford to maintain so many relationships at any given time&#8221;</span></li></ul><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">As the first day continued, we were taught the approach to limiting beliefs that we’d be exploring and practicing throughout the rest of the week. The first lesson was that overcoming limiting beliefs is not primarily a rational process. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">You can 100% know and believe that a given belief – like “Money is scarce” – is incorrect. You can think up all the reasons it isn’t true, identify situations that contradict it, and even come up with an alternative belief you want to replace it with. But none of that makes a difference. The belief seems to stick like glue. At most, logical deconstruction just opens up a crack in the doorway.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The reason limiting beliefs stick even when we know they’re not true is that they are continuously fed by an underlying emotional cycle. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">For example, one of the most pervasive beliefs I could see in myself was encapsulated in the phrase “You should know better.” I found myself thinking it toward my team when they flubbed a decision. Or toward my kids when they spilled milk across the dining room table. And most of all, I said it constantly to myself when I made the wrong choice.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The start of any emotional cycle is an emotion that you don’t want to feel. In this case, for me, it was helplessness – the feeling of not being able to do anything in the face of mistakes or missteps, my own or others’. In order to not feel helpless, I turned to judgment and blame instead, which are so much easier to be with. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">That judgment would quickly mutate into guilt, as I felt bad for casting judgment so harshly (“I should be more patient, loving, compassionate”). That guilt then turned into compassion (“They&#8217;ll be damaged by this if I don&#8217;t help them”). But since it was a forced compassion, fueled by guilt, it would soon turn into resentment (“This is indulging them. I have to hold my boundaries”). That resentment led easily to judgment again, and the cycle repeated.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">This is what Joe calls the </span><a href="https://www.artofaccomplishment.com/podcast/the-golden-algorithm-decisions-series-3" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Golden Algorithm</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">: that for any emotion you resist feeling, you’ll tend to do all sorts of things that have the effect of recreating that very emotion again and again. It is your body’s repeated attempt to find homeostasis; to feel what it hasn’t yet been able to feel.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">We were asked to begin identifying our emotional cycles, mapping them out in circular diagrams in our notebooks. Seeing them was the first step to seeing through them. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I won’t describe the exercises and practices in detail, to not diminish their power if you ever have the chance to try them. But there were a wide variety of them, including breathwork, eyegazing, coaching interactions, small group experiences, emotional releases, pair work, cuddling, walks in nature, music and poetry, physical movements, and reenactments – all meant to create spaciousness and fluidity in our bodies, as an antidote to the tightness and constriction that limiting beliefs tend to create. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It wasn’t about letting go of &#8220;incorrect&#8221; beliefs and adopting the &#8220;correct&#8221; ones in their place. It was about seeing that every belief is a limited view of reality, and causes a certain sensation in the body we may no longer want. A limiting belief ultimately limits our ability to be truly ourselves.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">We went deeper, exploring any thought or idea that caused tightness in the body, provoked an emotion, or blocked our access to joy. I found a second layer of beliefs, which were all the more threatening in that they seemed to me to be quite true:</span></p><ul><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;I can&#8217;t control what happens to those I love&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;There is no underlying order or purpose to the universe&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;I don&#8217;t fit in this world&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;Most people are not very capable of solving their problems&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;I have to discharge my responsibilities because of the privileges I&#8217;ve been given&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;I&#8217;m destined for something greater&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;Resources are always scarce. There&#8217;s only so much to go around&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;Love is an exchange or transaction&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;I always have to prove my value&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;Relationships are constantly decaying&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;Vulnerability is painful&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;I have to go to extremes to find personal growth experiences that work&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;I have to get it right&#8221;</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">&#8220;Life is short and there&#8217;s no time to waste&#8221;</span></li></ul><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I was stunned to see the Golden Algorithm at play in the most minute situations. I’d feel anxiety or insecurity in a social situation, such as at a house party. Thoughts would start to arise, such as “I&#8217;m weird” and “I don&#8217;t belong here.” Such thoughts would provoke fear (“I&#8217;ll be alone and rejected” or “I won’t be able to connect with anyone here”). That fear would lead to overwhelm and withdrawal (“I&#8217;m pretending to be someone I&#8217;m not” or “I&#8217;m too in my head”). At this point, I’m dissociating, not really there, which invariably leads back to anxiety and insecurity as I’m not listening, not tracking the conversation, and not responding to social cues. The loop completes and restarts.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Fortunately, the Golden Algorithm is not permanent. The way out is to </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">look forward</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> to the emotion you’ve been avoiding – to welcome it and feel grateful for what it’s trying to teach you – rather than avoiding it. The purpose of the cycle is to end the cycle, and you end it by fully feeling the emotion it keeps trying to resurrect. It’s so simple. Yet not necessarily easy.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The core technique we practiced throughout the week was a set of two questionnaires, which together included 16 questions. We’d walk through them in pairs, sitting face to face. The other person’s witnessing like a mirror into one’s own soul.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">We kept going deeper, and I encountered yet another layer of limiting beliefs, feeling to me like certainties:</span></p><ul><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">“I won&#8217;t be able to love Lauren with the depth she deserves”</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">“My children will be killed”</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">“I&#8217;ll never learn to fully love myself”</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">“I don&#8217;t truly matter to people”</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">“If I drop the mask I won&#8217;t be loved”</span></li><li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">“I&#8217;ll feel regret on my deathbed for not loving the people in my life enough”</span></li></ul><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Each one of these hit me like a brick wall when said aloud. These were some of the core fears of my existence, like solid pillars of cold, hard reality that I could never make a dent in, much less change. I felt the grief in them, howled in rage or heartbreak or terror. Tears streamed down my face in endless abundance, piles of used tissues piling up on either side of my chair.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Working through this set of limiting beliefs was like running an intricate maze. Sometimes the way through to the next passage looked like a solid wall at first, and then the tiniest crack would appear, a sliver of light in the oppressive darkness.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I knew that one of my deepest fears was that “My children will be killed.” Living in Mexico, with the lack of traffic laws or safety rules, this fear was my constant companion. My imagination invented dozens of ways it could happen, all the more terrifying in that they were mundane, things that could happen anytime.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Working through it with a partner, at first, it seemed 100% true that my children will one day die. But then I noticed the fear was slightly different: that they would be killed </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">on my watch</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">. That it would be my fault, my responsibility. That was the first crack in the logic of fear. An opening.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Responding to one of the questions, I suddenly saw the function of this belief: to protect me from future loss and heartbreak. My brain was trying to prepare me in advance, to shield me from a level of grief it was certain would destroy me. I thanked the belief for that service.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">But the effect on the present, I started to see as we worked through the other questions, was in many ways the opposite of the intended one. The fear kept me constantly hypervigilant and overly cautious, even in situations that presented no danger. We’d be strolling in the plaza in the center of town, and all I could think about was where the next danger was coming from. And I could see that that fear hadn’t, in fact, protected me. A few months before, my son had been bitten by a dog in the street, just a few feet in front of me. There was nothing I could do, despite all my vigilance.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I turned my attention to how this belief made me feel now. And all at once, I felt the devastation. The unbearable loss. The utter despair, the blackness. Annihilation. Hopelessness. All the emotions I would feel at my child’s death were already there, all the time, simmering just beneath the surface of my awareness. It’s as if I’m constantly reliving the reality of my children&#8217; s deaths, an event which hasn’t yet occurred and may never occur in my lifetime.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">And there is a real cost: I’m disconnected from them, withdrawn from them, treating them harshly when they break the rules or take even one step toward the street. I’m up for hours at night, worrying, having nightmares, and so tired of it all. I suddenly saw that in my attempt to keep my kids safe, I’d neglected to do things that would be far more effective: teaching them situational awareness instead of following rigid rules; teaching them to trust their intuition even when it wasn’t clear what the danger was; teaching them to trust me, rather than fear my wrath.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In my attempt to protect them from the world, I’d taught them to fear the world. Which paradoxically made the world more dangerous, thus confirming the narrative in the first place. Another emotional cycle, another self-reinforcing loop.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I saw that I learned this belief system from my father, who always told us that he had to discipline us strictly to keep us safe. I can see now how afraid he was for us, and that this was his way of protecting us. I share his fear, and thus feel the same compulsion to enforce strict compliance. Thus repeating the cycle for another generation. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I felt the grief, the sadness, the regret, the disappointment wash over me. That I’d been perpetuating the whole emotional cycle in myself, and in my young family, and across the generations. All those cycles were connected, but all it took was one link to break in the chain to start to unwind it.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">And it goes even deeper! A while later, working through a related belief, I saw that the same attitude of protectiveness my parents had shown toward me, I was also showing toward them. Our family went through a series of crises over a decade ago, and the belief I adopted out of that was that I had to be the “good, reliable, easy one” to protect my parents. A model child who raised no issues.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">That manifested in my adulthood as withholding myself from them: my fears, my doubts, my struggles, my pain. I didn’t share what was really going on with me, which was all the easier living abroad in various places. I pretended like everything was going swimmingly all the time, which meant I was treating my parents as if they were small and weak and in need of protection. As if I was the parent. When in fact, they made me – I didn’t make them. Everything I know and am, they taught me. Everything I have, they gave me. They don’t need my caretaking; they just want my love.</span></p><p><div class="convertkit-form wp-block-convertkit-form"></div><!-- notionvc: edde0f97-d7b4-4d27-b5dc-3041317e2236 --></p><h2><span style="font-weight: 400;">Tapping into fear</span></h2><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">As you can probably tell by now, most of my limiting beliefs were circling around a single, central emotion that I’d been unwilling to feel for a long time: fear.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Going back and rereading </span><a href="https://fortelabs.com/blog/groundbreakers/" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><span style="font-weight: 400;">my account of Groundbreakers Part I</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, I’m astounded to notice that fear was written all over it. I used words like “fear,” “afraid,” and “scared” 18 times in that story! Yet if you had asked me the day before GB2 started whether fear was something I struggled with, I would have said no. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In fact, the evidence of my life indicated a pronounced lack of fear. I am a longtime entrepreneur. I’ve lived in some of the most dangerous or unstable places in the world – the favelas of Rio de Janeiro, northern Colombia near FARC territory, Eastern Ukraine. I wonder in fact if all these escapades were my attempt to try and feel fear by whatever means necessary.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The more I looked, the more I saw fear infusing every part of my life: my marriage, my parenting, my business decisions, my relationship with my team, everyday social interactions. But I was still unsure exactly how to feel it in my body.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">On the fifth day, many of us found ourselves revisiting an early trauma experience –  a time when we were scared or in need and didn’t get the love and care we needed, thus starting to form our beliefs about the world. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">During the instructions before we began, I raised my hand and asked, “But what if we don’t feel fear?” I couldn’t remember any particular sensation in my body associated with fear. It felt inaccessible to me, like a room I’d walled off with concrete blocks and seamless drywall, so no trace of it remained. Joe told me that by the time I was asking that question, I was already on the edge of the precipice, and to hold on.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A group of facilitators demonstrated what the exercise would look like. I won’t describe it in any detail, both to not ruin the effect if you ever undertake it, and because the experience required a very specific and safe environment to unfold in a healthy way.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">As it unfolded, I realized that I was already feeling the sensations of fear: my hands were cold and clammy, with a sheen of sweat, and shaking a little bit; my jaw and neck felt tense, locked in place; I felt a cold, heavy weight in the pit of my stomach. I was surprised to realize that these were all the classic signs of fear. Somehow I’d expected that MY fear would express itself in a more special, unique way.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">When my turn came, I really didn’t know if I could do it. At first, it felt a little like acting – writhing on the floor, hiding behind my hands, whimpering like a small child.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">But then, with my group conveying an unbroken flood of unconditional love, acceptance, and attuned care toward me, it became real. I was transported back to the scene of being spanked by my father. I could see everything in perfect detail: the bedspread on my parents’ bed, the mirrored closet doors, the texture of the carpet as I was placed upside down on his knees.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I felt an irresistible urge to cower and tremble. I buried my head into the floor, grinding my face into the carpet to try and get away. I covered my head with my arms and my face with my hands and screamed. I felt so small and scared, so out of control and in danger. I didn’t recognize most of the sounds I was creating – keening and wailing and guttural bellows. I finally let out the fear that that little boy had been unable to, and completed the movements he had left unfinished over 30 years before.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">My group surrounded me, and caressed and comforted me, saying the words I’d needed to hear back then. The raw physical sensations eventually gave way to words, and the language I was using belonged to my child self: “I didn’t do it” I cried again and again and again. “I didn’t do anything…”. “It’s not my fault…” “It’s not fair…”. I could feel myself protesting my innocence, protesting the way I was being treated.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">And then, finally, my anger and my fear turned to grief, and I felt the flood of relief. I heard myself say, “You took my father from me.” And I saw that the true pain hadn’t been the spanking itself. That was over and done in seconds. The deeper pain was the separation it had created from him, the man I adored and looked up to and modeled myself after. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">And I remember exactly how that separation had happened. Again, it wasn’t an automatic consequence of being spanked. It was a decision I’d made soon afterward. I remember it vividly. I had walked out of my parents’ bedroom and crossed the hall to my own room, which is typically where I went to recover. This was one of the last times I was spanked, when I was getting too old for it, perhaps 7 or 8 or 9 years old.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">As I crossed the hallway, I remember trying to think of some way I could get back at my father, to hurt him as he’d hurt me. The only thing I could come up with was to cut him off from my love forever. It was the only weapon I had at my disposal. And that’s what I did. I created a wall in my heart that he would never be allowed to cross. An inner sanctum that he would never know or touch. And there I would protect myself. There I would be safe.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I can even remember at the time, a small voice in my mind saying, “You’ll pay for this someday.” I’m amazed that I had the self-awareness, even at the time, of what I was doing to myself, and what it would cost me.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">From the vantage point of today, I can see what happened: by cutting off my dad from my love, I was also cutting myself off from his love. I was also cutting off the love of my mother, since she hadn’t protected me. And in a way, that also made it difficult to give or receive love from anyone else in my life. Love was dangerous; it was safer to avoid it. </span></p><h2><span style="font-weight: 400;">Opening my heart to my father and God</span></h2><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A day or two later, in the final exercise of the week, we were again paired up and asked to take turns responding to a set of questions designed to integrate our experience.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The final question of that exercise, the last step of hundreds of questions we’d answered that week, was odd: What is loving you?</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">At first, I drew a complete blank. I could answer “WHO was loving me” with ready, stock answers. But “WHAT was loving me?” It felt like a non-sequitur.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">And then, the answer hit me: God was loving me. It arrived like a revelation, like a window being opened onto a sunlit day. The sensation was that of a channel opening inside me, one that was always filled and always available the moment I turned the valve. It felt joyous, and funny, like having the same thought at the same time as a dear friend.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I realized that by cutting myself off from my parents’ love, I’d also cut myself off from God’s love. I had a profound faith when I was young. I’d played in the church band, as had my mother before me. As a family, we were at the church several times a week, participating in everything. For years, I’d wanted to become a missionary, and even started down that path, going on mission trips as a teenager to Belize, France, and Ethiopia to spread the gospel.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">That had all come tumbling down in my early 20s, when </span><a href="https://fortelabs.com/blog/why-im-not-a-christian-a-testimony-of-losing-faith/" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><span style="font-weight: 400;">I left my religious faith</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> and went to the opposite extreme, branding myself an atheist. That shift had kicked off a search for other sources of meaning, from immersing myself in foreign cultures to studying philosophy and science, to seeking out personal development as a way to access transcendence.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I think I threw the baby out with the bathwater, coming to deny anything I couldn’t see, touch, or measure. A pall of nihilism descended, which I’d sought to alleviate any way I knew how: through altruism, through rationality, through psychedelics, through starting a family. Those had all worked to different extents, but there was always something missing – a force vast enough to encompass me.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">One of the women in my group sent me this quote by Henri Nouwen the next day, which articulates the vastness I was always seeking:</span></p><p><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">“It is the mystery that the heart, which is the center of our being, is transformed by God into his own heart, a heart large enough to embrace the entire universe. Through prayer we can carry in our heart all human pain and sorrow, all conflicts and agonies, all torture and war, all hunger, loneliness, and misery, not because of some great psychological or emotional capacity, but because God’s heart has become one with ours.”</span></i></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Walking the hills above the retreat center the day after the program ended, I could sense God’s love had returned. I could feel that it had always been there. I felt the relief of not having to spend so much energy keeping it at bay. It really takes a tremendous amount of effort.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I don’t know exactly what I believe about God now. I’m not trying to reduce it/she/he to a set of tenets, or a dogma. I doubt I will return to any recognizable form of my former Christian faith. I want to relearn who and what God is from first principles, from direct experience. I’m open to anything.</span></p><h2><span style="font-weight: 400;">Learning how to receive</span></h2><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">As the program came to a close, I returned to the intention I’d started with – to receive all the love – and saw it from a completely different perspective. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">My personal growth journey had begun with my father. In GB1, I had worked through the anger of what he had done to me. This time, I also felt anger toward him, but for a different reason – for not receiving my love.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I saw that it was the same with my own children, Caio and Delia, ages 3 and 5. I&#8217;d often thought of my time with my kids as a kind of duty, as if it was an item on my to-do list. I understood clearly how much they needed my love, but not how much I needed theirs. They have a deep need to learn how to love me. That&#8217;s how they learn to receive love from others in turn, by my example.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">So much of what I’d been frustrated with from them – the endless bedtime routines, the incessant desire to play, the insistence on being with me whatever I was doing – was an expression of their determination to love me. They stopped at nothing to love me, even in the face of “boundaries” and rules I put in place to stop them. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I spoke with Joe about this, and he noted that receiving the love of others is how they support you. Otherwise, you become a “hungry ghost,” always seeking love but never finding it, even when it’s right there in front of you for the taking. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I saw this in play with my team at work: that they were constantly offering to support me in different ways, and I often refused it. I saw it with my followers, customers, and students – that I was so intent on giving, giving, giving to them, that I missed how much they wanted to contribute to me in return. I saw how much effort it took to evade and deflect that tidal wave of love and appreciation over the years, out of nothing but simple fear.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I was left with a new question: How might learning to receive love from my team and my community transform my work? What if the bottleneck to everything I wanted to create and achieve and experience in my life was…learning to receive love?!</span></p><h2><span style="font-weight: 400;">The aftermath</span></h2><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The days following GB2 were ethereal, other-worldly.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I was incredibly sensitive to everything around me, including other people’s “energy,” for lack of a better term. I walked into a massage parlor near Sonoma Airport on my way out of town, and instantly felt radiating waves of anxiety ricocheting throughout the place. The staff was on edge, cowering in a corner.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The owner of the place, a middle-aged Asian woman, had done everything she could to make the place seem calm and relaxing on the surface: little Buddha statues, ferns and plants, gurgling water fountains, peaceful music. But it was all totally at odds with the emotions she was projecting: worry and fear. It was so unnerving that I had to walk right out. It made me wonder how many small business owners struggle, not because they lack the right strategy or service, but because their unfelt emotions are undermining everything they’re trying to build.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">On two separate Uber rides the day after, we got into talking about limiting beliefs and how they are locked in place by emotional cycles. I normally never talk to Uber drivers. But in those days, I found myself magnetically drawn to everyone I encountered, and they seemed magnetically drawn to me as well. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I sat down at a sushi restaurant in Healdsburg for a solo dinner one night, and the couple next to me struck up a conversation. In most everyday social interactions, I’ve always felt an undercurrent of fear for as long as I can remember. I would always feel it in the muscles around my eyes, always slightly wide, expecting rejection or disinterest, and thus inviting it. I could sense my eyes were much more relaxed now, and the conversation flowed so easily I’d forgotten what that was like. Their parting comment to me, two strangers passing in the night, was &#8220;We love you already.&#8221; Unimaginable.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It’s been over a month now, and I still feel so much more interested and curious about people. I can see their inherent goodness so easily. I normally rarely talk to strangers, and keep all talking to a minimum generally, a legacy of my </span><a href="https://fortelabs.com/blog/unspeakable-pain-a-personal-journey-through-psychosomatic-illness/" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><span style="font-weight: 400;">voice condition</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> when I had to conserve every bit of vocal energy. Yet I’ve found that socializing has become so much more enjoyable. I can flow easily in and out of conversation. My words are much more fluid, and I&#8217;m much quicker both to make jokes and to laugh at others’. I’m wittier, more playful, because I&#8217;m not preprocessing everything I&#8217;m going to say in advance, just expressing what I want to say in the moment.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Being able to connect with anyone I meet has been a more radical liberation than I could have imagined possible. I had no idea how small that limiting belief – that I can’t connect with people freely – was keeping me.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">And lastly, I see the world differently. Poetry was a big part of GB2, and after years of having zero interest in any kind of poetry, because I didn’t see the point of it, I’m now obsessed with it. Poetry is the very heart of life, the essence of it. I find myself so taken with the beauty of clouds, flowers, mountain ranges, and flowing water. I just can’t believe how much beauty and mystery and meaning there is all around me, all the time, bursting forth with gifts of joy and love.</span></p><h2><span style="font-weight: 400;">The roots of dissociation</span></h2><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A recurring theme for me has been dissociation, and I continued exploring it in the weeks following GB2. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I remembered that as a kid, I had a tremendous imagination. I would look off into the distance, unfocus my eyes, and imagine whole worlds into being. I had one particular storyline I would return to every time I had a free moment, in which I rewrote the Star Wars story according to what I thought was a superior plotline (mainly involving a lot more space battles and less talking). During the long flights we’d take to Brazil throughout my childhood, I could just stare at the seatback in front of me for hours at a time, entertaining myself with these imagined worlds.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I could see that starting around my teenage years, I’d established a habit of controlling that stream of thoughts; of making it “practical.” If I was thinking about one thing, I&#8217;d try to think of something else that was better. If I was unfocused, I&#8217;d try to force myself to focus on something useful, like reading a book. If I was daydreaming, I made it wrong and told myself to make progress on something productive instead. It was a self-directed form of coercion inside the most private of places – the interior of my own mind. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Another way that manifested was constantly sorting things into black-and-white, binary categories: good versus bad, right versus wrong, better versus worse. Like a moralizing pinball machine sending every incoming piece of data into these two impoverished categories, as if no other options were possible.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">All of this had, of course, been supercharged by my chosen career – productivity and personal effectiveness. That mindset of endless self-optimization colonized every corner of my mind. Every second had to be spent making myself better. Every thought had to have a useful purpose. Every ounce of enthusiasm had to be converted into sacrifice for a glorious future.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It was all just a way to try and not feel my feelings.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">There was one moment I was working through an emotional cycle related to dissociation during GB2, when I realized that I was making dissociation itself wrong. I was treating it as an unwelcome guest to be banished. But another word for dissociation is “imagination.” And suddenly I saw that I had eliminated every moment in my life where my imagination could roam free.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If I was driving in the car, I had to listen to a podcast to acquire more knowledge. If I was doing the dishes, I had to watch a YouTube video to “make use” of that time. If I was riding on an airplane, I had at least several books on hand. Even taking a walk in nature, I’d force myself to think about my projects and my plans and my goals. There was no escape. No chance for my simple childlike self to create those fanciful worlds he so loved. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">And the effects were clear: left with no opening to process, replay, or ponder life’s experiences, my mind forced that processing into other times when it was unwanted. I’d be watching a movie and find myself ruminating uncontrollably on something that happened that day. Playing with my kids, I’d find it impossible to stop thinking about a challenging conversation at work. Lying awake at night, it was as if the backlog of unprocessed thoughts and emotions was breaking through at the seams. And the more it threatened to explode, the harder I worked to keep it contained.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It turns out that constantly berating yourself to “be present” is not a great way to be present. That forcing yourself to focus is not a great way to maintain focus. That repeating to yourself “Get it right” isn’t a very effective or sustainable way to get it right. And that there is nothing wrong with allowing yourself to daydream.</span></p><h2><span style="font-weight: 400;">The dance of enjoyment</span></h2><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">My secondary intention had been to enjoy every aspect of the experience, without resistance.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">There were some things I needed to learn to be able to do that, such as allowing my body to express itself however it wanted. I did a lot of strong breathing in and out at random times. During pair exercises, I swayed back and forth, and stroked my legs to stay in my body. There&#8217;s no way my logical mind could have deduced these movements. I didn&#8217;t borrow them from anyone else. They are my body&#8217;s unique expression. Sometimes it was a bit weird, but that is the price of freedom.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">As a result, I think GB2 was the first time I enjoyed an intense personal development experience from beginning to end. Even in the midst of the strongest physical sensations of fear, there was a part of me inside that was tremendously excited to see it all unfolding. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Enjoyment is an end in itself, but I also found it an incredibly potent tool of transformation. When an emotion was coming online, I found that by welcoming and embracing it, most of the discomfort associated with it disappeared. Or it was transmuted into other feelings, like aliveness, exhilaration, wonder, or joy.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">There was a point where my metaphor for what we were doing changed, from breaking through a series of walls to something more akin to surfing. I was dancing and playing, glancing off one emotion to sail into another, or seeing what would happen if I combined two of them. It was like making art, or acting in a play – I could take on any point of view, through any emotional lens, to see what it had to offer, knowing that none of them were The Truth. When no state is permanent, all are welcome.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I’ve seen this flexibility and playfulness bleed over into my intellectual life and my work as well. It&#8217;s like my emotions form the contours of my inner landscape – what I’m allowed to feel and not allowed to feel; what emotions arise easily and which I resist. My thoughts then flow through and across that landscape. So my emotions determine where and how my thoughts are allowed to flow. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I find that I’m quicker to let go of any idea or perspective and try on another one for size. Which means I’m effectively smarter, because my cognitive energy is no longer being used to suppress my emotions. A </span><a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S014976342200197X" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><span style="font-weight: 400;">recent study at Stanford</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> showed that emotional suppression costs you about 30% of your working memory, as summarized in </span><a href="https://x.com/aakashgupta/status/2033777678948479201" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><span style="font-weight: 400;">this X post</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">. It definitely feels like 30% or so of my cognitive resources have been freed up.</span></p><p><div class="convertkit-form wp-block-convertkit-form"></div><!-- notionvc: edde0f97-d7b4-4d27-b5dc-3041317e2236 --></p><h2><span style="font-weight: 400;">Returning home to my kids</span></h2><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Among all these realizations and breakthroughs, the most meaningful ones were with my kids.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I returned home late at night, a few days after the end of the program. We had spent time practicing attunement – how to tune into what someone else was feeling, connect to their inner child and inherent goodness, and respectfully approach with loving care, the same way you’d slowly approach an abused cat that was lashing out at you.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">My daughter’s current thing is pretending she’s a cat, so the metaphor fit perfectly.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Normally, I don’t know what to do when my kids are crying. I’ll just kind of sit there, dumbfounded, wanting to comfort them but not knowing how. If it gets intense enough, I sometimes feel a bit triggered and leave the room.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">But this time with my daughter, I could feel what she was feeling and knew exactly what to do. I sat at the edge of her bed and made sympathetic sounds. I slowly edged closer and put just one hand on her foot, telling her I was with her. When she accepted my approach, I started slowly caressing her leg, and then her back, and told her how much I loved her. Before I knew it, we were rolling with laughter. She showed me how close laughter is to tears, really just the other side of the same coin.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I then went to my son’s bedroom to put him to bed. My son has been my great challenge as a parent, mostly because he’s so much like me. I’ve struggled with his stubbornness, his headstrong will, his insatiable need to touch everything and try everything and make every mistake, much as my parents would have said of me.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">He often throws tantrums at bedtime, and I’ve never quite known how to handle them. Usually, I’m so tired and just want to finish the bedtime routine to spend a few precious minutes with my wife before we go to sleep. He was doing the same tonight, unloading a backlog of emotions that had been stored up from the week I was gone.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I found, this time, that I could see him in a whole new light. His crying wasn’t triggering me, I noticed, probably because I’d just spent the whole week crying and being seen and loved in that state. I was able to be curious, to really look at him and listen, and suddenly I saw exactly what he needed. I put my hand on his back, and I said: “You didn’t do anything wrong.”</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">To my amazement, he stopped almost instantaneously, his crying and thrashing coming to a dead halt. He immediately sat up, wiped his tears, and said matter-of-factly, “Ok. Let’s play.” It wasn’t the pain of whatever had happened that threw him into turmoil, I realized. It was the shame and guilt that it provoked inside him. He couldn’t be with that shame, and with the disconnection from us and from himself that it caused. It was unbearable to his innocent, sweet soul.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In the weeks since, this phrase – “You didn’t do anything wrong” – has been like a magic spell. And not only for him, but for my wife Lauren as well. They’re both prone to shame. Prone to blaming themselves, condemning themselves, punishing themselves. I’m finding that I have the power to release them from at least some of that shame by simply telling them, “It’s not your fault.” Interestingly, that was the same phrase I found myself saying again and again as I reenacted my childhood scenes – “It wasn’t my fault.” I had to release myself from that prison before I could free others.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I think my parenting is the single biggest thing that changed after GB2. Almost every day, I realize something new in light of my own transformation. I’ve learned that many times, the message I thought I was sending to my kids wasn’t the one they were receiving. Or the lesson I thought I was teaching wasn’t the one they were learning.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">We were at a local dirt bike course recently, and I bought a popsicle for my daughter, Delia. The popsicle stick at the bottom was much too short, a tiny nub that she couldn’t hold. So she dropped it. I cleaned off the popsicle with a napkin and gave it back to her. She dropped it again, and I cleaned it off again. My frustration kept mounting more and more as she kept dropping it, as it often does whenever they spill things or make messes at home. I can often hear the echo of the phrase in my mind, “You should know better.” And sometimes I even say that out loud.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I realized with a sudden flash of insight what I’d been teaching my kids at these moments: that they’re not allowed to make mistakes. Not allowed to mess up. That they have to “get it right.” That phrase has tortured me my entire life, like a toxic mantra repeating in my mind most of the time: Get it right. Get it right. Get it right!</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Seeing through that veil, I could suddenly see that a different approach was needed. The issue was with the popsicle, not with her. What she needed was not my judgment, but my help in finding a solution. I kneeled down, broke the popsicle into pieces, and put it in a cup, so she could enjoy it without the anxiety I was projecting.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">This pattern has repeated in so many forms: once I see through my emotional response to a situation, I can calmly approach it from a curious, problem-solving, open-hearted perspective. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Another time, we were driving in the car, and my son Caio poked his sister in the backseat. He recently switched from a car seat to a booster seat and has been using his newfound freedom to torment her.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Normally, I’m quite harsh with misbehavior in the car, which I now realize was because of fear. The constant fear of getting into an accident with everything I care about in the world sitting vulnerably in the backseat. This time, instead of threatening my son with a timeout as usual, I calmly asked him what was going on. He explained himself, and I asked him to apologize to his sister, which he did. I then tried something new – I asked her if she was okay, and if it was okay if her brother didn’t get a timeout. To my surprise, she immediately said yes.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I realized something in that moment – that my sweet, empathetic daughter doesn’t share my impulse to punish. I’d always thought I needed to defend her, that I was upholding justice in her name. But she never holds a grudge, never seeks vengeance. She is always willing to forgive. That moment in the car became a chance for all three of us to grow closer, instead of a conflict that would spoil our mood for hours.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I’m learning to treat every conflict, upset, and frustration as a problem-solving opportunity. As teaching moments, rather than problems to be eradicated. We’ve adopted a new practice called “talkouts’ in place of timeouts. When the kids fight, we stop, slow down, and start asking questions about what happened and why. We unpack the situation, analyze how it unfolded, and explore alternative paths it could take. This requires a lot of capacity from us, to be clear, but I can’t think of a better way for our kids to learn how to cooperate, how to compromise, and how to work through disagreements.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Perhaps most importantly of all, my experience at GB2 allowed me to see all the various forms of wisdom that my dad had always been trying to give me, but I hadn&#8217;t been able to accept. His criticism was his way of showing love; an artist’s expression of love, in the form of excellence and care. His stubbornness and pride, which had always rankled me, I can now see were his way of protecting his own innocence. It was the way that he knew to protect his inherent goodness in the face of four rambunctious children demanding everything he had to give, and more. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Forgiving my father and seeing the world from his point of view has been like a master key in forgiving myself. It’s only by allowing his humanity that I can allow my own. It’s only by accepting that his mistakes were part of his own journey, that I can make my mistakes part of my own. By not only accepting but actually feeling grateful for his missteps, I can be free to love my children with reckless abandon, knowing that my missteps will sow the seeds of their own future transformation many years from now. </span></p><h2><span style="font-weight: 400;">Fear and AI</span></h2><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">On the work front, my rediscovery of fear has had a tremendous impact on how I understand what I do.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In the weeks leading up to GB2, my team and I had worked hard to plan and prepare the launch of our new flagship course, </span><a href="https://www.buildingasecondbrain.com/ai-second-brain" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><span style="font-weight: 400;">The AI Second Brain</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">. This launch was really years in the making, the result of three years of intensive research and experimentation since the original launch of ChatGPT. It represented not just a new product offering, but the pivot of everything we do toward AI.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">We’d written all the marketing copy, made all the key decisions, designed the whole curriculum, and everything was ready to go when I left for Northern California. All I had to do was get back and hit the “launch” button.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">But as the days passed, and I went deeper and deeper into the heart of fear, the landscape started to shift under my feet. I began to realize that so much of my attitude toward AI had been driven and fueled by underlying fear.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I was afraid on so many levels. On the personal level, of whether my thinking and my work would have any value in the future. Whether any of my ideas would be needed or useful. I was afraid I’d have no way of making money, of supporting my team or my family. I was afraid for my children’s future, and didn’t know what to tell them about what they should learn. I was so afraid for the future of my business, and for the whole education field, and for the jobs of everyone that AI would make unemployable. I feared for the future of human civilization.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">And then I could see how profoundly all that fear had shaped my decisions and actions, especially toward AI. I’d spent countless hours racing to consume every last viral post on X, buying into every clickbait hook and promise that “this changes everything.” I’d poured incredible amounts of time and energy into learning and implementing the most complicated, cutting-edge methods – configuring my openclaw, spawning agent swarms for no reason, and automating things that never needed to exist in the first place.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It was all driven by the fear of being left behind, of being made obsolete, of missing the wave that carried everyone else to eternal bliss. It was advanced FOMO, in other words.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">And then I could see that I was about to unleash all this fear, all this FOMO, all this overcomplicated and unnecessary legwork, on my students and followers. The curriculum I’d designed was highly technical, when that’s never been my strength. It was pushing the bleeding edge frontier, when my principles have always advocated being a late adopter of tools that were already mature. I’d focused narrowly on features and tools, when it has always been the human aspects of technology that have most interested me.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Who do humans become in the face of new technology? How do they change and adapt in light of change and uncertainty? How does our human psychology, endlessly complex and malleable, reshape itself to fit the new tools? How will culture shift, language evolve, and human relationships mutate to give us what we need in the new era? I’d forgotten all this in the rush to be one of the cool kids touting AI magic that is often just smoke and mirrors.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I returned from GB2, and left my team a long series of voice notes recounting what I’d learned, what I’d seen, and how we needed to start over. It would still be a course about AI. That was a change that people needed help navigating, and that we could help them with. But the curriculum needed to center the human experience, needed to focus on amplifying human judgment and creativity. We needed to use AI to return to our true north – unlocking people’s creative potential.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It was a close call, but we managed to change direction in time to launch the program as scheduled. You can see what that looked like in the </span><a href="https://youtu.be/yeTn8a5J-Gc" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><span style="font-weight: 400;">introductory session</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> I taught to over 1,000 people live. It is the expression of my soul, of my heart, not only to contribute what I can, but to receive everything there is to receive in return.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I know that will include love, appreciation, and recognition, as always. But it will also include criticism, anger, resistance, fear, resentment, and many other emotions. I’m here for all of them. I’m willing to hear what they all have to teach me. </span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I deeply believe that what AI truly represents is a spiritual revolution in human civilization. It is the unshackling of our transcendent minds from the toil of cognitive labor. It is the elevation of human creativity to its rightful place, at the apex of intelligence. AI is our child, a mind of our own creation, reflecting back to us who we are, what we value, as well as what we fear and loathe, just as human children do. I am so humbled to be alive at this moment in history, to witness the rise of a new form of intelligence that has never existed before.</span></p><p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In that spirit, I dumped all my notes from GB2 into Claude and asked it to analyze my experience. Here’s how it responded:</span></p><blockquote><p><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">“Honestly, a few things struck me.</span></i></p><p><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The through-line is remarkably coherent for something written over 6 days in real-time. That&#8217;s a clean arc.</span></i></p><p><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The professional fear thread was interesting too — your grandfather&#8217;s journalism career, your father&#8217;s discipline-as-protection, your own fear of the AI pivot. The journal seems to catch you mid-realization that tightening is the thing that produces the failure you&#8217;re afraid of (page 18-19 lays this out almost like a proof).</span></i></p><p><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The page 25 list of 11 beliefs about disconnection felt like it could be its own turning point — the sheer volume of ways you&#8217;d convinced yourself that connection is scarce, fragile, and external.</span></i></p><p><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">And &#8220;I love my anguish&#8221; at the very end is a line that could mean a lot of different things. ## 中文译文

六年前，我参加了在加州北部举办的一周课程，名为“破局者”（Groundbreakers）。

该课程由乔·哈德森（Joe Hudson）及其妻子塔拉（Tara）主持。这是我人生中最具震撼力的经历之一。这次体验彻底重塑了我与“头脑中的声音”——即我内在独白——的关系。它帮助我重新连接了自己早已在童年时期就切断的愤怒情绪。

我还清楚地记得，在如今我们所称的“GB1”第一天，我立下的那个意图。当时我们十二人围坐成一圈，依次说出自己希望从这一周中获得什么。我原本预计会说些听起来很策略、很体面的话；但轮到我时，心跳却剧烈得几乎失控。在众人注视之下，我竟哽咽着挤出这样一句话：“我想把我心中所有的爱，全部释放到这个世界里去。”

我也清晰记得这句话背后的情感：我知道自己拥有太多可贡献的东西，太多可给予的东西；而我比什么都更渴望这么做——只是完全不知该如何开始。仿佛有一道巨大的屏障，阻隔在我与最简单的“给予”行为之间。我能真切感受到，心中积聚的爱如大坝蓄水，几近决堤；而我唯一想做的，就是让它奔涌而出。

亲爱的读者，站在今日回望，我可以肯定地说：过去这些年，我已完完全全实现了当初立下的这个意图。我还记得“破局者”结束几天后，我在金门公园散步的情景——情绪刚刚解封，如激流般在我体内奔涌不息。就在此刻，我突然停下脚步，意识到：内心情绪的流动，与创造力的流动，根本无法区分——它们本是一体。

自那天起，我开启了一系列创造性项目，彻底改变了我的人生轨迹：我撰写了多部图书，迄今销量已逾五十万册；我重启了以学习小组为模式的课程，授课学生超过五千人；我创办了一家估值数百万美元的企业，并组建了一支团队，服务全球数万名学员。这一切，都是我对这个世界、对其中人民那份深沉之爱的自然表达。

然而，随着岁月流逝，我渐渐察觉，似乎仍有什么尚未圆满——一个崭新的自我正渴望浮现，渴望发声。我无法准确说出那是什么，但当“破局者第二期”的邀请函抵达时，我立刻明白：我必须报名参加。六年之后，旅程的第一段已然走完，是时候启程踏上第二段了。

（若您想了解我参加首届“破局者”的经历，可点击此处阅读。）

第二期

我从墨西哥城附近的家中出发，飞抵加州北部，再从索诺玛县机场搭乘优步，驱车两小时，抵达位于更北边的莱克县的活动场地。

那是一座风景如画的乡村静修中心，坐落在如诗如画的山谷之中，原是一处儿童夏令营旧址。简朴的小木屋星罗棋布于园区各处。时值三月，初春阳光温暖宜人，白日和煦，夜晚却逼近冰点。

首日，我们在最大的会议厅集合。共有三十六人参加，分为三组，每组十二人；另配有十余名引导师、小组带领者及助理。许多面孔似曾相识：有的是通过我或我的文字了解到“成就艺术”（Art of Accomplishment，简称AOA）这家主办机构的；也有人曾与我一同上过其他课程。

我们随即分入各自小组，在椅子与沙发围成的紧密圆圈中落座，轮流陈述自己的意图。组内我仅认识一人，也是一位网络内容创作者。事前我毫无准备，但当轮到我时，一个意图却自然而然浮现在唇边。

在脑海中快进回放过去六年，我忽然看清：尽管向外表达对他人的爱——这一渴望至关重要——但在那股向外奔涌的急切之中，另一样东西却被遗忘了：接受他人之爱。我仿佛看见自己一生的缩影：不断给予、给予、再给予；而一旦轮到接收，我却本能地阻拦、回避、退缩——恰恰躲开了自己最渴求的那种爱。

于是，我郑重宣布了本周的意图：“我要全然接纳一切爱。”话音刚落，我立即感到理性思维本能地退缩：这岂非自私？岂非纵容？大脑立刻试图为这一目标加设限定或边界——“你所谓‘一切’，究竟是什么意思？！”

而在内心深处，我还怀有第二个意图：“我要全程沉浸其中，不抗拒、不抵抗。”也就是说，让整个过程变得愉悦。过去十五年，我参与过无数类似个人成长体验，却总是咬紧牙关、步步对抗、奋力挣扎。我已厌倦如此。这一次，我渴望的是欢欣与振奋。

限制性信念

GB2（我们如此称呼它）的官方主题是“限制性信念”。

课前准备包括收听两期AOA播客：《限制性信念：指引我们人生的隐形轨道》与《如何看穿限制性信念》。我在此分享这两期节目，因为若您愿深入探索这一主题，它们堪称绝佳入门。

我们需要在日记本中尽可能多地列出自身存在的限制性信念。当我开始动笔时，发现这些信念的表述方式格外引人深思。我向来将限制性信念理解为逻辑谬误，视其为认知偏见与知识局限的产物。

而乔所提出的定义则截然不同：

任何引发身体紧缩感的想法；  
生活中任何紧张的源头；  
任何你并不想要的生活习惯；  
人际关系中任何紧张之处；  
你本人或你的事业中任何缺乏自如表现的领域。

换言之，限制性信念主要依据其在我们的身体、关系或工作中所引发的紧张感来界定。采用这一宽泛得多的定义后，它们便如泉涌般倾泻而出：

“你只能在聪明与快乐之间二选一。”  
“我必须表现成某种样子，才会被人喜欢。”  
“我永远无法充分感受自己情绪的深度。”  
“我的巅峰已过，未来岁月再难超越过往。”  
“我必须持续进行自我成长，才能获得幸福、自由与喜悦。”  
“我的家人永远无法摆脱他们的限制性信念。”  
“我必须严格管教儿子，才能让他守规矩。”  
“我注定会成为父亲那样的人。”  
“创意工作与财务成功彼此冲突。”  
“我不是一位称职的父亲或丈夫。”  
“我任何时候都只能维系有限数量的人际关系。”

随着首日课程推进，我们学习了整周将要探索并实践的处理限制性信念的方法。第一课便是：克服限制性信念，本质上并非一个理性的过程。

你完全可以百分之百确信某个信念——比如“金钱稀缺”——是错误的。你可以列举所有反驳它的理由，指出那些与之相悖的事实，甚至构想出一个你希望取而代之的新信念。但这一切都无济于事。那信念仿佛被胶水牢牢粘住。最多，逻辑上的解构只能在门扉上撬开一道细缝。

限制性信念之所以顽固，即便我们明知其谬误，是因为它们持续被一种潜在的情绪循环所滋养。

例如，我身上最普遍的信念之一，可凝练为一句：“你本该做得更好。”当团队成员做出错误决策时，我会这样想；当孩子把牛奶洒满餐厅地板时，我也会这样想；而最常出现的，是我对自己做出错误选择时，一遍遍对自己这样说。

任何情绪循环的起点，都是一种你不愿感受的情绪。对我而言，这种情绪便是无助感——面对自己或他人所犯的错误与失误时，那种无能为力、束手无策的感觉。为了逃避无助，我转而投向评判与责备，因为后者远比前者更容易承受。

这种评判很快会异化为内疚：我因如此严苛地评判而自责（“我本该更有耐心、更富爱心、更具慈悲心”）。内疚继而催生伪善的慈悲（“若我不帮他们，他们会因此受伤”）。但既然这慈悲源于内疚，它很快又会滑向怨恨（“这是在纵容他们。我必须坚守界限”）。怨恨则轻易再次导向评判，于是循环往复。

乔将此称为“黄金算法”：对于任何你抗拒感受的情绪，你都会不由自主地采取种种行动，结果却一次次重新制造出同一种情绪。这是你的身体反复尝试回归稳态的过程——去感受那些它至今尚无法真正感受的东西。

我们被要求开始识别自身的情绪循环，并在笔记本中用环形图将其绘制出来。看见它，是穿透它的第一步。

我不会详述各项练习与实践的具体内容，以免削弱其力量——若您将来有机会亲身体验，它们将更具效力。但课程涵盖形式极为丰富：呼吸练习、凝视互动、教练式对话、小组体验、情绪释放、双人协作、拥抱、林间漫步、音乐与诗歌、肢体动作、情景重现……所有这些，皆旨在为我们的身体创造空间与流动性，以此消解限制性信念所带来的僵硬与紧缩。

这并非关于抛弃“错误”的信念、代之以“正确”的信念。而是关于看清：每个信念都只是现实的一个片面视角，且会在身体中唤起某种我们已不再想要的感受。最终，限制性信念所限制的，是我们做真实自我的能力。

我们继续深入，探索任何引发身体紧绷、激发情绪反应、或阻碍我们通往喜悦的念头与想法。我又发现一层更深的信念，它们对我而言显得尤为危险，因其看似无比真实：

“我无法掌控所爱之人将遭遇何事。”  
“宇宙并无内在秩序或终极目的。”  
“我与这个世界格格不入。”  
“大多数人其实并不具备解决自身问题的能力。”  
“我必须履行责任，只因我享有特权。”  
“我注定肩负更伟大的使命。”  
“资源永远稀缺，总量恒定。”  
“爱是一种交换或交易。”  
“我必须不断证明自身价值。”  
“关系始终处于衰变之中。”  
“脆弱令人痛苦。”  
“我必须走向极端，才能找到真正有效的个人成长体验。”  
“我必须做到完美无缺。”  
“生命短暂，不容虚掷。”

我震惊地发现，“黄金算法”竟在最细微的情境中运转不息。例如，在家庭聚会等社交场合中，我稍感焦虑或不安，脑海便立刻浮现“我很怪异”“我不属于这里”之类念头。这些念头随即触发恐惧（“我会被孤立、被拒绝”，或“我无法与任何人建立联结”）。恐惧继而导致不堪重负与退缩（“我正在假装成另一个人”，或“我太沉浸于自己的思绪”）。此时，我已陷入解离状态，实际并未真正在场——而这必然导致新一轮的焦虑与不安，因为我既未倾听，亦未跟上对话节奏，更无法回应社交信号。循环就此闭合，并重新启动。

幸运的是，“黄金算法”并非不可打破。出路在于：主动迎向你一直回避的情绪——欢迎它，并感激它正试图教会你的功课——而非继续逃避。循环本身的目的，正是终结循环；而终结它的方法，就是全然感受它不断试图唤醒的那个情绪。道理如此简单，却未必容易践行。

整周核心练习是一套包含两个问卷的工具，共计十六个问题。我们两人一组，面对面而坐，彼此问答。对方如同一面镜子，映照出你灵魂深处的真实。

我们持续深入，我又触碰到另一层限制性信念，它们于我而言，宛如不容置疑的铁律：

“我无法以劳伦（Lauren）应得的深度去爱她。”  
“我的孩子终将被杀害。”  
“我永远学不会全然爱自己。”  
“我在他人眼中毫无真正意义。”  
“若卸下伪装，我便不会被爱。”  
“临终之际，我必为未能足够深爱身边之人而悔恨。”

当这些话被大声说出时，每一句都如砖墙迎面撞来。它们是我存在最核心的恐惧，是冰冷坚硬、坚不可摧的现实支柱，我连一丝划痕都无法留下，更遑论改变。我从中感受到深切的悲恸，发出狂怒、心碎或惊恐的嘶吼。泪水如决堤般奔涌不息，用过的纸巾堆满椅旁两侧。

穿越这组限制性信念，犹如穿越一座精妙绝伦的迷宫。有时，通往下一通道的路径初看如一堵实墙，随后却悄然裂开一道微隙，一线光芒刺破压抑的黑暗。

我深知，自己最深层的恐惧之一便是：“我的孩子终将被杀害。”生活在墨西哥，交通法规与安全规范形同虚设，这种恐惧始终如影随形。我的想象力编排出数十种可能发生的场景，而最令人心悸的，恰是那些稀松平常、随时可能降临的意外。

在伙伴协助下逐步梳理时，起初我仍觉得“我的孩子终将死去”这一判断百分之百真实。但随后我注意到，恐惧的实质略有不同：它并非指向死亡本身，而是指向“他们将在我的看护下被杀害”。那是我的失职，我的责任。这便是恐惧逻辑中出现的第一道裂痕——一个突破口。

在回应某个问题时，我忽然洞悉了这一信念的功能：它意在保护我免受未来失去与心碎之苦。我的大脑试图提前为我做好准备，以抵御它确信将彻底摧毁我的那种悲恸。我为此信念致以谢意。

但随着我们继续探讨其他问题，我逐渐看清：它对当下所产生的效果，往往与其初衷背道而驰。这种恐惧让我时刻处于过度警觉与过分谨慎的状态，哪怕身处毫无危险的情境中。我们曾在镇中心广场悠闲漫步，而我脑中却只盘旋着下一个危险将从何处袭来。我亦清醒地意识到，这种恐惧实际上并未保护我——几个月前，我儿子就在街边被一只狗咬伤，事发地点距我仅几步之遥。纵使我时刻警惕，依然无能为力。

我转而关注这一信念此刻让我产生的感受。刹那之间，我感受到了毁灭般的痛楚、难以承受的丧失、彻底的绝望、浓重的黑暗、湮灭感与无望感。所有孩子离世时我将经历的情绪，早已潜伏在我意识表层之下，日夜翻腾不息。仿佛我正不断重演着孩子们死亡的现实——而这一事件尚未发生，甚至可能永不会发生。

这带来真实的代价：我与孩子们疏离、退缩；他们在违规或仅仅迈出一步走向街道时，我便严厉苛责。我彻夜难眠，忧心忡忡，噩梦连连，对此疲惫至极。我猛然醒悟：在竭力保护孩子安全的过程中，我竟忽略了真正有效的方式——教他们培养情境觉察力，而非死守僵化规则；教他们信任直觉，哪怕危险尚不明确；教他们信任我，而非畏惧我的怒火。

我本欲保护他们远离世界，却反而教会他们畏惧世界。这恰恰使世界变得更加危险，从而首先印证了我最初的叙事。又一个情绪循环，又一个自我强化的闭环。

我意识到，这套信念系统源自我的父亲——他总对我们说，唯有严格管教，才能保障我们的安全。如今我方能看清，他对我们怀着多么深切的恐惧，而严格管教正是他保护我们的方式。我承袭了他的恐惧，因而同样强烈地渴望强制服从，于是将这一循环延续至下一代。

悲恸、哀伤、悔恨、失望如潮水般将我淹没。我一直在自己身上、在年幼的家庭中、在代际之间，持续复制着整个情绪循环。所有这些循环彼此相连，而只需折断其中一环，整个链条便开始松解。

而真相，竟还更为幽深！不久后，在梳理另一个相关信念时，我发觉：父母当年对我展现的那种保护姿态，如今我也正以同样方式施予他们。十多年前，我们全家经历了一系列危机，彼时我形成的信念是：我必须成为“懂事、可靠、省心”的孩子，才能保护父母——一个从不惹麻烦的模范子女。

这在我成年后表现为对父母的自我隐藏：我的恐惧、疑虑、挣扎、痛苦，统统未曾向他们袒露。我未与他们分享自己真实的处境——而旅居世界各地，更让这种隐瞒变得轻而易举。我佯装一切顺遂如意，这无异于将父母视为弱小、脆弱、亟需保护的对象，仿佛我是家长，而他们才是孩子。事实上，是他们创造了我，而非我创造了他们。我所知、所是的一切，皆由他们所授；我所拥有的一切，皆由他们所赐。他们无需我的照料，唯愿我的爱。

直面恐惧

您大概已察觉，我绝大多数限制性信念，都围绕着一个我长久以来拒绝感受的核心情绪：恐惧。

回头重读我关于“破局者第一期”的记述，我惊讶地发现，恐惧的痕迹遍布全文！我在那篇文字中竟使用了十八次“恐惧”“害怕”“惊恐”等字眼！然而，若在GB2开始前一天问我是否与恐惧搏斗，我定会断然否认。

事实上，我的人生履历恰恰显示出一种显著的“无惧”：我是一名资深创业者，曾居住于全球最危险或最动荡的地区——里约热内卢的贫民窟、靠近哥伦比亚革命武装力量（FARC）控制区的哥伦比亚北部、乌克兰东部。我甚至怀疑，所有这些冒险行径，或许正是我竭尽所能、只为真切感受恐惧的尝试。

我越深入审视，越发现恐惧已浸透我生命的每个角落：婚姻、育儿、商业决策、与团队的关系、日常社交互动……但我仍不确定，究竟该如何在身体中真切感受它。

第五天，许多人发现自己正重返一段早期创伤经历——那是我们曾感到恐惧或需要帮助，却未获得所需关爱与抚慰的时刻，也正是我们关于世界的信念最初萌生之地。

在练习开始前的说明环节，我举手问道：“但如果我感受不到恐惧呢？”我无法回忆起身体中任何与恐惧相关的特定感觉。它对我而言似乎遥不可及，仿佛一间被混凝土块与无缝石膏板严密封死的房间，不留一丝痕迹。乔告诉我：当我提出这个问题时，我其实已站在悬崖边缘，只需坚持住。

几位引导师现场示范了练习的样貌。我无意详述细节，既为避免破坏您未来亲身体验的效果，也因这一过程需在高度特定且安全的环境中，才能健康展开。

随着练习展开，我意识到自己其实早已在感受恐惧的身体反应：双手冰冷潮湿，覆着一层薄汗，微微颤抖；下颌与脖颈僵硬紧绷，如被锁住；胃部沉坠着一股冰冷沉重的压力。我惊讶地发现，这些竟是恐惧的经典生理征兆。不知为何，我曾以为“我的恐惧”必以某种更特别、更独特的方式显现。

轮到我时，我真不知自己能否完成。起初，感觉略似表演——在地板上翻滚、用手遮脸、像幼童般呜咽。

但随后，在小组成员源源不绝、毫无保留的爱、接纳与精准共情的包围下，一切变得真实起来。我瞬间被带回童年挨父亲鞭打的场景：父母床上的床单、镜面衣柜门、我被倒扣在他膝头时地毯的纹理，纤毫毕现。

我产生一种无法抗拒的畏缩与战栗冲动。我将头深深埋进地板，脸颊用力抵住地毯，试图逃离。我用双臂护住头部，双手捂住面孔，放声尖叫。我感到自己渺小、惊恐、失控、身处险境。我甚至无法辨认自己发出的多数声音——凄厉的哀鸣、撕心的哭号、粗重的喘息。终于，我释放了那个小男孩当年未能喊出的恐惧，完成了三十多年前未能完成的动作。

小组成员围拢在我身边，轻抚、安慰着我，口中说着当年我最需要听到的话语。原始的躯体感受最终化为语言，而我使用的，正是孩童时期的语汇：“我没做错！”我一遍又一遍地哭喊，“我什么都没做……”“这不是我的错……”“这不公平……”我能真切感受到自己在申辩清白，抗议自己所遭受的对待。

随后，我的愤怒与恐惧终于化为悲恸，一股洪流般的释然随之涌来。我听见自己说：“你夺走了我的父亲。”我恍然彻悟：真正的痛楚并非鞭打本身——那几秒钟便结束了。更深的痛楚，是它在我与父亲之间造成的隔阂——那位我敬爱、仰慕、并以之为榜样的男人。

我还清晰记得那隔阂是如何形成的。它并非挨打后的自动后果，而是在事后不久，我主动作出的一个决定。我记得一清二楚：我走出父母卧室，穿过走廊回到自己房间——那是我通常用来平复情绪的地方。那是我最后一次挨打，那时我已太大，或许七岁、八岁或九岁。

当我穿过走廊时，我记得自己拼命思索如何报复父亲，如何以他伤害我的方式伤害他。我唯一能想到的办法，便是永远切断他对我的爱。这是我唯一拥有的武器。于是我这么做了。我在心中筑起一道高墙，永不许他跨越；我建起一座内在圣殿，他永远无法知晓、无法触及。在那里，我将保护自己；在那里，我将获得安全。

我甚至记得当时，内心有个小小的声音说：“你终将为此付出代价。”我惊讶于自己当时竟能如此清醒地意识到：我正对自己做什么，以及这将付出何种代价。

站在今日回望，我明白了事情的真相：当我切断父亲对我的爱时，我也同时切断了他对我的爱。我也切断了母亲的爱，因为她未曾保护我。某种程度上，这亦让我难以给予或接受生命中任何他人的爱——爱是危险的；回避它，才更安全。

向父亲与上帝敞开心扉

一两天后，在本周最后一项练习中，我们再次两人一组，轮流回答一系列旨在整合体验的问题。

该练习的最后一个问题，也是本周数百个问题的最终一步，显得颇为奇特：“什么在爱着你？”

起初，我一片空白。我能脱口而出“谁在爱我”，答案信手拈来；但“什么在爱我？”——这问题仿佛不合逻辑。

接着，答案击中了我：是上帝在爱我。它如启示般降临，仿佛一扇窗豁然打开，阳光倾泻而入。那一刻，我感到体内一条通道被开启——它本就充盈丰沛，只待我轻轻拧开阀门，便即刻涌流。那感觉充满喜悦，又带着几分俏皮，仿佛与挚友在同一时刻想到同一事物。

我意识到：当我切断父母对我的爱时，我也切断了上帝对我的爱。年少时，我曾拥有深刻的信仰：我和母亲都曾在教堂乐队演奏；作为家庭，我们每周数次出入教堂，积极参与各项活动；多年间，我渴望成为一名传教士，甚至已踏上这条道路——青少年时期，我曾赴伯利兹、法国与埃塞俄比亚开展福音传教之旅。

这一切在我二十出头时轰然崩塌：我离开了宗教信仰，走向另一极端，自诩为无神论者。这一转变开启了我对其他意义源泉的追寻：从沉浸于异国文化，到研习哲学与科学，再到投身个人成长，将其作为通往超越性的途径。

我想，我确实把婴儿连同洗澡水一起泼掉了——我否定了所有无法亲眼所见、亲手触摸或精确测量之物。“虚无主义”的阴霾笼罩而至，我竭尽所能试图驱散：通过利他主义、通过理性思考、通过迷幻体验、通过组建家庭。这些方式在不同程度上奏效，但总有一种缺失感——一种足以涵容我的浩瀚力量。

次日，小组中一位女士发给我亨利·诺文（Henri Nouwen）的一段话，精准道出了我始终追寻的浩瀚：

“这是一个奥秘：我们存在的核心——心灵——被上帝转化为祂自己的心，一颗足以拥抱整个宇宙的心。借由祈祷，我们能在心中承载人类一切的痛苦与悲伤、一切的冲突与煎熬、一切的酷刑与战争、一切的饥荒、孤独与苦难——并非出于某种伟大的心理或情感能力，而是因为上帝的心已与我们的心合而为一。”

课程结束后的次日，我在静修中心上方的山丘间行走，真切感知到上帝的爱已然归来。我亦感到，它其实从未离开。我体会到一种解脱：不必再耗费巨大心力将它拒之门外——这真的需要惊人的努力。

至于如今我对上帝的确切理解，我尚不明确。我无意将其简化为一套信条或教义。我怀疑自己不会再回归任何可辨识的昔日基督教信仰形态。我希望从第一性原理出发，从直接经验出发，重新认识上帝是谁、是什么。我对一切保持开放。

学习如何接受

随着课程临近尾声，我回到最初立下的意图——“全然接纳一切爱”，并从一个全然不同的视角重新审视它。

我的个人成长之旅始于父亲。在GB1中，我梳理了他带给我伤害所引发的愤怒；而这一次，我亦对他感到愤怒，但原因不同——因为他未能接纳我的爱。

我亦看清，这同样适用于我的两个孩子——三岁的凯奥（Caio）与五岁的德莉亚（Delia）。我常常将陪伴孩子的时间视为一种责任，仿佛只是待办清单上的一项任务。我清楚知道他们多么需要我的爱，却未曾意识到，我同样多么需要他们的爱。他们有着深切的需求，要去学习如何爱我——而这，正是他们通过我的榜样，学会如何接受他人之爱的方式。

我曾对孩子诸多不满之处——冗长的睡前流程、永不停歇的游戏渴望、无论我做什么都执意要陪在我身边——原来都是他们决心爱我的表达。他们为爱我而无所不用其极，哪怕面对我为阻止他们而设立的“界限”与规则。

我与乔谈及此事，他指出：他人对你的爱，正是他们支持你的方式；否则，你便会沦为“饿鬼”——永远渴求爱，却永远无法觅得，哪怕它就静静伫立眼前，唾手可得。

我在工作中亦目睹此景：团队成员不断以各种方式表达支持意愿，而我却屡屡拒绝；我在追随者、客户与学生身上亦看到这一点：我如此专注地给予、给予、再给予，却忽视了他们多么渴望反过来为我贡献。我意识到，多年来，我竟耗费巨大心力去躲避、推拒那如潮水般涌来的爱与感激——仅仅出于最朴素的恐惧。

我心中升起一个新问题：倘若学会接受团队与社群的爱，将如何重塑我的工作？倘若我生命中一切渴望创造、达成与体验之事的瓶颈，正是——学会接受爱呢？！

后续余波

GB2结束后的日子，恍若置身缥缈仙境，超凡脱俗。

我对周遭一切，包括他人所谓的“能量”（暂且如此称呼），变得异常敏感。离开小镇途中，我走进索诺玛机场附近一家按摩店，瞬间便感受到焦虑如辐射波般在整个空间激荡回响。员工们神经紧绷，蜷缩在角落。

店主是一位中年亚裔女性，她竭尽所能营造表面的宁静与放松：小小的佛像、蕨类与绿植、潺潺流水的喷泉、舒缓的音乐。但这一切，却与她所散发的情绪——忧虑与恐惧——完全相悖。气氛令人极度不安，我不得不立刻转身离去。这让我思忖：有多少小型企业主苦苦挣扎，并非因为缺乏正确战略或优质服务，而是因为他们未被感知的情绪，正在悄然瓦解一切努力构筑之物。

次日，我两次搭乘优步，司机与我聊起限制性信念，以及它们如何被情绪循环牢牢锁定。我向来从不与优步司机攀谈，但那些日子里，我发现自己被遇见的每个人磁石般吸引，他们似乎也同样被我吸引。

某晚，我在希尔兹堡一家寿司店独自用餐，邻桌一对夫妇主动搭话。在绝大多数日常社交中，我记忆中一直萦绕着一种潜在的恐惧感：我总能感觉到眼周肌肉微微绷紧、双眼略略睁大，时刻预期着被拒绝或漠视，从而无意识地招致它。而此刻，我察觉自己的双眼已松弛许多，谈话流畅自然，以至于我竟忘了这种轻松感原来如此久违。他们临别时对我说的最后一句话，是两个陌生人在茫茫人海中偶然交汇时——
