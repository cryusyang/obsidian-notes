<img alt="Negotiation Skills" height="133" src="https://www.pon.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/images/posts/negotiation-skills-1.jpg" title="Negotiation Skills" width="200" /><br /><p>Many people say they dread negotiating and avoid it whenever possible. Why? Most often, it’s because they see negotiation as a competition in which one party’s gains come directly at the expense of the other. What we tend to overlook is that most negotiations allow room for <em>both</em> competition and collaboration. Add the collaborative element to the table, and negotiation becomes not only more productive but also far more rewarding.</p>
<p>A small subset of negotiations are <a href="http://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/negotiation-skills-daily/find-more-value-at-the-bargaining-table/">distributive</a>, or single-issue negotiations. A negotiation over the price of a used car, for example, may focus on just one issue: price. In this type of value-claiming negotiation, the parties are competing over a fixed pool of resources, and one side’s gain does come at the other’s expense.</p>
<p>Far more often, however, negotiations involve multiple issues. These are known as integrative negotiations, or value-creating negotiations, because they allow parties to expand the pool of value through tradeoffs and creative deal design. Even a used-car negotiation can become integrative if you look beyond price. For instance, if you run a public relations business, you might negotiate a lower price in exchange for offering the dealership some PR support—a tradeoff that could benefit both sides.</p>

<h3><strong>3 guidelines for creating value in negotiation</strong></h3>
<p>How can you make the most of integrative negotiations, creating as much value as possible before dividing it? Here are three guidelines drawn from research and teaching at the Program on Negotiation at Harvard Law School.</p>
<p><strong>1. Capitalize on differences. </strong></p>
<p style="padding-left: 30px;">When negotiators encounter differences, they often see them as obstacles. In reality, differences are frequently opportunities to create value, write Max H. Bazerman and **Don A. Moore their book <em>Judgment in Managerial Decision Making</em>. By trading across different preferences—a process known as logrolling—negotiators can gain more of what they value most by conceding on issues they value less.</p>
<p style="padding-left: 30px;">For example, you might negotiate a lower per-unit price in exchange for faster payment, a longer contract, or a larger order size.</p>
<p style="padding-left: 30px;">Differences go beyond preferences. You can also create value by leveraging different expectations about the future through <a href="http://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/dealmaking-daily/dealmaking-before-you-sign-on-the-dotted-line/">contingent contracts</a>. Suppose you’re negotiating with a contractor over a home renovation and believe the project is unlikely to be completed by the stated deadline. You might propose paying a higher price for on-time completion and a lower price if the project runs late. If the contractor is confident in her timeline, she may be happy to accept the arrangement.</p>
<p><strong>2. Ask questions and share information. </strong></p>
<p style="padding-left: 30px;">Negotiators often reach impasse because they focus narrowly on stated positions, such as a long closing period or a firm delivery date. One way to move past these sticking points is to ask thoughtful questions that uncover underlying interests.</p>
<p style="padding-left: 30px;">For example, instead of rejecting a lengthy closing period outright, you might ask, “Can you help me understand why you need that much time?” The answer may reveal interests that are open to tradeoff. If the delay is tied to a legitimate constraint, you might agree to it in exchange for a better price or other concessions that offset the inconvenience.</p>
<p style="padding-left: 30px;">Sharing information can also help break deadlock. While you should never reveal your bottom line or other sensitive details that would weaken your position, Bazerman and Moore advise negotiators to share limited information about the trades they are willing to consider. Doing so incrementally can encourage reciprocity and lead to more productive problem-solving.</p>
<p><strong>3. Negotiate multiple issues simultaneously. </strong></p>
<p style="padding-left: 30px;">Many negotiators believe they are being systematic when they address issues one at a time—price first, then delivery, then service terms. In fact, this approach often limits opportunities to create value.</p>
<p style="padding-left: 30px;">A more effective strategy is to negotiate multiple issues simultaneously. Start by agreeing that nothing is final until everything is agreed upon. This mindset encourages flexibility and makes it easier to identify beneficial tradeoffs across issues. From there, propose package deals that reflect your preferred combinations of outcomes, rather than isolated demands.</p>
<p>Integrative negotiation doesn’t require sacrificing your interests in the name of cooperation. On the contrary, it allows both sides to get more of what they want by identifying what each party values most. Once you’ve expanded the pie, you’re in a much stronger position to claim your share of it.</p>

## 中文译文

许多人表示，他们害怕谈判，尽可能避免参与谈判。为什么？最常见的原因是，他们将谈判视为一场零和博弈，即一方的所得直接以另一方的所失为代价。而我们往往忽视的是，大多数谈判既存在竞争空间，也存在合作余地。一旦将协作元素引入谈判桌，谈判不仅会变得更加富有成效，也会带来远为丰厚的回报。

一小部分谈判属于“分配式谈判”，即单议题谈判。例如，就一辆二手车的价格进行谈判，可能只聚焦于一个议题：价格。在这类价值索取型谈判中，双方争夺的是固定总量的资源，一方获益的确意味着另一方受损。

然而，更常见的情形是，谈判涉及多个议题。这类谈判被称为“整合式谈判”或“价值创造型谈判”，因为双方可通过权衡取舍与创造性地设计交易方案，共同扩大整体价值池。即便是二手车买卖谈判，只要超越单纯的价格视角，同样可以转变为整合式谈判。例如，如果你经营一家公关公司，就可协商降低购车价格，同时为经销商提供一定的公关支持——这一互惠性权衡对双方都有利。

在谈判中创造价值的三大原则

如何最大限度地利用整合式谈判，在分割利益之前尽可能多地创造价值？以下三条原则源自哈佛大学法学院谈判项目组的研究成果与教学实践。

1. 善用差异。

当谈判者遇到差异时，常将其视作障碍。实际上，差异往往是创造价值的契机。马克斯·H·巴泽曼（Max H. Bazerman）与唐·A·摩尔（Don A. Moore）在《管理者决策中的判断力》一书中指出：通过在不同偏好之间进行交换（即所谓“互投赞成票”），谈判者可在自己更看重的议题上获得更多收益，同时在自己较不看重的议题上做出让步。

例如，你可协商降低单位产品价格，作为加快付款、延长合同期限或扩大订单数量的交换条件。

差异不仅体现在偏好层面；还可借助双方对未来不同的预期，通过“或有合同”（contingent contracts）来创造价值。假设你正与承包商就住宅装修工程进行谈判，且你认为项目极难按约定工期完工。此时，你可提议：若如期完工，则支付较高价格；若延期，则支付较低价格。倘若承包商对其工期安排充满信心，便很可能欣然接受该条款。

2. 主动提问并分享信息。

谈判者常常陷入僵局，原因在于仅拘泥于对方明示的立场，例如冗长的交割周期或不可更改的交付日期。要突破这些卡点，一种有效方式是提出深思熟虑的问题，以挖掘对方立场背后的真实利益诉求。

例如，与其直接拒绝漫长的交割期，不如询问：“能否请您说明一下，为何需要这么长的时间？”对方的回答可能揭示出可协商的利益点。如果延迟确由合理约束所致，你或许愿意接受该安排，但要求更优惠的价格或其他补偿，以抵消由此带来的不便。

分享信息同样有助于打破僵局。当然，你绝不可透露自己的底线或任何可能削弱己方地位的敏感信息；但巴泽曼与摩尔建议，谈判者可适度分享自己愿意考虑的权衡选项。这种渐进式的信息披露，有助于激发对方的互惠回应，从而推动更具建设性的问题解决进程。

3. 同时就多项议题展开谈判。

许多谈判者误以为，逐项推进——先谈价格、再谈交付、最后谈服务条款——就是系统化做法。事实上，这种线性方式往往严重限制了价值创造的空间。

更有效的策略是多项议题同步谈判。首先应达成共识：在所有议题全部敲定前，任何单项协议均不具最终效力。这一思维模式能增强灵活性，也更容易识别跨议题的互利权衡。在此基础上，应提出反映你理想结果组合的“一揽子方案”，而非孤立地提出各项要求。

整合式谈判并非要求你为追求合作而牺牲自身利益；恰恰相反，它帮助双方更清晰地识别各自最珍视的价值所在，从而实现双赢。当你成功把“蛋糕”做大之后，你也就处于更有利的地位，去争取自己应得的那一份。
