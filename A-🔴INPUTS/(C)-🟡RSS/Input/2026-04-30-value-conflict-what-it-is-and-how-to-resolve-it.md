<img alt="Conflict Resolution" height="133" src="https://www.pon.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/images/posts/conflict-resolution.jpg" title="Conflict Resolution" width="200" /><br /><p>Some of our most heated negotiations and disputes stem from value conflict tied to our deepest convictions—our personal moral standards, our religious and political beliefs, and our family’s welfare. Because these issues touch on who we are and what we stand for, they can quickly elevate tensions and make compromise feel especially challenging. Understanding the nature of value conflict is an important first step toward navigating these emotionally charged negotiations more thoughtfully and effectively.</p>
<p>Consider these <a href="https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/conflict-resolution/conflict-resolution-scenarios-negotiating-values/">value conflict examples</a>:</p>
<ul>
<li>Business partners clash over the ethical standards they expect each other to uphold.</li>
<li>A negotiator refuses to do business with a potential counterpart she deems unsavory on moral grounds.</li>
<li>Parents bar their teenager from attending an event they think might be dangerous.</li>
</ul>
<p>These <a href="https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/conflict-resolution/types-conflict/">types of value conflict</a>, which highlight our norms, beliefs, and identities, can be incredibly difficult to resolve. When our most deeply held beliefs and principles are at stake, we often ratchet up value conflict out of a desire to be heard, refuse to make any concession that would appear to compromise our values, or categorically refuse to negotiate. Drawing on new research, we present three strategies for negotiating value-based conflict.</p>

<p><strong>1. Assess Whether the Value Is Truly Sacred</strong></p>
<p>Value conflict often arises because one or more of the parties involved consider a value to be sacred and nonnegotiable. In some cases, our values truly are sacred and not open to compromise. In other situations, however, our values turn out to be “pseudo-sacred”—that is, we are willing to negotiate them under certain conditions, notes Harvard Business School professor Max H. Bazerman.</p>
<p>For example, in one study, negotiators with little power were more likely to compromise on a seemingly sacred issue than were negotiators with greater power, Notre Dame University professor Ann E. Tenbrunsel and her colleagues found in <a href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1750-4716.2009.00040.x" rel="noopener" target="_blank">their research</a>. A lack of alternatives led low-power negotiators to become more flexible.</p>
<p>Before refusing to budge on an issue you deem sacred, try to envision an outcome that would allow you to abide by the <em>spirit</em> of your values even as you make concessions on the specifics. Similarly, if a counterpart insists a particular issue is sacred, you might make a proposal that honors their values while also bringing you closer to agreement.</p>
<p>This doesn’t mean putting price tags on your most cherished beliefs; rather, it means thinking creatively about how to meet broader goals. Imagine, for example, that you adamantly oppose your sibling’s intention to sell some of your late parents’ possessions. But what if you donated your share of the proceeds from the sale to a charity your parents supported? You might prefer this outcome and avoid a protracted value conflict.</p>
<p><strong>2. Offer a Concession on a Core Value</strong></p>
<p>You may be able to induce cooperation from a reluctant counterpart in a value conflict by making a difficult but symbolic concession on a key principle.</p>
<p>In a <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC1863499/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">2007 study</a>, Jeremy Ginges of the New School for Social Research and his team presented various proposals for resolving the Palestinian-Israeli conflict to citizens residing in the West Bank and Gaza: Jewish-Israeli settlers, Palestinian refugees, and Palestinian student supporters of Hamas. All three groups rejected proposals that would require their group to make a concession on a core issue in exchange for peace. And when each side was also offered significant economic aid, they were repulsed by the idea of trading their sacred values for cash.</p>
<p>However, when asked whether they would accept the peace deal if accompanied by a significant concession from the opposing side on one of <em>its</em> sacred values, all three groups became willing to negotiate. The Israeli settlers agreed to make concessions if Hamas accepted Israel’s right to exist. The Palestinian refugees grew more flexible if Israelis would relinquish their claim to the West Bank. And the Palestinian students became ready to bargain if the Israelis were willing to officially apologize for Palestinian suffering in the conflict.</p>
<p>When sacred values are at stake, we are likely to be offended by the suggestion that our support can be “bought.” Proposing a meaningful sacrifice on one of your own core values may demonstrate your seriousness, inspire reciprocation, and avoid a value conflict.</p>
<p><strong>3. Affirm the Other Side’s Positive Qualities</strong></p>
<p>In a value conflict, thinking about qualities you appreciate in your counterpart, such as trustworthiness or deep convictions, can help parties create value, researchers Fieke Harinck of Leiden University in the Netherlands and Daniel Druckman of George Mason University found in <a href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0022002715569774" rel="noopener" target="_blank">their research</a>.</p>
<p>This result dovetails with findings from University of California at Los Angeles professor Corinne Bendersky showing that negotiators can soften their counterpart’s firm stance on a seemingly sacred value by making statements that affirm the counterpart’s status, such as “I have a lot of respect for people like you who stand by their principles.” It seems an opponent’s affirmation of our status buffers us against the identity threat we’d suffer if we compromised on a core issue and makes us more open to <a href="https://www.pon.harvard.edu/tag/value-creation/">value creation</a> in the process.</p>

## 中文译文

我们一些最激烈、最胶着的谈判与争执，往往源于价值观冲突——这种冲突根植于我们最深层的信念：个人道德标准、宗教与政治信仰，以及对家庭福祉的关切。由于这些议题直接关乎“我们是谁”以及“我们所坚守的是什么”，它们极易迅速激化矛盾，使妥协显得尤为艰难。因此，理解价值观冲突的本质，是更审慎、更有效地应对这类情绪化谈判的重要第一步。

以下是一些价值观冲突的实例：

• 商业合作伙伴就彼此应遵守的道德标准发生分歧；  
• 一位谈判者基于道德判断，拒绝与她认为品行不端的潜在合作方开展业务；  
• 父母禁止子女参加一场他们认为可能存在危险的活动。

此类凸显我们行为规范、核心信念与身份认同的价值观冲突，往往极难化解。当人们最珍视的信念与原则受到挑战时，我们常常会本能地加剧冲突：或急于表达立场以求被听见，或坚决拒绝任何看似有损自身价值观的让步，甚至彻底拒绝进入谈判进程。基于最新研究成果，我们提出三种应对价值型冲突的策略。

1. 评估该价值观是否真正“不可让渡”  

价值观冲突常源于一方或多方将某一价值视为神圣不可侵犯、不容协商。在某些情况下，我们的价值观确实具有神圣性，无法妥协；但在另一些情境中，所谓“神圣价值”实为“伪神圣”——即在特定条件下，我们仍愿意对其进行协商。哈佛商学院教授马克斯·H·巴泽尔曼（Max H. Bazerman）指出，这一现象值得关注。

例如，圣母大学（Notre Dame University）教授安·E·滕布鲁内尔（Ann E. Tenbrunsel）及其同事的研究发现，在一项实验中，权力较弱的谈判者比权力较强的谈判者更可能在表面看似神圣的问题上做出妥协。缺乏替代选项，促使低权力方展现出更强的灵活性。

因此，在因认定某议题“神圣不可侵犯”而拒绝让步之前，不妨先尝试构想一种结果：它既能让你在精神层面恪守自身价值观，又允许你在具体细节上作出适当让步。同样地，若对方坚称某一议题对其而言神圣不可触碰，你也可主动提出一项既尊重其价值观、又能推动双方达成共识的方案。

这并非给最珍视的信念明码标价，而是以创造性思维思考如何实现更宏大的目标。举例而言，你可能强烈反对兄弟姐妹出售已故父母遗留的部分物品；但倘若将你应得的售物所得悉数捐赠给你父母生前长期支持的慈善机构，你是否会更乐于接受这一结果？如此一来，或许就能避免一场旷日持久的价值观冲突。

2. 在核心价值上主动让步  

在价值观冲突中，若你能就一项关键原则作出艰难却富有象征意义的让步，便有可能促使原本抗拒合作的对方转而配合。

在2007年的一项研究中，纽约社会研究新学院（New School for Social Research）的杰里米·金吉斯（Jeremy Ginges）及其团队，向约旦河西岸与加沙地带的居民——包括犹太裔以色列定居者、巴勒斯坦难民，以及支持哈马斯的巴勒斯坦大学生——提出了若干旨在解决巴以冲突的方案。结果发现，这三个群体均拒绝接受任何要求本方就核心议题让步以换取和平的提议；即便额外提供丰厚经济援助，他们亦反感将“神圣价值”兑换成金钱的想法。

然而，当研究人员询问：如果对方愿就其一项神圣价值作出重大让步，你们是否愿意接受该和平协议？所有三类群体均转而表示愿意展开谈判。具体而言：以色列定居者表示，若哈马斯正式承认以色列的合法存在权，他们愿意让步；巴勒斯坦难民则表示，若以色列放弃对约旦河西岸的主权主张，他们将更具灵活性；而巴勒斯坦大学生则表示，只要以色列官方就冲突中巴勒斯坦民众所遭受的苦难正式道歉，他们便准备进入谈判。

当神圣价值面临考验时，我们极易因“被收买”的暗示而感到被冒犯。此时，若你能主动就自身一项核心价值作出真诚且分量十足的牺牲，不仅可彰显诚意，更能激发对方的互惠意愿，从而有效规避价值观冲突。

3. 肯定对方的积极特质  

荷兰莱顿大学（Leiden University）研究员菲克·哈林克（Fieke Harinck）与美国乔治梅森大学（George Mason University）教授丹尼尔·德拉克曼（Daniel Druckman）的研究表明：在价值观冲突中，若能主动关注并提及对方身上你所欣赏的品质（如可信度、坚定信念等），有助于双方共同创造价值。

这一发现与加州大学洛杉矶分校（UCLA）教授科琳·本德斯基（Corinne Bendersky）的研究结论相呼应。她的研究表明，谈判者可通过肯定对方地位的表述（例如：“我非常敬佩像您这样始终坚守原则的人”），软化对方在表面神圣议题上的强硬立场。看来，当对手公开肯定我们的身份地位时，便能在一定程度上缓冲我们因让步于核心议题而产生的身份威胁感，从而让我们更愿意在谈判过程中探索价值共创的可能性。
