<h3>&#8220;These are the times in life — when nothing happens — but in quietness the soul expands.&#8221;</h3>
<hr />
<p><a href="https://www.amazon.com/exec/obidos/ASIN/0819552933/braipick-20" target="_blank"><img alt="Wilderness, Solitude, and Creativity: Artist and Philosopher Rockwell Kent&#8217;s Century-Old Meditations on Art and Life During Seven Months on a Small Alaskan Island" class="cover alignright size-medium" height="439" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/rockwellkent_wilderness1936.jpg?fit=320%2C439&amp;ssl=1" width="320" /></a></p><p>Not often &#8212; a handful of times in a lifetime, if you are lucky &#8212; you come upon a work of thought and feeling &#8212; a book, a painting, a song &#8212; that becomes a fountain to which you return again and again, and which returns you to your life refreshed each time.  </p>
<p>For me, <em>The Little Prince</em> has been one, and <em>Leaves of Grass</em>, and <em>I Put a Spell on You</em>, and <em>Spiegel im Spiegel</em>. <a href="https://www.amazon.com/Wilderness-Adventure-Alaska_Including-Extensive-Unpublished/dp/0819552933/?tag=braipick-20" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><strong><em>Wilderness</em></strong></a> (<a href="https://www.worldcat.org/title/wilderness-a-journal-of-quiet-adventure-in-alaska-including-extensive-hitherto-unpublished-passages-from-the-original-journal/oclc/832616530&#038;referer=brief_results" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><em>public library</em></a>) by the painter, printmaker, and philosopher <strong>Rockwell Kent</strong> (June 21, 1882&ndash;March 13, 1971) is another. (Ample gratitude to <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2021/08/14/analogia-george-dyson/">George Dyson</a> for bringing this soul-slaking treasure into my life.)</p>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_75930"><a href="https://society6.com/product/moonlight-winter-by-rockwell-kent-1940_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-75930" height="567" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/rockwellkent_moonlightwinter.jpg?resize=680%2C567&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text"><em>Moonlight, Winter</em> by Rockwell Kent. <p>In the last days of August, in the last months of the world&#8217;s first global war, while the Spanish Flu pandemic was savaging civilization, Kent arrived on a small island in Resurrection Bay off the coast of Alaska, searching for the ultimate. He was thirty-six, dispirited and destitute, as passionate about his art and as pained by the world&#8217;s indifference to it as Walt Whitman had been when he self-published <em>Leaves of Grass</em> at that same age, from that same precarious place, intimate with the same depths of depression, buoyed by the same reverence for life. </p>
<p>Drawing on that experience, Kent would later formulate the closest thing to a personal credo:</p>
<blockquote><p>Often I think that however much I draw or paint, or however well, I am not an artist as art is generally understood. The abstract is meaningless to me save as a fragment of the whole, which is life itself&#8230; It is the ultimate which concerns me, and all physical, all material things are but an expression of it&#8230; We are part and parcel of the big plan of things. We are simply instruments recording in different measure our particular portion of the infinite. And what we absorb of it makes for character, and what we give forth, for expression.</p></blockquote>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_75928"><a href="https://society6.com/product/vision-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-75928" height="606" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/rockwellkent_vision.jpg?resize=680%2C606&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text"><em>The Vision</em> by Rockwell Kent, 1919. (Available as <a href="https://society6.com/product/vision-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">a print</a> and as <a href="https://society6.com/brainpicker/cards?sort=new?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">stationery cards</a>.)</figcaption></figure>
<p>Kent arrived at this uncommon life in art via an uncommon path. His parents had pressured him to channel his talent into a practical, profitable career in form and function, but he had dropped out of Columbia University&#8217;s architecture program to devote himself to the work of form and feeling, moving to a rugged island off the coast of Maine. He built himself a small house there and spent his days in solitude &#8212; reading Emerson and Tolstoy, and painting; laboring as a lobsterman, and painting. Immersed in <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2022/02/04/universe-in-verse-animated-episode-1/">Haeckel&#8217;s inception of ecology</a>, he grew enchanted with the interwoven life of nature; immersed in <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2021/05/25/thoreau-walden-nature/">Thoreau&#8217;s journals</a>, he absorbed the will &#8220;to live deliberately&#8221; in wild places where he could find and nurture his inner wilderness &#8212; those lush and desolate landscapes of the soul, from which all art is born.  </p>
<p>So it is that, in his late twenties, Rockwell Kent voyaged to Newfoundland in the hope of establishing a communal art school with a friend in the untrammeled northern wilderness. The hope crumbled against reality, but the Great North cast a permanent enchantment. He returned four years later, in 1914, this time with his wife and three children, just as the world was coming unworlded by the Great War. </p>
<p>In a small-town community where the notion of an artist was alien and suspect, the large-spirited, liberal-minded Kent was soon accused of being a German spy. Driven away, the family had to make the long voyage back &#8212; Kathleen pregnant with their fourth child, the other three ill with whooping cough. </p>
<p>But the northern wilderness kept calling to the artist&#8217;s soul: </p>
<blockquote><p>I crave snow-topped mountains, dreary wastes, and the cruel Northern sea with its hard horizons at the edge of the world where infinite space begins. Here skies are clearer and deeper and, for the greater wonders they reveal, a thousand times more eloquent of the eternal mystery than those of softer lands.</p></blockquote>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_75929"><a href="https://society6.com/product/the-star-lighter-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-75929" height="597" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/rockwellkent_star-lighter.jpg?resize=680%2C597&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text"><em>The Star-Lighter</em> by Rockwell Kent, 1919. (Available as <a href="https://society6.com/product/the-star-lighter-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">a print</a> and as <a href="https://society6.com/brainpicker/cards?sort=new?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">stationery cards</a>.)</figcaption></figure>
<p>Four years later, at the peak of his struggle to support the growing family, Rockwell Kent returned to the North in the hope of resuscitating his spirit and his ability to, quite simply, go on. </p>
<p>“Never did I enter upon any course with such a sense of necessity, of duty, as drives me into this Alaska trip,&#8221; he told Kathleen. </p>
<p>Fatherless himself since the age of five, having inherited nothing more than his father&#8217;s silver flute, which he carried everywhere, he voyaged to the Far North with his nine-year-old son, also named Rockwell, and his silver flute. &#8220;We came to this new land, a boy and a man,&#8221; he wrote, &#8220;entirely on a dreamer’s search; having had vision of a Northern Paradise, we came to find it.&#8221; </p>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_75938"><a href="https://society6.com/product/untitled-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-75938" height="538" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/rockwellkent2.jpg?resize=680%2C538&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text"><em>Untitled</em> by Rockwell Kent. <p>They came with one duffle bag stuffed with the warmest clothes they owned and one heavy trunk full of books, paints, and provisions. Sprawling across three diary pages, Kent&#8217;s inventory includes these essentials:</p>
<blockquote>
<ul>
<li>8 lbs. chocolate</li>
<li>1 gal. peanut butter</li>
<li>4 pots</li>
<li>2 pillows</li>
<li>10 lbs. lima beans</li>
<li>10 lbs. white beans</li>
<li>100 lbs. potatoes</li>
<li>1 broom</li>
<li>6 lemons</li>
<li>6 agate cups</li>
<li>4 agate plates</li>
<li>4 agate bowls</li>
<li>5 lbs. salt</li>
<li>6 Ivory soap</li>
<li>2 cans dried eggs</li>
<li>1 tea kettle</li>
<li>12 candles</li>
</ul>
</blockquote>
<p>These they brought to Fox Island, welcomed there by an elderly Swede named Olson, who had arrived long ago prospecting for gold; having failed to find any, and having been dismissed by the mainland townspeople as a &#8220;crazy old man, he had made a home on the small and isolated island, tending to two pairs of blue foxes and four goats. Kent found Olson to be &#8220;a kind-hearted, genial old man with a vast store of knowledge and true wisdom,&#8221; a man of &#8220;deep experience, strong, brave, generous and gentle like a child,&#8221; a &#8220;keen philosopher [who] by his critical observations gives his discourse a fine dignity.&#8221;</p>
<p>Father and son set about converting Olson&#8217;s goat-house into a home. On either side of the log cabin, Kent &#8212; an excellent carpenter from a young age &#8212; built two long wall-to-wall shelves: one to hold their provisions, the other for paints, toys, clothes, and the flute. In the far corner, he built a bookshelf for their miniature library &#8212; sustenance for mind and spirit, as vital as the canned goods they had carried across the landmass and rowed across the icy strait of Arctic waters. Among the books were <em>The Iliad</em> and <em>The Odyssey</em>; <em>Robinson Crusoe</em> and the fairy tales of Hans Christian Andersen; a book of Indian philosophy and a literary history of Ireland; a natural history of the ocean and a basic medical handbook; William Blake&#8217;s poems and <em>Life of Blake</em> &#8212; the biography with which Anne Gilchrist had wrested Blake from obscurity a generation earlier to establish him as a creative icon for generations, celebrated by Patti Smith as <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2018/11/28/willim-blake-poems-patti-smith/">&#8220;the loom’s loom, spinning the fiber of revelation,”</a> and casting upon Kent a spell of &#8220;intense and illuminating fervor.&#8221;</p>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_75922"><a href="https://society6.com/product/the-cabin-window-by-rockwell-kent-1918_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-75922" height="617" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/rockwellkent_cabin.jpg?resize=680%2C617&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text"><em>Cabin Window</em> by Rockwell Kent, 1919. <p>Despite these marginal comforts, the cabin remained a ramshackle structure invaded by the elemental cold. Kent tried calking the gaping openings between the logs with dried moss, but the moss never managed to dry enough for insulation under the interminable rain. Indeed, from the moment they set foot on Fox Island, father and son waded into a world ruled by rain, an <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2015/05/20/anne-sexton-love-poems-song-for-a-lady-ohara-hale/">Anne Sexton kind of rain</a>. In their first seventeen days, a single cloudless sunrise greeted them. &#8220;It will be a strange life without the dear, warm sun!&#8221; Kent lamented in his journal. The absence of the sun &#8212; like any absences of cherished warmth and radiance &#8212; made its rare returns all the dearer, aglow with ecstasy:</p>
<blockquote><p>Ah, the evenings are beautiful here and the early mornings, when the days are fair! No sudden springing of the sun into the sky and out again at night; but so gradual, so circuitous a coming and a going that nearly the whole day is twilight and the quiet rose color of morning and evening seems almost to meet at noon. We glance through our tiny western window at sunrise and see beyond the bay the many ranges of mountains, from the somber ones at the water’s edge to the distant glacier and snowcapped peaks, lit by the far-off sun with the loveliest light imaginable.</p></blockquote>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_75920"><a href="https://society6.com/product/day-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-75920" height="902" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/rockwellkent_day.jpg?resize=680%2C902&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text"><em>Day</em> by Rockwell Kent, 1919. (Available as <a href="https://society6.com/product/day-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">a print</a> and as <a href="https://society6.com/brainpicker/cards?sort=new?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">stationery cards</a>.)</figcaption></figure>
<p>But by early November, the unremitting gloom began eclipsing the sparse ecstasies of light:</p>
<blockquote><p>Endlessly, day after day, the journal goes on recording a dreary monotony of rain and cloud. Who has ever dwelt so entirely alone that the most living things in all the universe about are wind and rain and snow?</p></blockquote>
<p>As the days grew shorter and shorter and the weeks unspooled into months, the weather became a sort of teacher. In an entry penned the day after the deepest snow and the coldest cold snap on the record &#8212; &#8220;the cold very many degrees below zero&#8221; &#8212; Kent exclaims in the diary: &#8220;Such mild weather!&#8221; It was still far below freezing, but not nearly as far as the previous day &#8212; a study in the delight of contrasts, the same contrasts that give shape and texture to art and life. </p>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_75921"><a href="https://society6.com/product/night-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-75921" height="908" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/rockwellkent_night.jpg?resize=680%2C908&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text"><em>Night</em> by Rockwell Kent, 1919. (Available as <a href="https://society6.com/product/night-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">a print</a> and as <a href="https://society6.com/brainpicker/cards?sort=new?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">stationery cards</a>.)</figcaption></figure>
<p>Eventually, he arrives at a sort of existential acceptance, as applicable to the elements as to the ever-shifting weather system that is life itself:</p>
<blockquote><p>I have learned to expect nothing of the weather but what it gives us.</p></blockquote>
<p>We create our own weather, he intimates in an entry from the clutch of February:</p>
<blockquote><p>A little snow, a little rain, but altogether a pleasant day. It’s always pleasant when I paint well.</p></blockquote>
<p>Throughout the journal, Kent interpolates so naturally between the elemental and the existential, between observation and contemplation &#8212; nowhere more so than in this reflection on the totality of his wilderness experience:</p>
<blockquote><p>These are the times in life &#8212; when nothing happens &#8212; but in quietness the soul expands.</p></blockquote>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_75919"><a href="https://society6.com/product/lone-man-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-75919" height="859" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/rockwellkent_loneman.jpg?resize=680%2C859&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text"><em>Lone Man</em> by Rockwell Kent, 1919. (Available as <a href="https://society6.com/product/lone-man-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">a print</a> and as <a href="https://society6.com/brainpicker/cards?sort=new?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">stationery cards</a>.)</figcaption></figure>
<p>Kent soon finds a new kind of liberation in the quiet expanse &#8212; freedom not only from the bustling tumults of the warring present, but from the totality of any collective human culture, which can so ossify identity and become a straitjacket for the soul:</p>
<blockquote><p>So little do we feel ourselves related, here in this place, to any one time or to any civilization that at a thought we and our world become whom and what we please.</p></blockquote>
<p>Father and child become, in the way <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2019/10/21/iris-murdoch-unselfing/">only art and nature afford us</a>, unselfed &#8212; not persons, scarred with identities and ideologies, but fields of grateful awareness. They go berry-picking along the coast of Resurrection, skate on the pond &#8220;frozen hard and thick,&#8221; and watch the killer whales play in the cove by their cabin, &#8220;their terrible, mysterious, black arms that beat the water with a sound like cannon.&#8221; </p>
<p>Recording these encounters with the elemental, Kent&#8217;s diary entries read like prose poetry, as any fully attentive and pure-hearted observation of nature always does &#8212; deeply affecting yet unaffected, fresh from the source. One mid-October evening, after quoting from memory a lullaby verse by a German poet born 100 years earlier, Kent exults:</p>
<blockquote><p>The night is beautiful beyond thought. All the bay is flooded with moonlight and in that pale glow the snowy mountains appear whiter than snow itself. The full moon is almost straight above us, and shining through the tree tops into our clearing makes the old stumps quite lovely with its quiet light. And the forest around is as black as the abyss.</p></blockquote>
<p>The following evening, a wholly different guise of beauty:</p>
<blockquote><p>To-night the sun set in the utmost splendor and left in its wake blazing, fire-red clouds in a sky of luminous green.</p></blockquote>
<p>And the following:</p>
<blockquote><p>The moon has risen and illuminates the mountain tops &#8212; but we and all our cove are still in the deep shadow of the night. It is most dramatic; the spruces about us deepen the shadow to black while above them the stone faces of the mountain glisten and the sky has the brightness of a kind of day.</p></blockquote>
<p>In another entry:</p>
<blockquote><p>From our feet the cliff dropped in a V-shaped divide straight down to the green ocean; and at its base the ground swell curled, broke white and eddied. The jagged mountains across shone white against black clouds, &#8212; what peaks! huge and sharp like the teeth of the Fenris-Wolf.</p></blockquote>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_75925"><a href="https://society6.com/product/victory-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-75925" height="617" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/rockwellkent_victory.jpg?resize=680%2C617&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text"><em>Victory</em> by Rockwell Kent, 1919. (Available as <a href="https://society6.com/product/victory-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">a print</a> and as <a href="https://society6.com/brainpicker/cards?sort=new?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">stationery cards</a>.)</figcaption></figure>
<p>It is impossible to place oneself amid such staggering beauty &#8212; &#8220;it is so beautiful here at times that it seems hard to bear,&#8221; Kent writes &#8212; and not wish to reverence it, to channel it, to magnify it and add to the world&#8217;s store of wonder with one&#8217;s own creations. And so, one cold October day seven weeks after alighting to Fox Island, Kent records:</p>
<blockquote><p>We came home and had a good dinner. I cut more wood and at last, after one month here on the island, I PAINTED. It was a stupid sketch, but no matter, I’ve begun!</p></blockquote>
<p>He feels &#8220;the goddess Inspiration returning&#8221; and soon the floodgates of his creative force rush open: </p>
<blockquote><p>After the morning’s wood cutting I worked hard on my pictures. I’m now at last fully launched upon my work with small pictures going well. That’s both a relief and a concern to me. From now on my mind can never be quite free.</p></blockquote>
<p>In a passage that captures every true artist&#8217;s savage and restless devotion to their art &#8212; the kind Beethoven conveyed in his <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2017/05/18/beethoven-emilie-letter/">letter of advice</a> to a little girl longing to be an artist, the kind at the heart of Martha Graham&#8217;s exquisite notion of <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2015/10/02/martha-graham-creativity-divine-dissatisfaction/">&#8220;divine dissatisfaction&#8221;</a> &#8212; Kent writes one October day two months after his arrival:</p>
<blockquote><p>Today was a day of hard work for me. I cut wood, baked bread and painted on three canvasses&#8230; Over to-day’s painting I’m filled with pride; it will be equalled by to-morrow’s despair over the very same pictures.</p></blockquote>
<p>He becomes a channel for the majesty around him, seeing in it a reflection of his own worldview, mirroring it back to the world in the paintings nature draws out of him:</p>
<blockquote><p>A wonderfully beautiful day with a raging northwest wind. I must sometime honor the northwest wind in a great picture as the embodiment of clean, strong, exuberant life, the joy of every young thing, bearing energy on its wings and the will to triumph.</p></blockquote>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_75931"><a href="https://society6.com/product/the-north-wind-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-75931" height="834" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/rockwellkent_northwind.jpg?resize=680%2C834&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text"><em>North Wind</em> by Rockwell Kent, 1919. (Available as <a href="https://society6.com/product/the-north-wind-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">a print</a> and as <a href="https://society6.com/brainpicker/cards?sort=new?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">stationery cards</a>.)</figcaption></figure>
<p>Immersed in &#8220;the profound and characteristic winter silence of the out-of-doors,&#8221; a grateful gladness slips over him each time the wind parts the curtain of clouds:</p>
<blockquote><p>It is no little thing to have one’s work on a day like this out under such a blue sky, by the foaming green sea and the fairy mountains.</p></blockquote>
<p>From the outset, Kent decides that if his art is to ever be shown in civilization, the exhibition must be titled &#8220;Paintings of Paradise&#8221; &#8212; an homage to his love for his son and for his son&#8217;s love of the wilderness: &#8220;I know nothing in all life more beautiful than the perfect belief of Rockwell in his Paradise here,&#8221; Kent writes in one entry. It is a paradise build of what his literary hero Hermann Hesse, writing in the same era on a different landmass in a wholly different landscape, called <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2017/03/06/hermann-hesse-little-joys-my-belief/">&#8220;the little joys&#8221;</a> &#8212; those smallest atoms of aliveness. Kent records:</p>
<blockquote><p>Mornings we get up together and go through a set of Dr. Sargent’s exercises, do them with great energy. Then we go naked out-of-doors&#8230; No matter what the weather is we go calmly out into it, lie down in the drift, look up into the sky, and then scrub ourselves with snow. It’s the finest bath in the world.</p></blockquote>
<p>One day, looking around the ramshackle goat-house that is now his home, filled with books and wind, filled with a man&#8217;s paintings and a child&#8217;s love, Kent observes:</p>
<blockquote><p>I don’t see why people need better homes than this.</p></blockquote>
<p>In an entry from the peak of winter, he contemplates how such simple life in harsh conditions can so salve and enlarge his creative spirit:</p>
<blockquote><p>We have&#8230; turned out of the beaten, crowded way and come to stand face to face with that infinite and unfathomable thing which is the wilderness; and here we have found OURSELVES &#8212; for the wilderness is nothing else. It is a kind of living mirror that gives back as its own all and only all that the imagination of a man brings to it&#8230; and if we have not shuddered at the emptiness of the abyss and fled from its loneliness, it is because of the wealth of our own souls that filled the void with imagery, warmed it, and gave it speech and understanding.</p></blockquote>
<p>Punctuating this surrender to the grandeur of nature and soul are various quotidian tragicomedies. Violent wind sweeps in through the cracks in the cabin and powders Kent&#8217;s drawing table with snow. The cold grows so ferocious that his fountain pen and paint freeze solid, the foxes&#8217; food freezes solid, the water pails freeze solid ten feet from the booming stove. One of Olson&#8217;s goats &#8212; &#8220;foolish-faced Angoras&#8221; &#8212; eats the broom, then breaks into the house, leaving &#8220;boxes, pails, sacks of grain, cans, rope, tools, all lie piled in confusion about the floor.&#8221; Such happenings only foment Kent&#8217;s deep-souled reflections on life:</p>
<blockquote><p>Where little happens and the gamut of expression is narrow, life is still full of joy and sorrow. You’re stirred by simple happenings in a quiet world.</p></blockquote>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_75939"><a href="https://society6.com/product/bowspirit-by-rockwell-kent-1930_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-75939" height="543" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/rockwellkent_bowsprit1.jpg?resize=680%2C543&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text"><em>Bowsprit</em> by Rockwell Kent, 1930. (Available as <a href="https://society6.com/product/bowspirit-by-rockwell-kent-1930_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">a print</a> and as <a href="https://society6.com/brainpicker/cards?sort=new?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">stationery cards</a>.)</figcaption></figure>
<p>That simplicity becomes a portal to immensity. In consonance with poet Elizabeth Bishop&#8217;s insistence on <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2016/02/08/elizabeth-bishop-solitude/">why everyone should experience at least one long period of extreme solitude in life</a>, and with his contemporary Hermann Hesse&#8217;s insight into <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2019/01/15/hermann-hesse-solitude-suffering-destiny/">the destiny-sculpting value of hardship and solitude</a>, Kent writes:</p>
<blockquote><p>These days are wonderful but they are terrible. It is thrilling&#8230; to reflect that we are absolutely cut off from all mankind, that we cannot, in this raging sea, return to the world nor the world come to us. Barriers must secure your isolation in order that you may experience the full significance of it. The romance of an adventure hangs upon slender threads. A banana peeling on a mountain top tames the wilderness. Much of the glory of this Alaska is in the knowledge I have that the next bay &#8212; which I may never choose to enter &#8212; is uninhabited, that beyond those mountains across the water is a vast region that no man has ever trodden, a terrible ice-bound wilderness.</p></blockquote>
<p>And yet, as much as nature might gladden human nature, it is our nature also to long for love and connection with our fellow beings. After twenty weeks of such extreme isolation, in an entry penned in the pit of winter, in a sentiment acutely relatable to any twenty-first-century person who has anguished to see an email go unanswered or to watch the three dots on their phone blink and disappear, Kent writes:</p>
<blockquote><p>It is terribly depressing to have your heart set upon that mail that doesn’t come.</p></blockquote>
<p>His suicidal depression returns:</p>
<blockquote><p>I feel like making no record of these days. I take pleasure only in their quick passage.</p></blockquote>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_75923"><a href="https://society6.com/product/go-to-bed-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-75923" height="620" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/rockwellkent_insomnia.jpg?resize=680%2C620&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text"><em>Go to Bed</em> by Rockwell Kent, 1919. (Available as <a href="https://society6.com/product/go-to-bed-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">a print</a> and as <a href="https://society6.com/brainpicker/cards?sort=new?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">stationery cards</a>.)</figcaption></figure>
<p>And then, just like that &#8212; <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2017/09/27/having-it-out-with-melancholy-jane-kenyon-amanda-palmer/">like it always does and we always forget it does</a> &#8212; daybreak comes for the dark night of the soul, the curtain of depression open, and he grows porous to beauty again, wakeful to the light of aliveness:</p>
<blockquote><p>The day has been glorious, mild, fair, with snow everywhere even on the trees. The snow sticks to the mountain tops even to the steepest, barest peaks painting them all a spotless, dazzling white. It’s a marvelous sight&#8230; There never was so beautiful a land as this!</p></blockquote>
<p>Eventually, confusions about time arise. His only timepiece &#8212; a dollar watch handed down to Olson by its previous owner &#8212; stops working. Father and son begin living by animal instinct: They rise at daybreak, have a prompt breakfast &#8212; always the same: oatmeal, cocoa bread, and peanut butter &#8212; then eat only when hungry as they immerse themselves in the day&#8217;s work and in the living world around them, noticing, noticing, and turning those noticings into art; in the evenings, Kent plays the flute for little Rockwell and reads to him (but not stories about kings and queens, which the boy tells his father he dislikes because &#8220;they’re always marrying and that kind of stuff&#8221;), until they &#8220;go to bed without any notion of the hour.&#8221; A typical entry reads:</p>
<blockquote><p>Hard, hard at work, little play, not too much sleep. The wind blows ceaselessly. Rockwell is forever good, &#8212; industrious, kind, and happy. He reads now quite freely from any book. Drawing has become a natural and regular occupation for him, almost a recreation &#8212; for he can draw in both a serious and a humorous vein. At this moment he’s waiting in bed for some music and another Andersen fairy tale.</p></blockquote>
<p>With time so elusive, they lose track of the date. There are practical consequences: The steamer to and from Seward &#8212; the “New York of the Pacific” &#8212; runs on a spare and strict schedule, on which they rely for their mail and provisions. There are poetic consequences, too: Unsure when to celebrate little Rockwell&#8217;s tenth birthday, they designate a best-guess day, on which Kent begins teaching his son to sing and presents him with his sole, precious present &#8212; &#8220;a cheap child’s edition&#8221; of a popular natural history encyclopedia. It so delights the boy with its depictions of his beloved wild animals that he decides, a generation before Borges, to begin writing and drawing an encyclopedia of imaginary beasts. </p>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_75927"><a href="https://society6.com/product/zarathustra-and-his-playmates-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-75927" height="541" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/rockwellkent_zarathustra.jpg?resize=680%2C541&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text"><em>Zarathustra and His Friends</em> by Rockwell Kent, 1919. (Available as <a href="https://society6.com/product/zarathustra-and-his-playmates-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">a print</a> and as <a href="https://society6.com/brainpicker/cards?sort=new?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">stationery cards</a>.)</figcaption></figure>
<p>Kent grows acutely aware of how these spare gladnesses &#8212; books and nature, freedom and love &#8212; are the fundaments of life, and all the rest is noise. Something quickens in him under the conditions of this new life &#8212; so spartan, so primal &#8212; and deconditions the habits of mind by which civilization bridles the spirit: </p>
<blockquote><p>Here in the supreme simplicity of life amid these mountains the spirit laughs at man’s concern with the form of Art, with new expression because the old is outworn! It is man’s own poverty of vision yielding him nothing, so that to save himself he must trick out in new garb the old, old commonplaces, or exalt to be material for art the hitherto discarded trivialities of the mind.</p></blockquote>
<p>There are days too short and dark to paint, too bleak to access the aliveness from which art springs &#8212; days when &#8220;the spirit didn’t work.&#8221; But there are also days, rosaries of them, that consecrate Kent&#8217;s painting with a state of total flow:</p>
<blockquote><p>It is weeks since I have stopped my work even for a walk. In this “out-of-doors life” I see little of out-of-doors.</p></blockquote>
<p>Five months into this Fox Island life, having &#8220;struck a fine stride,&#8221; Kent settles into a peculiar creative routine:</p>
<blockquote><p>During the day I paint out-of-doors from nature by way of fixing the forms and above all the color of the out-of-doors in my mind. Then after dark I go into a trance for a while with Rockwell subdued into absolute silence. I lie down or sit with closed eyes until I “see” a composition, &#8212; then I make a quick note of it or maybe give an hour’s time to perfecting the arrangement on a small scale. Then when that’s done I’m care free. Rockwell and I play cards for half an hour, I get supper, he goes to bed.</p></blockquote>
<p>Again and again, it is nature &#8212; so immediate, so alive, so numinous &#8212; that becomes the portal to this trance, leaving him with a magnified capacity for art and a clarified lens on life:</p>
<blockquote><p>One night, one midnight out on the black waters of a Newfoundland harbor, the million stars above, and on the wretched vessel’s deck the horde of half-drunk, soul-starved men saying their passionate farewells, &#8212; on the dull plain of their life a flash of lightning revealed an abyss; &#8212; this night on the still, dark cove of Resurrection Bay, rimmed with wild mountains and the wilderness, strong men about you, mad, loosened speech and winged, prophetic vision, &#8212; God! but sane daylight seeing seems to touch but the white, hard surface of where life is hidden.</p></blockquote>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_75926"><a href="https://society6.com/product/superman-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-75926" height="839" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/rockwellkent_superman.jpg?resize=680%2C839&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text"><em>Superman</em> by Rockwell Kent, 1919. (Available as <a href="https://society6.com/product/superman-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">a print</a> and as <a href="https://society6.com/brainpicker/cards?sort=new?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">stationery cards</a>.)</figcaption></figure>
<p>And so, seven months into his search for the hiding-place of life, Kent writes:</p>
<blockquote><p>This beautiful adventure of ours has come to an end. The enchantment of it has been complete; it has possessed us to the very last. How long such happiness could hold, such quiet life continue to fill up the full measure of human desires only a long experience could teach. The still, deep cup of the wilderness is potent with wisdom. Only to have tasted it is to have moved a lifetime forward to a finer youth&#8230; We have learned what we want and are therefore wise. As graduates in wisdom we return from the university of the wilderness.</p></blockquote>
<p>On March 18 &#8212; their last day in the wilderness, and the last days of the world&#8217;s first winter after the end of the war &#8212; Kent writes:</p>
<blockquote><p>Fox Island will soon become in our memories like a dream or vision, a remote experience too wonderful, for the full liberty we knew there and the deep peace, to be remembered or believed in as a real experience in life. It was for us life as it should be, serene and wholesome; love &#8212; but no hate, faith without disillusionment&#8230; Ah God, &#8212; and now the world again!</p></blockquote>
<p>But as he reentered the world, with its falsehoods and human ferocities, Kent carried the wilderness with him, its indelible imprint on his soul. Looking back on his time in Alaska, he wrote:</p>
<blockquote><p>In living and recording these experiences I have sensed a fresh unfolding of the mystery of life. I have found wisdom, and this new wisdom must in some degree have won its way into my work.</p></blockquote>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_75924"><a href="https://society6.com/product/woman-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-75924" height="791" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/rockwellkent_woman.jpg?resize=680%2C791&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text"><em>Woman</em> by Rockwell Kent, 1919. (Available as <a href="https://society6.com/product/woman-by-rockwell-kent-1919_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">a print</a> and as <a href="https://society6.com/brainpicker/cards?sort=new?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">stationery cards</a>.)</figcaption></figure>
<p>And indeed it did. The two New York exhibitions of his paintings that followed his return from Alaska were artistically and financially triumphal, sparking a new chapter of solvency for him and Kathleen, and liberating him at last to devote himself wholly to art. Timed with the second exhibition, the publication of his Alaska journal was heralded by England&#8217;s most esteemed culture magazine as &#8220;the most remarkable book to come out of America since <em>Leaves of Grass</em>.&#8221; (An epoch earlier, the English &#8212; much thanks to Anne Gilchrist&#8217;s <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2019/03/05/anne-gilchrist-walt-whitman-letters/">impassioned advocacy</a> &#8212; had been early to recognize Whitman&#8217;s genius when his own country derided and dismissed him.) </p>
<p>When the first exhibition of his Alaska drawings was being mounted, the gallery engaged one of New York&#8217;s preeminent art critics to compose the introduction for the catalogue. He wrote to Kent to learn more about how this time in the wilderness shaped his artistic practice. Kent responded with a letter so exquisite, so vibrant with his authentic spirit, that it was printed as the introduction instead. In it, he wrote:</p>
<blockquote><p>It has always been hard for me to understand myself, to know why I work and love and live. Yet it is fortunate that such matters find a way of caring for themselves. I came to Alaska because I love the North. I crave snow-topped mountains, dreary wastes, and the cruel Northern sea with its hard horizons at the edge of the world where infinite space begins. Here skies are clearer and deeper and, for the greater wonders they reveal, a thousand times more eloquent of the eternal mystery than those of softer lands.</p></blockquote>
<p>While elsewhere in New York <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2018/08/03/the-universe-in-verse-natalie-batalha-edna-st-vincent-millay/">Edna St. Vincent Millay</a> was composing her now-iconic sonnet that begins with &#8220;My candle burns at both ends,&#8221; to be published months later, Kent reflects on the allure of the Great North&#8217;s elemental brutality, on the magnetic misery in the &#8220;gloom of the long and lonely winter nights,&#8221; and writes:</p>
<blockquote><p>Always I have fought and worked and played with a fierce energy, and always as a man of flesh and blood and surging spirit. I have burned the candle at both ends and can only wonder that there has been left even a slender taper glow for art.</p>
<p>And so this sojourn in the wilderness is in no sense an artist’s junket in search of picturesque material for brush or pencil, but the fight to freedom of a man who detests the petty quarrels and bitterness of the crowded world &#8212; the pilgrimage of a philosopher in quest of Happiness! But the wilderness is what man brings to it, no more. If little Rockwell and I can live in these vast silences beside the heartless ocean, perched high up on the peak of the earth with the wind all about us, if we can stand here and not flee from the terror of emptiness, it is because the wealth of our own souls warms the mountains and sea, and peoples the great desolate spaces. For the time we look into ourselves and are not afraid. We find here life, true life &#8212; life rich, resplendent, and full of love. We have learned not to fear destiny but to live for the heaven that can be made upon earth.</p></blockquote>
<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" id="attachment_75932"><a href="https://society6.com/product/untitled-by-rockwell-kent_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-75932" height="992" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/rockwellkent1.jpg?resize=680%2C992&#038;ssl=1" width="680" /></a><figcaption class="wp-caption-text"><em>Untitled</em> by Rockwell Kent, 1919. (Available as <a href="https://society6.com/product/untitled-by-rockwell-kent_print?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">a print</a> and as <a href="https://society6.com/brainpicker/cards?sort=new?curator=brainpicker" rel="noopener" target="_blank">stationery cards</a>.)</figcaption></figure>
<p>With the distance of eleven years, Kent looked back on the experience to find in it the kernels of a larger truth &#8212; personal and universal, humanistic and more-than-human. (<em>Some necessary calibration for the ahistorical bristling modern readers often experience at our ancestors&#8217; word-choices: Women were not yet citizens and would finally win the right to vote two months after the artist&#8217;s return from Alaska, which was not yet a state and wouldn&#8217;t be for another forty years; the word &#8220;man&#8221; was both the unexamined universal pronoun &#8212; to remain so until Ursula K. Le Guin <a href="https://www.themarginalian.org/2014/10/17/ursula-k-le-guin-gender/">so exquisitely unsexed it</a> two generations later &#8212; and a reflection of what was practically possible and culturally permissible for women&#8217;s access to independent travel and wilderness adventuring.</em>) Kent writes in the introduction of the second edition of <a href="https://www.amazon.com/Wilderness-Adventure-Alaska_Including-Extensive-Unpublished/dp/0819552933/?tag=braipick-20" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><strong><em>Wilderness</em></strong></a>:</p>
<blockquote><p>The thought that was born to me in the quietness of that adventure &#8212; that in the wilderness, in uneventful solitude, men for companionship must find themselves &#8212; has come to be for me the truth. Maybe the only truth I know. </p>
<p>Go, young men to grow wise and wise men to stay young, not West nor East nor North nor South, but anywhere that men are not. For we all need, profoundly, to maintain ourselves in our essential, God-descended manhood against the forces of the day we live in &#8212; to be at last less products of a culture than the makers of it. There, in that wilderness so anciently unchanged it might have seen a hundred cultures flower and die, there realize &#8212; you must &#8212; that what is you, what feels and fears and hungers and exalts, is ancient as the wilderness itself, rich as the wilderness and kin to it. And of those ancient values of the soul, Art through all its fashions of utterance, despite them all, despite the turmoil of this age, despite New York and Harlem, steel and jazz, proclaims above the riot of Godlessness that there, in Man, eternally, is all the very much man ever knew of God.</p></blockquote>

## 中文译文

“这些是生命中的时光——看似什么也没有发生——然而，正是在寂静之中，灵魂得以舒展。”

人生中，这样的时刻并不常有——倘若幸运，一生中或许仅有寥寥数次——你会邂逅一种融思想与情感于一体的作品：一本书、一幅画、一首歌，它成为你反复归来啜饮的泉源，而每一次归来，它又将你焕然一新地送回生活本身。

于我而言，《小王子》是这样的一泓清泉，《草叶集》是，《我对你施了魔法》是，施托克豪森的《镜中镜》也是。而洛克威尔·肯特（Rockwell Kent，1882年6月21日－1971年3月13日）所著《荒野》（Wilderness，公共图书馆藏本）——这位画家、版画家兼哲人——亦是其中一脉。（衷心感谢乔治·戴森（George Dyson）将这份滋养灵魂的珍宝带入我的生命。）

《冬夜月光》，洛克威尔·肯特绘。（可作印刷画及信笺卡购买。）

八月末，在人类历史上第一场全球性战争的最后数月间，正当西班牙流感肆虐文明之际，肯特抵达阿拉斯加海岸外复活湾（Resurrection Bay）一座小岛，追寻那终极之物。彼时他三十六岁，心灰意冷、身无分文，其对艺术的炽热与世人对其作品的漠然所造成的痛楚，恰如沃尔特·惠特曼在同一年龄、同样困顿处境下自费出版《草叶集》时所经历的——同样深陷抑郁的幽谷，同样怀抱对生命不可动摇的虔敬。

基于这段经历，肯特日后提炼出近似个人信条的思想：

我常常思忖：无论我画得多勤、多好，我都并非通常意义上所理解的“艺术家”。抽象于我毫无意义，除非它是整体的一个碎片，而那整体，便是生命本身……真正令我关切的，是那终极之物；一切有形的、物质的存在，不过是它的某种表达……我们本就是万物宏大秩序中不可分割的一部分。我们不过是些乐器，以各自不同的尺度，记录着自身所分得的那一份无限。我们从中所汲取的，铸就我们的品格；我们向外所倾注的，则成为我们的表达。

我常常思忖：无论我画得多勤、多好，我都并非通常意义上所理解的“艺术家”。抽象于我毫无意义，除非它是整体的一个碎片，而那整体，便是生命本身……真正令我关切的，是那终极之物；一切有形的、物质的存在，不过是它的某种表达……我们本就是万物宏大秩序中不可分割的一部分。我们不过是些乐器，以各自不同的尺度，记录着自身所分得的那一份无限。我们从中所汲取的，铸就我们的品格；我们向外所倾注的，则成为我们的表达。

《幻象》，洛克威尔·肯特，1919年。（可作印刷画及信笺卡购买。）

肯特通往这种不凡艺术人生的路径，本身亦非同寻常。父母曾竭力敦促他将天赋导向实用、盈利的造型与功能领域，但他却从哥伦比亚大学建筑系辍学，转而投身于形式与情感的创作，并移居至缅因州海岸外一座崎岖孤岛。他在那里亲手筑起一座小屋，日复一日独处其中——阅读爱默生与托尔斯泰，作画；做龙虾捕捞工，作画。浸润于海克尔（Haeckel）所开创的生态学思想，他为自然生命彼此缠绕、相互依存的图景深深着迷；沉潜于梭罗的日记，他内化了“有意识地生活”的意志——在荒野之地，寻得并培育内心那片旷野：灵魂中既丰饶又荒凉的风景，一切艺术皆由此而生。

因此，二十九岁末，洛克威尔·肯特远航纽芬兰，希望与一位友人于未被侵扰的北方荒原共建一所集体艺术学校。希望终被现实击碎，但广袤北方已对他施下永恒的魔咒。四年后，即1914年，他携妻子与三个孩子重返此地——此时，世界正因大战而分崩离析。

在一个视“艺术家”为陌生乃至可疑概念的小镇社群里，心胸开阔、思想开明的肯特很快被指控为德国间谍。一家被迫仓皇离去，踏上漫长归途——妻子凯瑟琳（Kathleen）怀有第四胎，其余三个孩子均患百日咳。

然而，北方荒野始终在召唤艺术家的灵魂：

我渴慕雪峰、苍茫荒原，以及那冷酷的北国之海——它在世界尽头划出坚硬的地平线，而无限空间正由此启程。此处的天空更澄澈、更深邃；因其所揭示的壮丽奇观，它比温润之地的天空更能千倍地诉说那永恒的奥秘。

四年后，在艰难支撑日益庞大之家的巅峰时刻，洛克威尔·肯特再度北上，只为重振精神，只为简简单单地继续活下去。

“我从未以如此强烈的必要感与责任感，踏上这次阿拉斯加之行。”他告诉凯瑟琳。

他五岁丧父，除了一支父亲留下的银质长笛随身携带外，别无长物。此次远赴极北，他带着九岁的儿子——也叫洛克威尔——和这支银笛。“我们来到这片新土地，一个男孩与一个男人”，他写道，“全凭梦想者的追寻；因曾梦见北方的天堂，故而来此寻找。”

他们带着这些物资抵达狐岛（Fox Island），受到一位名叫奥尔森（Olson）的老年瑞典人热情接待。此人早年曾来此淘金，一无所获后，又被大陆城镇居民讥为“疯老头”，遂在这一偏僻孤岛上安家落户，照料两对蓝狐与四只山羊。肯特称奥尔森为“一位心地善良、性情爽朗的老人，拥有广博的知识与真正的智慧”，一位“阅历深厚、坚强勇敢、慷慨仁厚如孩童般纯真”的人，一位“敏锐的哲人——以其犀利的观察赋予言谈以高贵风范”。

父子二人着手将奥尔森的羊圈改造成家园。在原木小屋两侧，肯特——自幼便是一位出色的木匠——建造了两排顶天立地的长架：一排存放给养，另一排则搁置颜料、玩具、衣物与长笛。房间最远角落，他搭起一座书架，安放他们微型的私人图书馆——这是滋养心智与灵魂的食粮，其重要性不亚于他们穿越大陆、划过冰封北极海峡运来的罐头食品。书目包括《伊利亚特》与《奥德赛》；《鲁滨逊漂流记》与安徒生童话；一本印度哲学著作与一部爱尔兰文学史；一部海洋自然史与一本基础医学手册；威廉·布莱克的诗集及其传记《布莱克生平》——这本由安妮·吉尔克里斯特（Anne Gilchrist）于前一代人手中撰写的传记，曾使布莱克摆脱湮没无闻的命运，确立其作为几代人心目中创造偶像的地位；帕蒂·史密斯（Patti Smith）盛赞布莱克为“织机之织机”，“纺出启示的纤维”，而这部传记亦在肯特心中激起“强烈而启迪性的热忱”。

《小屋窗畔》，洛克威尔·肯特，1919年。（可作印刷画及信笺卡购买。）

尽管有这些微薄的慰藉，小屋仍是一副摇摇欲坠的模样，寒气无孔不入。肯特尝试用晒干的苔藓填塞原木间的缝隙，但连绵不绝的阴雨使苔藓始终无法彻底干燥，因而毫无保温之效。事实上，自踏足狐岛那一刻起，父子二人便步入一个被雨水主宰的世界——一种安妮·塞克斯顿（Anne Sexton）式的雨。头十七天里，仅有一个清晨云开日出。“没有亲爱的、温暖的太阳，生活将是多么奇异啊！”肯特在日记中慨叹。太阳的缺席——如同任何被珍视的温暖与光辉的缺席——反使其偶尔回归时愈发珍贵，闪耀着狂喜的光芒：

啊，晴朗的日子里，这里的黄昏与清晨多么美！太阳并非骤然跃入天空，又于夜晚倏忽隐去；而是如此徐缓、如此迂回地升起与沉落，几乎整日都浸润在暮色之中，晨昏那静谧的玫瑰色仿佛在正午时分悄然相接。我们透过西面那扇窄小的窗眺望日出，越过海湾，望见层层叠叠的山脉——从水边肃穆的山峦，到远方冰川与雪冠峰顶，皆沐浴在遥远太阳洒下的、想象中最美妙的光线里。

肯特很快在寂静的辽阔中发现一种崭新的自由——这自由不仅在于逃离战火纷飞的喧嚣当下，更在于挣脱一切集体人类文化的桎梏；而文化往往使身份僵化，成为灵魂的紧身衣：

在此地，我们与任何特定时代、任何文明之间，竟如此疏离，以至于只需一个念头，我们与我们的世界便可成为我们所愿成为的任何人、任何事物。

在艺术与自然所能赋予我们的独特方式中，父亲与孩子双双“去自我化”——不再是伤痕累累、被身份与意识形态所定义的个体，而成为一片感恩的觉知之域。他们沿复活湾海岸采摘浆果，在“冻得又硬又厚”的池塘上滑冰，也观赏杀人鲸在小屋旁的海湾嬉戏，“它们那可怕而神秘的黑色臂膀拍击水面，发出如大炮轰鸣般的声响”。

肯特以文字记录这些与自然元素的相遇，其日记读来宛如散文诗——任何全然专注、赤诚纯粹的自然观察，莫不如此：深具感染力，却又毫不矫饰，新鲜如初生之源。十月某个中旬的夜晚，他默诵了一位百年前出生的德国诗人所作的摇篮曲诗句后，欣然高呼：

置身于如此令人震撼的壮美之中——“此地有时美得令人难以承受”，肯特写道——人不可能不心生敬畏，不渴望将其传递、放大，并以己之创造为世界增添一份惊奇。于是，在登陆狐岛七周后一个寒冷的十月天，肯特记下：

我们回家，享用了丰盛晚餐。我劈了更多柴，终于，在岛上居住整整一个月之后，我开始作画了！那不过是一幅笨拙的速写，但无关紧要，我毕竟开始了！

在这样的一天，于这般湛蓝的天空之下、泡沫翻涌的碧绿大海之畔、童话般的群山之间工作，绝非小事一桩。

在这样的一天，于这般湛蓝的天空之下、泡沫翻涌的碧绿大海之畔、童话般的群山之间工作，绝非小事一桩。

自始至终，肯特便决意：倘若他的画作有朝一日能在文明世界展出，展览名称必须定为“天堂之画”——以此致敬他对儿子的爱，以及儿子对荒野的热爱：“我深知，生命中再无任何事物，能比洛克威尔对眼前这片天堂的完美信念更美。”肯特在一则日记中写道。这天堂由他文学偶像赫尔曼·黑塞（Hermann Hesse）在同一时代、不同大陆、迥异地貌上所称的“微小的欢愉”构筑而成——那些构成生命最细微的原子。肯特记录道：

清晨，我们一同起床，操练萨金特博士（Dr. Sargent）设计的一套体操，动作充满活力。随后，我们赤身裸体走出屋外……无论天气如何，我们都平静地步入其中，躺倒在雪堆里，仰望天空，再用雪擦拭全身。这是世上最棒的沐浴。

某日，环顾这座如今已成为家园的破旧羊圈——里面堆满书籍与风，充斥着一个男人的画作与一个孩子的爱，肯特感叹道：

我看不出人们为何需要比这更好的家园。

在隆冬时节的一则日记中，他思索着：在如此严酷条件下过一种如此简朴的生活，何以竟能抚慰并拓展自己的创作精神：

我们……已偏离那条被踩踏得拥挤不堪的道路，直面那无限而不可测度之物——荒野；而在此，我们找到了“我们自己”——因为荒野别无他物。它是一面活着的镜子，只映照并认领人之想象力所赋予它的一切，且仅此而已……倘若我们未曾因深渊的空洞而战栗，亦未因它的孤寂而逃遁，那是因为我们灵魂自身的丰饶，以意象填满了虚空，以温暖驱散了寒意，并赋予它言语与理解。

在这向自然与灵魂之宏伟俯首称臣的过程中，亦穿插着种种日常的悲喜剧。狂风从木屋缝隙灌入，将雪花吹满肯特的画桌。严寒变得如此暴烈，他的钢笔与颜料冻成坚冰，狐狸的食物冻成硬块，水桶在距轰鸣炉火十英尺远处便已结冰。奥尔森的一只山羊——“面孔愚蠢的安哥拉山羊”——吃掉了扫帚，继而闯入屋内，“箱子、水桶、粮袋、罐头、绳索、工具，全都杂乱无章地堆在地板上。”此类事件非但未令肯特沮丧，反而催生了他深沉的灵魂省思：

当鲜有事情发生、表达的范围又如此狭窄时，生活依然充盈着喜悦与悲伤。在寂静的世界里，简单的事件便足以令你心潮起伏。

与诗人伊丽莎白·毕肖普（Elizabeth Bishop）坚持“每个人都应至少经历一次漫长而极致的孤独”之主张相呼应，亦契合其同时代人赫尔曼·黑塞关于“困厄与孤独塑造命运”的洞见，肯特写道：

这些日子既美妙，又可怕。它令人振奋……想到我们与全人类彻底隔绝，想到在这狂怒的大海上，我们无法返回尘世，尘世亦无法抵达我们。唯有屏障才能确保你的孤立，使你得以体味其全部意义。冒险的浪漫，悬于纤细的丝线之上。山顶上一只香蕉皮，便足以驯服荒野。阿拉斯加的诸多荣光，正在于我深知：下一个海湾——我或许永远无意驶入——杳无人烟；而在那水天相接的山峦之外，是一片广阔无垠、亘古无人涉足的恐怖冰封荒原。

然而，纵使自然能令人性欢欣，我们亦天生渴望与同类相爱、联结。在经历二十周如此极致的隔绝后，于隆冬最深的低谷中，肯特写下一段话，其情感之真切，令任何二十一世纪之人——曾为一封未回复的邮件而焦虑，或凝视手机上闪烁又消失的三个小圆点而怅然若失者——无不感同身受：

一心期盼的邮件迟迟不来，实在令人极度沮丧。

而后，就在那一瞬——如同它总在发生，而我们却总忘记它终将发生——灵魂的漫漫长夜迎来黎明，抑郁的帷幕豁然拉开，他再次变得对美敞开，重新觉醒于生命之光：

今日辉煌壮丽，气候温和宜人，处处皆雪，甚至树梢亦覆白雪。积雪牢牢附着于山巅，直至最陡峭、最光秃的峰顶，将所有山峦染成一尘不染、 dazzling 的纯白。此景蔚为奇观……世间从未有过如此美丽的土地！

最终，时间感开始模糊。他唯一的计时器——一只由前任主人传给奥尔森的廉价手表——停摆了。父子二人开始依循动物本能生活：日出即起，准时早餐——永远是同一餐：燕麦粥、可可面包与花生酱；随后仅在饥饿时进食，全身心投入当日劳作与周遭活生生的世界之中，专注地观察、再观察，并将这些观察转化为艺术；入夜，肯特为小洛克威尔吹奏长笛，为他朗读（但绝不讲国王与王后的童话故事，因为男孩告诉父亲他讨厌这类故事，“它们老是结婚，还有诸如此类的东西”），直到他们“浑然不知时辰，便上床安寝”。一则典型日记写道：

辛勤劳作，极少玩耍，睡眠亦不足。风永不停歇地吹拂。洛克威尔永远那么乖巧——勤奋、善良、快乐。他如今已能相当自如地阅读任何书籍。绘画已成为他一种自然且规律的活动，近乎一种消遣——因为他既能严肃作画，亦能幽默涂鸦。此刻，他正躺在床上，等待一段音乐与另一个安徒生童话。

时间如此飘渺难寻，他们渐渐失去了日期的概念。这带来实际后果：往返于锡沃德（Seward）——“太平洋上的纽约”——的轮船班次稀疏而严格，他们依赖它寄送邮件与补给。这也带来诗意的后果：不确定何时庆祝小洛克威尔的十岁生日，他们便选定一个最可能的日子。当天，肯特开始教儿子唱歌，并赠予他唯一、珍贵的礼物——“一本廉价儿童版”的流行自然史百科全书。书中描绘他心爱的野生动物，令男孩欣喜若狂，于是他决定——比博尔赫斯早了一代人——开始撰写并绘制一部关于想象中怪兽的百科全书。

《查拉图斯特拉与他的朋友们》，洛克威尔·肯特，1919年。（可作印刷画及信笺卡购买。）

肯特日益深切地意识到：这些朴素的欢愉——书籍与自然、自由与爱——才是生命的根本，其余一切皆为噪音。在这种新生活——如此简朴、如此原始——的境遇下，他内心某种东西被唤醒，解构了文明长久以来束缚精神的思维惯性：

置身于群山之间至简至朴的生命中，精神不禁嘲笑人类对艺术形式的关注，嘲笑因旧形式过时而追求新表达的执念！实乃人类自身目光之贫瘠，使其一无所获，故而为拯救自己，只得为陈腐的旧套换上新装，或将历来被弃如敝履的心灵琐屑，抬升为艺术的素材。
