<h3>Less Air Time, More Interruptions</h3>
<p>Early in the Covid-19 pandemic, the U.S. Supreme Court began hearing oral arguments via conference calls rather than in person. Chief Justice John Roberts tried to impose order on the process by calling on justices one by one, in order of seniority, and he tried to give them roughly equal speaking time.</p>
<p>Despite his efforts to be evenhanded, over the course of 10 cases, Roberts disproportionately interrupted the three female justices (Ruth Bader Ginsburg, Elena Kagan, and Sonia Sotomayor), even when they spoke the same amount or less than their male colleagues, according to <a href="https://scholarlycommons.law.emory.edu/faculty-articles/39/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">an analysis</a> by researcher Tonja Jacobi and colleagues.</p>
<p>Such inequities on the court existed before it (temporarily) switched to phone calls, <a href="https://www.virginialawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/JacobiSchweers_Online.pdf" rel="noopener" target="_blank">a study</a> by Jacobi and Dylan Schweers found. Yet this bias may be exacerbated in online negotiations. With body language and other nonverbal cues missing from phone calls and difficult to read in video calls, meeting attendees may be more likely to interrupt one another, with women often losing the battle for speaking time.</p>
<p>If you notice on your phone and video calls that some people seem reluctant to speak or are being interrupted, you’ll want to look for ways to make the environment more hospitable and equitable. For Roberts, this might have meant putting someone else in charge of timekeeping during the Court’s conference calls. Doing so would have allowed him to focus more on the discussion’s substance and foster more equitable turn taking, Jacobi’s study suggests.</p>
<p>Calling on those who may be marginalized during online negotiations and meetings is another obvious fix. As head of diversity and inclusion at Unilever, Mita Mallick <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2020/04/14/us/zoom-meetings-gender.html" rel="noopener" target="_blank">told the <em>New York Times</em></a> that she relied on the chat function and nonverbal cues during Zoom calls to encourage those who were more reticent. During a 25-person online meeting, she said, “I was giving a thumbs up, I was making a heart with my hands, I was smiling” to show someone “nonverbally that I was excited about what they’re saying.”</p>
<h3>Gender Differences in “Zoom Fatigue”</h3>
<p><a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2451958823000040" rel="noopener" target="_blank">One recent study</a> with nearly 10,000 participants finds that women are significantly more likely than men to experience so-called Zoom fatigue—a sense of mental, emotional, and physical depletion or exhaustion after participating in videoconferencing calls.</p>
<p>In fact, women report experiencing severe Zoom fatigue at more than twice the rate of men (13.8% versus 5.5%). In particular, women are disproportionately worn out by what the authors term “mirror anxiety,” or the stress of constantly viewing one’s own reflection. Because their physical appearance and social presentation tend to be scrutinized more than men’s, women are more socially conditioned to monitor their appearance. When one’s computer screen becomes a mirror, constantly monitoring one’s appearance can create cognitive strain and negative emotions.</p>
<p>The researchers advise anyone who might be stressed or distracted on video calls to turn off the mirror function (on Zoom, by right-clicking and selecting “hide self-view”) so they’re still seen by others but don’t see their reflection. In addition, the researchers encourage organizations to establish “camera-optional” norms and make phone calls for routine check-ins.</p>
<h3>Text Versus Video Outcomes</h3>
<p>Some recent research evidence suggests that gender inequities are more likely in video negotiations than in text-based chat negotiations.</p>
<p>In their research, Wolfram Emanuel Lipp and Alwine Mohnen found no difference in the message length and outcomes of men and women in chat negotiations. But when participants engaged in the same <a href="https://www.pon.harvard.edu/tag/negotiation-simulation/">negotiation simulation</a> via video, female participants spoke less than male participants, and the women earned lower individual profits as a result. For organizations, these findings suggest that requiring job candidates or employees to negotiate via video could disadvantage women.</p>
<p>Yet, even when negotiations are conducted online without video, implicit bias can still persist. In <a href="https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2023-07859-001" rel="noopener" target="_blank">a study of 435 human resource professionals</a>, researcher Carolin Schuster and her coauthors found that in a text-based online negotiation with simulated candidates, these experts offered significantly lower salaries to the female candidates than to male candidates with identical qualifications.</p>
<p>However, when the HR professionals received reminders about equal pay before negotiating, such as “Most companies pay equally now” or “Companies are actively changing and trying harder to pay equally,” they offered female candidates more money. The reminder appeared to make the HR professionals feel like they were part of a team effort or larger movement working toward a shared goal of fairness in negotiation. (By contrast, simply reminding the professionals that a gender wage gap exists didn’t boost their offers to women.)</p>
<p>In fact, the reminders about equal pay led to an overcorrection: The professionals now offered female candidates higher compensation than they offered identically qualified male candidates! Thus, such messages need to be tinkered with before they can be considered a panacea for equalizing <a href="https://www.pon.harvard.edu/tag/job-negotiations/">job negotiations</a> and offers across gender.</p>

发言时间更少，被打断更多

新冠疫情初期，美国联邦最高法院开始通过电话会议而非现场方式听取口头辩论。首席大法官约翰·罗伯茨（John Roberts）试图通过按资历顺序逐一指定大法官发言、并尽量均衡分配每人发言时长的方式，来维持会议秩序。

然而，尽管罗伯茨努力做到一视同仁，但研究者托恩贾·雅各比（Tonja Jacobi）及其同事的分析显示，在10起案件的听证过程中，他打断三位女性大法官（露丝·巴德·金斯伯格、埃琳娜·卡根与索尼娅·索托马约尔）的次数明显多于男性同僚——即便她们的发言时长相同甚至更短。

雅各比与迪伦·施韦尔斯（Dylan Schweers）此前的一项研究发现，此类不平等现象在法院转为电话听证前就已存在。但线上谈判可能进一步加剧这一偏见。由于电话会议中完全缺失肢体语言等非言语线索，而视频会议中这类线索又难以准确捕捉，与会者彼此打断的情况更易发生，而女性往往在争夺发言权的“拉锯战”中处于劣势。

若您在电话或视频会议中察觉某些人似乎不愿开口，或频繁被他人打断，便需主动设法营造更友善、更公平的沟通环境。对罗伯茨而言，这意味着可指定他人专门负责计时工作，从而让他本人能更专注于讨论内容本身，并促进更公平的轮流发言——这正是雅各比研究提出的建议。

另一项显而易见的补救措施，是在线上谈判与会议中主动点名邀请那些可能被边缘化的参与者发言。联合利华（Unilever）多元与包容事务负责人米塔·马利克（Mita Mallick）曾向《纽约时报》透露，她在Zoom会议中善用聊天框功能与非言语信号，鼓励较为内敛的与会者表达观点。例如，在一场25人的线上会议中，她会“竖起大拇指”“双手比心”“面带微笑”，以非言语方式向某位发言者传递“我非常欣赏你所说的内容”这一积极信号。

女性更易遭遇“Zoom疲劳”

一项涵盖近一万名参与者的最新研究发现，女性比男性显著更易经历所谓“Zoom疲劳”——即在参加视频会议后，产生心理、情绪乃至生理上的耗竭感或疲惫感。

事实上，女性报告出现严重Zoom疲劳的比例是男性的两倍以上（13.8% vs. 5.5%）。尤其值得注意的是，女性更容易因作者所称的“镜像焦虑”（mirror anxiety）而精疲力竭——即持续面对自己实时影像所引发的压力。由于社会对女性外貌与社交形象的关注度普遍高于男性，女性也更习惯于监控自身仪态；当电脑屏幕变成一面“镜子”，这种持续不断的自我审视便会带来认知负荷与负面情绪。

研究人员建议，任何在视频会议中感到压力或分心的人，都可关闭“自视镜像”功能（在Zoom中，右键点击画面并选择“隐藏自我视图”），如此既可确保他人仍能看到自己，又能避免直视自身影像。此外，研究人员还呼吁各类组织确立“摄像头可选”的规范，并对日常例行沟通优先采用电话形式。

文字沟通与视频沟通的谈判结果差异

部分最新研究证据表明，相较于基于文字的在线聊天谈判，视频谈判中更易出现性别不平等现象。

沃尔夫拉姆·埃马努埃尔·利普（Wolfram Emanuel Lipp）与阿尔温·莫恩恩（Alwine Mohnen）的研究发现，在文字聊天谈判中，男女双方的信息长度与最终结果并无差异；但当参与者通过视频开展同一场模拟谈判时，女性发言时间明显短于男性，由此导致其个人收益也更低。这对企业而言意味着：若强制求职者或员工通过视频方式进行谈判，可能对女性构成不利影响。

然而，即便线上谈判全程无视频参与，隐性偏见依然可能持续存在。在一项针对435名人力资源专业人士的研究中，研究者卡罗琳·舒斯特（Carolin Schuster）及其合作者发现：当这些专业人士通过纯文字方式与模拟候选人进行线上谈判时，他们向资质完全相同的女性候选人所提供的薪资，显著低于男性候选人。

但若在谈判前向HR人员提示“薪酬平等”相关信息——例如“多数公司如今已实行同工同酬”或“各企业正积极变革，努力推进薪酬公平”——则他们向女性候选人开出的薪资便明显提高。此类提示似乎让HR人员感受到自己正融入一支致力于共同目标（即推动谈判公平）的团队或更大规模的社会运动之中。（相比之下，仅简单提醒“性别薪酬差距依然存在”，却并未提升他们向女性提供的薪资水平。）

事实上，“薪酬平等”的提示甚至引发了矫枉过正现象：HR人员此时给予女性候选人的薪酬，竟高于资质完全相同的男性候选人！因此，在将此类提示语视为弥合招聘谈判与录用待遇中性别差距的“万能解药”之前，还需对其措辞与应用方式进行审慎优化与调整。
