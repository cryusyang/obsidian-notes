<p><strong>1. Can a manager lead a Bible study?</strong></p>
<blockquote><p>I am a mid-level supervisor for a state government agency. While I directly supervise several employees, about half of the employees in my section directly report to my manager, “Michael.” He, in turn, reports to “David” and “Jan,” who are the chief and the second-in-command. There are several other sections within our agency that do separate work, and those sections have their own corresponding Michaels, who also report to David and Jan.</p>
<p>Hypothetically (I do not have any plans to do this currently), given the fact that I supervise some employees, would it be inappropriate for me to organize a Bible study, book club where we read a religious book, etc., which would occur, say, over lunch?</p>
<p>On the one hand, my religion is extremely important to me and my religious beliefs aren’t exactly a secret. (For example, I told a coworker who lost a family member that I would pray for them, because I know they follow the same religion as me.)</p>
<p>However, I would not want anyone, especially those I supervise, to think that their participation (or lack thereof) in this sort of activity is being encouraged by me by virtue of my position. That is, I wouldn’t want there to be any pressure for people to participate because someone in management is doing it. Does the fact that I only supervise a handful of people when there are well over 100 employees in our agency, many of whom do entirely separate work from me, make a difference?</p></blockquote>
<p>No, a manager should not organize any kind of religious discussion at work. No matter how sincere you are in saying that people who attend wouldn’t get favorable treatment from you, some of your staff will still worry that they will and/or will feel pressured to participate and/or will find it unfair that people are getting extra networking opportunities with you based on a shared religious practice. And frankly, it <i>would</i> be unfair — that <i>is</i> an extra networking opportunity with you, and it shouldn’t be open only to people willing to talk about religion with you (or be based around religion at all).</p>
<p><strong>2. Can I ask an interviewer about their terrible customer reviews?</strong></p>
<blockquote><p>After being laid off a month ago, I&#8217;m in the early stages of interviewing for a learning and development role at a midsize corporation. Unlike my last job, where I was creating customer-facing trainings, this role would entail creating internal trainings on a wide range of topics, which is much more aligned with my long-term career goals. However, when I was researching the company, I discovered that the service the company provides garners VERY low customer ratings (as in, 1.4 stars on Google reviews and Yelp and an enormous pile of Better Business Bureau complaints). While of course I realize that these online reviews don&#8217;t reflect the good experiences (there must be some, right?), I was appalled by some of the claims people were making.</p>
<p>Right now, I&#8217;m leaning toward not moving forward in the interview process, as I&#8217;m not desperate for a job (yet!) and I am reluctant to work for a company that seems to have no compunction about scamming its customers. But there is a part of me that is curious as to how they would respond if I asked them about those reviews — I mean, maybe they&#8217;re working to address the issues. I have been considering asking something like, &#8220;I have seen some online reviews where customers are really unhappy with Company&#8217;s services. I know that people who are happy with Company aren&#8217;t likely to go online to rave about it, but I was wondering what steps Company takes to address customer feedback and how your customer service reps fit into the vision you have for the L&amp;D team.&#8221; Or something along those lines. Is that totally unhinged? Should I just cut and run now?</p></blockquote>
<p>It’s not unhinged at all. That’s a pretty normal question to ask in this context, and they’ve probably been asked it before! They’re aware of their reviews, and they’re aware (some) candidates will be too. Your wording isn’t particularly aggressive or adversarial; it’s reasonable.</p>
<p>That doesn’t mean you shouldn’t cut and run. But if you’d otherwise be interested in the job, go to the next interview and ask.</p>

<p>The front-end guy, Kevin, and I talked regularly, and he&#8217;d often talk junk about her. He’d regularly make comments to me like, “Well, what the hell does Amanda do all day? Why don&#8217;t we see her on X date?” It made me uncomfortable because neither of us was Amanda&#8217;s supervisor and I felt it was none of our business as it didn&#8217;t affect our jobs in the slightest.</p>

<p>Are you close with Amanda and do you consider her a friend? If so, sure, you could go ahead and tell her — if you think she needs to know that Kevin is potentially stirring up drama that could affect her. If you’d just be telling her on principle and not because it potentially could have repercussions for her, then no; in that case I’d leave it alone since there’s no point in getting involved when you’re not even there anymore.</p>
<p>And if you’re not close with Amanda, then there’s nothing to do. You’re no longer there and that office&#8217;s issues don’t need to take up any space in your brain (and it risks coming across as odd and drama-stirring to contact someone you’re not close to after leaving to share something like this).</p>

<p>What&#8217;s frustrating is that the administration and HR tell us that we have to be on campus for a particular eight hours a day (even when not teaching) and sometimes even on days with no classes or meetings. As teachers, we are a pretty highly educated bunch; most have advanced degrees and could earn a lot more in other industries. It feels kind of disrespectful of our time and dedication for us to be told that we have to be on campus when nothing is scheduled, and it feels inconsistent with other jobs for salaried professionals. What are your thoughts?</p></blockquote>
<p>Yes, it’s disrespectful of your time, your obvious commitment to the work, and the amount of additional hours you put in over and above a normal work week. It&#8217;s also pretty par for the course in teaching, unfortunately, which is still very much a “you need to be in the building to be seen to be working” culture from what I understand.</p>

1. 管理者能否组织圣经学习小组？

我是某州政府机构的一名中层主管。我直接管理若干名员工，而我所在部门约一半的员工则直接向我的上级“迈克尔”汇报；迈克尔本人再向部门正、副主任“大卫”和“简”汇报。我们机构内还有多个其他部门，各自承担不同职能，这些部门也各有其对应的“迈克尔”，均向大卫和简汇报。

假设（目前我并无此计划），鉴于我确实直接管理部分员工，那么我在工作时间（例如午休时段）组织圣经学习小组、以宗教类书籍为读物的读书会等活动，是否不妥？

一方面，我的宗教信仰对我而言极为重要，且我信奉的宗教也并非什么秘密。（例如，当一位同事家中遭遇亲人离世时，我曾主动告知对方：我会为他祷告，因为我了解他与我信奉同一宗教。）

但另一方面，我绝不愿任何人——尤其是我所管理的下属——误以为，我凭借自身管理职位在变相鼓励他们参与或不参与此类活动。换言之，我不希望因自己身为管理者而使他人感到必须参加的压力。那么，考虑到我仅直接管理少数几人，而整个机构员工总数远超百人，其中许多人从事的工作与我完全无关，这一事实是否会影响上述判断？

不，管理者不应在工作场所组织任何形式的宗教讨论。无论你多么真诚地强调，参与者不会因此获得你的特殊优待，你的部分下属仍可能担心自己会因此被区别对待，或感到不得不参与的压力，又或认为仅凭共同宗教信仰便能额外获得与你建立人脉的机会，这本身就不公平。事实上，这确实是额外的人脉机会——而它本不该只向愿意与你探讨宗教话题（或任何以宗教为核心）的人开放。

2. 我能否在面试中向面试官询问该公司糟糕的客户评价？

一个月前我被裁员，目前正处在一家中型企业的学习与发展岗位面试初期阶段。与上一份工作不同（当时我负责面向客户的培训课程开发），这份新岗位将主要开发覆盖各类主题的内部培训课程，更契合我的长期职业发展目标。然而，在调研该公司时，我发现其提供的服务客户评分极低（谷歌与Yelp平台均为1.4星，美国商业改进局网站上亦堆满大量投诉）。我当然明白，网络评价未必反映全部真实情况（毕竟总该有些满意客户吧？），但某些用户提出的指控仍令我震惊。

这完全不算离谱。在此情境下提出此类问题相当正常，对方很可能已多次被问及！他们清楚自己的评价状况，也清楚（部分）候选人同样会注意到这一点。你的措辞并不尖锐或具有对抗性，而是合情合理的。

但这并不意味着你不该果断退出。如果你原本对该职位确有兴趣，不妨继续参加下一轮面试，并提出这个问题。

3. 我是否该告诉前同事有人在背后议论他？

我最近任职于一个七人团队，其中包括系统管理员阿曼达。她出勤率不高，但并未影响到我们其他人。我认为，她与团队管理层之间达成的任何安排都属于内部事务，与我们无关。

前端工程师凯文与我关系较熟，常有交流，但他却频繁在背后议论阿曼达。他常对我说：“阿曼达整天到底在忙什么？为什么X日那天根本见不到她人影？”这类言论让我很不舒服——毕竟我们既非阿曼达的上级，此事也丝毫不影响我们的本职工作，纯属无谓的闲言碎语。

倘若你与阿曼达本就不熟，那就更无需行动。你已离开该单位，昔日办公室的种种纠葛，本就不该再占据你的心神（况且，离职后突然联系一位并不亲近的前同事，只为转述此类流言，反而容易显得古怪，甚至有挑拨是非之嫌）。

4. 即便无需到场，仍被要求亲自到校
