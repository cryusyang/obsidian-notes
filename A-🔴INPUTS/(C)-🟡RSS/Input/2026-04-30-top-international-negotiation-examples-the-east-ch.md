<img alt="International Negotiation" height="133" src="https://www.pon.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/images/posts/international-negotiation.jpg" title="International Negotiation" width="200" /><br /><p>During an international negotiation, even when all parties genuinely aim for an outcome that feels fair to everyone involved, their perceptions of fairness often end up being self-serving. Each side is inclined to view its own position, contributions, and needs as more deserving than the other’s. As a result, negotiators may believe they merit a larger share of a resource or more favorable terms than an impartial observer would consider fair. Recognizing this natural bias is an important step toward crafting agreements that truly reflect mutual interests rather than inflated self-assessments.</p>
<p>China’s establishment of an “air defense” zone over a disputed chain of islands in the East China Sea back in November of 2013 is just one salvo in an escalating international dispute. Japan and China both laid claim to the islands, known as the Senkaku by Japan and the Diaoyu by China, which is believed to be rich in oil and is also strategically important, according to CNN.</p>

<p>China’s “unilateral action constitutes an attempt to change the status quo in the East China Sea. Escalatory action will only increase tensions in the region and create risks of an incident,” U.S. Secretary of State John Kerry said in response to China’s air defense move.</p>
<p>China began patrolling the zone, and its planes reportedly came within miles of Japanese airspace several times. Each time, Japan launched fighter jets in response. Meanwhile, Vietnam and the Philippines made their own claims to the sea.</p>
<p>This type of conflict over scarce resources can be particularly tricky to resolve. Business negotiators facing this type of resource division may be able to avoid conflict by spending time thinking about each party’s contributions and claims. By consciously taking another person’s perspective, we become equipped to consider the situation with greater rationality and fairness.</p>
<p>In our own <a href="http://www.pon.harvard.edu/tag/business-negotiation/">business negotiations</a>, how can we convince a counterpart that concessions we view to be essential—whether on financial, moral, or other grounds—is not the first in a line of toppling dominoes?</p>
<h4><em><strong>First</strong></em>, <strong>work to build trust in international negotiation</strong>.</h4>
<ul>
<li>If your counterpart is new to you, or if past international <a href="https://www.pon.harvard.edu/tag/negotiation/">negotiation</a> hasn’t gone their way, you can’t expect them to trust your motives. Give the other side space to air their concerns and past grievances, and apologize and make amends for any actions of yours that created mistrust.</li>
</ul>
<h4><em><strong>Second</strong></em>, <strong>ask questions and share information during an international negotiation</strong>.</h4>
<ul>
<li>Devote plenty of time to a back-and-forth dialogue about the issues at stake. By asking the other party lots of questions about their positions, you can reveal their underlying interests. In addition, share information about your own interests. This type of open exchange should allow you to uncover potential tradeoffs.</li>
</ul>
<h4><em><strong>Third</strong></em>, <strong>demonstrate principles of good-faith <a href="http://www.pon.harvard.edu/tag/bargaining/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">bargaining</a> during an international negotiation</strong>.</h4>
<ul>
<li>Rather than demanding a unilateral concession, look for ways to try to make the other party whole. It’s unrealistic to expect any negotiator to make a big concession without the promise of a corresponding benefit or evidence that you are making concessions in return.</li>
</ul>

## 中文译文

在国际谈判中，即便各方都真诚地希望达成一个令所有参与者都感到公平的结果，他们对“公平”的认知却往往带有自我服务倾向。每一方都倾向于认为自身立场、所作贡献及需求比对方更应得到重视。因此，谈判者可能坚信自己理应获得更大份额的资源或更有利的条款，而这种主张在中立观察者看来未必公平。认识到这种天然存在的偏见，是迈向制定真正反映各方共同利益、而非基于过度自我评估之协议的重要一步。

2013年11月，中国在东海存在主权争议的一组岛屿上空设立所谓“防空识别区”，这仅是日益升级的国际争端中的一次交锋。据美国有线电视新闻网（CNN）报道，日本与中国均声称对该群岛拥有主权——日本称其为“尖阁诸岛”，中国则称其为“钓鱼岛”。该群岛据信蕴藏丰富石油资源，且具有重要战略价值。

针对中国的这一防空识别区举措，时任美国国务卿约翰·克里（John Kerry）回应称：“中方单方面行动意在改变东海现状。此类升级性举动只会加剧地区紧张局势，并增加发生意外事件的风险。”

此后，中国开始在该识别区内开展巡逻，据报道其军机曾多次逼近距日本领空仅数英里的区域；每次中方飞机接近，日本均紧急出动战斗机升空应对。与此同时，越南与菲律宾也各自就南海相关海域提出了主权主张。

此类围绕稀缺资源展开的冲突，尤其难以化解。面对类似资源分配问题的商务谈判者，或可通过花时间深入思考各方的贡献与主张，从而避免陷入对立。若能主动换位思考、设身处地理解他人立场，我们便更有能力以更理性、更公正的态度审视整个局面。

在我们自身的商务谈判中，如何说服对方：我们视作不可或缺的让步——无论出于财务、道义或其他考量——并非引发连锁反应的第一块多米诺骨牌？

第一，致力于在国际谈判中建立信任。  
倘若对方对你尚不熟悉，或过往国际谈判未能使其如愿，你便不能指望对方信任你的动机。应给予对方充分空间，使其得以表达关切与历史积怨；同时，就自身曾导致信任受损的行为诚恳致歉并作出弥补。

第二，在国际谈判过程中积极提问并共享信息。  
就关键议题投入大量时间开展双向对话。通过向对方提出大量关于其立场的问题，可逐步揭示其深层利益诉求；同时，也应主动分享己方的利益关切。此类开放、坦诚的信息交换，有助于双方发现潜在的互惠权衡空间。

第三，在国际谈判中践行善意协商的基本原则。  
切勿一味要求对方单方面让步，而应努力寻找使对方“整体利益不受损”的解决方案。期望任何谈判者在未获相应回报承诺、或未见你方同步让步证据的情况下做出重大让步，都是不现实的。
