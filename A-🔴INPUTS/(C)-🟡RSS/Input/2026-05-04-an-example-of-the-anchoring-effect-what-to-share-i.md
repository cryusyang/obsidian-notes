<img alt="Negotiation Skills" height="133" src="https://www.pon.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/images/posts/negotiation-skills-1.jpg" title="Negotiation Skills" width="200" /><br /><p>The prospect of sharing information with a negotiating counterpart can be unsettling. It may trigger the <a href="http://www.pon.harvard.edu/tag/anchoring-effect/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">anchoring effect</a>, fixing the other party to a position you never intended or leading them to assume you are eager to reach a deal at any cost. There is also the risk of revealing privileged information the other side could use against you. Taking time to carefully analyze the pros and cons of sharing information in negotiation will help you approach <a href="http://www.pon.harvard.edu/tag/negotiation-scenarios/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">negotiation scenarios</a> with greater confidence and a stronger sense of control.</p>
<h3><strong>Information Sharing at the Bargaining Table</strong></h3>
<p>Don’t wait for the other side to open up to you first. The advantages of sharing information during negotiation have been well documented. Thanks to the power of reciprocity, your counterpart is likely to match any information you share with valuable information of his own.</p>
<p>In general, you should feel comfortable revealing information about your interests in the negotiation, as well as your priorities across different issues. That doesn’t mean that if there are five issues on the table, you should reveal that you care about only two of them. Rather, stress that all the issues are important to you, but you’d have a hard time budging on two of them.</p>

<p>Here are some other types of information you generally should feel comfortable sharing, according to Georgetown University Law Center professor Carrie Menkel-Meadow:</p>
<p><strong>Information required by law.</strong></p>
<p>Don’t risk serious anchoring effect issues such as getting into hot water by concealing information you’re legally or ethically required to disclose. Home sellers, for instance, may need to reveal known property defects to potential buyers. Research relevant laws and professional standards before you negotiate.</p>
<p><strong>Information that requires “damage </strong><strong>control.”</strong></p>
<p>Just as a defense attorney questions his client in court about incriminating information before the prosecution can raise the issue, the best way to handle troubling facts may be to come clean to your counterpart. For both ethical and strategic reasons, for example, a job applicant who was fired from her previous position would be wise to explain what happened to an interviewer rather than waiting for him to discover it during a reference check.</p>
<p><strong>Readily available information.</strong></p>
<p>These days, information that was once difficult to track down—including financial and disclosure statements, legal documents, and news reports—may be just a Google search away. When deciding whether to share sensitive information that’s widely available, assess what would happen if your counterpart discovered such information on his own.</p>

## 中文译文

与谈判对手分享信息，这一前景可能令人不安。它可能引发“锚定效应”，使对方固守于你本无意确立的立场，或误以为你急于达成协议、不惜一切代价。此外，还存在泄露机密信息的风险——对方可能利用这些信息对你不利。花时间仔细分析谈判中信息共享的利与弊，将有助于你以更强的信心和更明确的掌控感应对各类谈判情境。

谈判桌上的信息共享  

切勿坐等对方先向你敞开心扉。谈判过程中分享信息的优势已有大量实证研究支持。得益于互惠原则的力量，你的谈判对手很可能会以同样有价值的信息回应你所披露的内容。

总体而言，你大可放心地向对方说明自己在本次谈判中的利益诉求，以及你在各项议题上的优先顺序。但这并不意味着：倘若谈判桌上共有五个议题，你就该直接坦言自己只关心其中两个。相反，你应强调所有议题对你都很重要，只是其中两个议题你很难让步。

根据乔治城大学法律中心教授卡莉·门克尔-米多（Carrie Menkel-Meadow）的观点，以下几类信息通常也适合在谈判中坦诚分享：

依法必须披露的信息  

切勿因隐瞒法律或职业伦理要求必须披露的信息而招致严重后果，例如触发不利的锚定效应。例如，房屋卖方须向潜在买家如实告知已知的房产缺陷。因此，在开展谈判前，请务必查阅相关法律法规及行业规范。

需主动进行“危机管理”的信息  

正如辩护律师会在检察官提出指控前，率先在法庭上就对当事人不利的事实向其发问，处理棘手事实的最佳方式，往往正是主动向谈判对手坦白。例如，一位曾被前雇主解雇的求职者，出于道德与策略双重考量，明智之举是主动向面试官解释离职原因，而非等到对方在背景调查中自行发现。

公开易得的信息  

如今，许多过去难以获取的信息——包括财务报表、信息披露文件、法律文书及新闻报道等——只需一次谷歌搜索即可获得。当你评估是否要分享某项虽敏感但广为人知的信息时，请先设想：若对方自行查到此类信息，将会产生何种影响？
