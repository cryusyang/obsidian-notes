> 文章探讨了美国在重大灾难后由“特别主事人”（special masters）主导的受害者赔偿计划设计与实施经验。三位资深特别主事人——肯尼思·费恩伯格、埃里克·格林和弗朗西斯·麦戈文——强调，面对大量索赔人和复杂情感诉求，赔偿方案需兼顾公平性、透明度与公众参与，尤其要通过协商获得受害者“认同”以建立合法性。他们指出，量化伤害（如用医疗支出替代心理创伤评估）虽不完美，但若获广泛接受即具可行性；在资金有限的前提下，分配模型必须逻辑清晰、开放公正，并鼓励受影响者参与决策过程。

<img alt="Dispute Resolution" height="133" src="https://www.pon.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/images/posts/dispute-resolution-1.jpg" title="Dispute Resolution" width="200" /><br /><p>In the aftermath of a large-scale catastrophe or disaster in the United States—such as 9/11, the opioid epidemic, and mass shootings—the courts can be ill-equipped to take on the complex task of engaging in multiparty negotiations with large numbers of claimants over compensation. Instead, “special masters” are often assigned to create and administer victim-compensation programs, a job that typically requires thousands of negotiations on emotionally wrenching issues.</p>
<p>At a <a href="https://www.pon.harvard.edu/events/resolving-disputes/">Program on Negotiation (PON) panel discussion</a> on October 22, 2019, PON chair Guhan Subramanian spoke with three special masters who have overseen many mass-disaster programs: Kenneth Feinberg (<a href="https://www.vcf.gov/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">9/11 Victim Compensation Fund</a>, <a href="https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/mediation/mediating-tragedy-managing-the-boston-victims-compensation-fund/">Boston Marathon bombings</a>, <a href="https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2019/04/15/what-do-the-churchs-victims-deserve" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Catholic Church sexual-abuse crisis</a>); Resolutions, LLC cofounder Eric Green (Enron securities, <a href="http://www.takataspecialmaster.com/index" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Takata airbags</a>); and <a href="https://law.duke.edu/news/duke-law-mourns-francis-mcgovern-preeminent-expert-adr-resolving-mass-tort-claims" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Duke University professor Francis McGovern</a> (BP Deepwater Horizon oil spill, opioid epidemic), who passed away in 2020. Their remarks, overviewed here, offer insights to those facing <a href="https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/business-negotiations/bringing-multiparty-agreement-back-brink-nb/">complex multiparty negotiations</a>.</p>

<p><strong><em>Guhan Subramanian:</em> In your work, is it useful to use a </strong><a href="https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/teaching-negotiation-daily/howard-raiffa-taught-us-to-make-better-decisions-nb/"><strong>scoring system</strong></a><strong> to quantify harm?</strong><strong> </strong></p>
<p><strong><em>Francis McGovern:</em> </strong>You can use numbers as surrogates for intangibles, such as psychological harm. It can be really difficult to put a number on psychological harm, so [for the 2003 Rhode Island nightclub fire, in which 100 people were killed and 230 were injured] we used medical expenses: If you went to your psychiatrist and you had medical expenses, then that would be counted in the metric. It ends up, at the end of the day, being acceptable, because the people [harmed] will accept it. . . . If everybody agrees to a particular approach, I don’t really care what the approach is, as long as you have that buy-in.</p>
<p><strong><em>Eric Green:</em> </strong>At some point, all these cases come down to who gets compensation for grievous injuries, and no amount of money is really sufficient to compensate, but it’s all we have. And most of the time we have a limited fund. . . . So one must come up with a model to allocate within that limited fund among the range of injuries. And if it’s an <a href="https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/dispute-resolution/alternative-dispute-resolution-examples-restorative-justice/">alternative system</a> and not being litigated, it has to be a model that has logic to it, that is transparent, where the people affected have a say in it. It has to be done with integrity, fairness, and openness. All of those things demand a lot of innovation at every stage of the way.</p>
<p><strong><em>Guhan Subramanian:</em> I was struck when you said that the victims should have some say in the process.</strong><strong><em> </em></strong></p>
<p><strong><em>Francis McGovern:</em> </strong>We’re special masters. How are we legitimate? . . . We don’t have the imprimatur of a jury verdict. And so the way we achieve legitimacy . . . is getting people to participate in the decision-making process.</p>
<p><strong><em>Guhan Subramanian:</em> In addition to the quantitative models you use, do you make qualitative judgment calls?</strong></p>
<p><strong><em>Eric Green:</em> </strong>Almost every model that I can think of that I’ve been involved in has provisions for extraordinary cases, so there is an element of discretion. And, of course, we know from the outset that we’re never going to make everybody happy. There are going to be people who are not going to accept any compensation offer, who feel they weren’t treated the way they should have been, and are going to let you know it.</p>
<p><strong><em>Guhan Subramanian:</em> We talk in <a href="https://www.pon.harvard.edu/keyword-index/#:~:text=Keyword%20Index-,negotiation,-%2C%20negotiators%2C">negotiations</a> about expressing empathy. Ken [Feinberg], I think, if the public reports are accurate, you and your team agreed to hear from 9/11 victims. Anyone who wanted to speak to you or your team could do so. Obviously, that’s incredibly powerful, but is it a useful approach in general?</strong></p>
<p><strong><em>Kenneth Feinberg:</em> </strong>It’s the key! All of these models that I’ve been involved in substantively can vary, but there’s one common denominator, and that is the emotionalism of victims or their surviving family members. And empathy and providing those who voluntarily want an opportunity to be heard in private are essential.</p>
<p>I’m now dealing with . . . Catholic Church clergy sexual-abuse cases in five states, and the one common denominator is the victim who was abused as a minor, and is now 30, 40, 50 years old, and comes in private to see me—not to complain about the compensation offer. They want validation. They want acknowledgment of what was done. They want somebody independent to say to them, “I believe you. You were wronged. It’s awful.” The number of people who were afforded that opportunity to be heard and leave feeling better, knowing that somebody said, “Yes, you were accurate, truthful, credible,” is critical to the success of all of these programs—from Agent Orange or 9/11 right up to the present time.</p>

## 中文译文

在美国发生大规模灾难或危机事件之后——例如“9·11”恐怖袭击、阿片类药物滥用危机以及大规模枪击案——法院往往难以胜任一项复杂任务：即就赔偿问题，与数量庞大的索赔人开展多方谈判。取而代之的是，法院常指定“特别调解官”（special masters）来创设并管理受害者赔偿计划。这项工作通常涉及成千上万次谈判，而谈判议题又往往牵涉极为沉重的情感创伤。

2019年10月22日，在哈佛大学法学院“谈判项目”（Program on Negotiation, PON）举办的一场专题研讨会上，PON主席古汉·苏布拉马尼安（Guhan Subramanian）与三位曾主导过多项重大灾难赔偿项目的特别调解官展开对话：肯尼思·费恩伯格（Kenneth Feinberg，曾负责“9·11”受害者赔偿基金、波士顿马拉松爆炸案赔偿计划、天主教会性侵丑闻赔偿事务）；雷斯解决方案公司（Resolutions, LLC）联合创始人埃里克·格林（Eric Green，曾负责安然公司证券欺诈案、高田气囊缺陷案）；以及杜克大学教授弗朗西斯·麦戈文（Francis McGovern，曾负责英国石油公司“深水地平线”漏油事件、阿片类药物危机赔偿事务），他于2020年逝世。本文综述了他们的发言要点，为面临复杂多方谈判的人士提供宝贵洞见。

古汉·苏布拉马尼安：在你们的工作中，采用计分制来量化损害是否具有实际效用？

弗朗西斯·麦戈文：我们可以用数字作为无形损害（例如心理创伤）的替代指标。但要为心理创伤精确赋值，确实极其困难。因此，在2003年罗得岛州夜总会火灾事故（造成100人死亡、230人受伤）的赔偿方案中，我们采用了医疗支出作为衡量标准：只要当事人曾前往精神科医生处就诊并产生相关费用，该项支出即被纳入评估指标。最终，这一方法被广泛接受——因为受害者本人认可它……只要所有相关方都同意某种特定方法，我并不在意该方法具体为何，关键在于获得各方的认同与支持。

埃里克·格林：在所有这类案件中，归根结底的问题都是：谁有权就严重伤害获得赔偿？而实际上，任何数额的金钱都不足以真正弥补所受伤害，但金钱却是我们唯一可用的补偿手段。此外，大多数情况下，赔偿资金是有限的……因此，我们必须设计一种模型，在有限的资金池内，依据伤害程度的不同进行合理分配。如果这是一种替代诉讼的机制，那么该模型就必须具备内在逻辑性、高度透明性，并确保受影响群体拥有表达意见的权利。整个过程还必须秉持诚信、公平与公开原则。所有这些要求，意味着我们在每个环节都需要大量创新。

古汉·苏布拉马尼安：当您提到受害者应参与决策过程时，这一点令我印象深刻。

弗朗西斯·麦戈文：我们身为特别调解官，其合法性从何而来？……我们并未获得陪审团裁决所赋予的权威性。因此，我们实现合法性的途径，恰恰在于让公众切实参与到决策过程中来。

古汉·苏布拉马尼安：除了您们所采用的量化模型之外，是否也会作出定性判断？

埃里克·格林：几乎所有我参与过的模型，均设有针对特殊个案的例外条款，因而保留了一定程度的自由裁量空间。当然，我们从一开始就清楚：不可能让所有人满意。总有人会拒绝任何赔偿方案，认为自己未得到应有的对待，并且会明确向我们表达不满。

古汉·苏布拉马尼安：我们在谈判中常强调“共情”的表达。肯（Feinberg），据公开报道，您与团队曾承诺听取“9·11”受害者的意见——凡希望与您或团队面谈者，皆可如愿。这显然极具力量，但这种做法是否普遍适用？

肯尼思·费恩伯格：这是关键所在！我所参与过的各类赔偿模型在具体内容上虽各不相同，却有一个共同核心：即受害者本人，或其幸存家属所承载的强烈情绪。而共情，以及为那些自愿寻求倾诉机会的人提供私密、安全的表达渠道，正是不可或缺的要素。

目前，我正处理五个州的天主教会神职人员性侵案件。其中最显著的共性在于：受害者均为未成年时遭侵害，如今已三四十岁乃至五十多岁。他们私下前来见我，并非为了抱怨赔偿金额，而是渴望获得认可——希望有人承认其所遭受的伤害；希望一位独立第三方能对他们说：“我相信你。你受到了不公正对待。这一切太可怕了。”那些曾获得倾诉机会、并在离开时感到内心有所释然的人数，至关重要——因为正是有人对他们说：“是的，你的陈述准确、真实、可信”，才使得从“橙剂”事件、“9·11”事件直至今日的所有赔偿计划得以成功推进。
